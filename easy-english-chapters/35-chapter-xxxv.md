# Chapter XXXV

Elizabeth woke the next morning with the same thoughts that had finally allowed her to sleep. She had not recovered from the previous night’s surprise and could think of nothing else. She felt unable to work. Soon after breakfast, she decided that fresh air and exercise might help. She began walking directly towards her favourite path. Then she remembered that Mr. Darcy sometimes went there and stopped. Instead of entering the park, she took the lane farther from the main road. The park fence still formed one side, and she soon passed one of its gates.

Elizabeth walked along that part of the lane two or three times. The morning was pleasant, so she stopped at the gate to look into the park. During her five weeks in Kent, the countryside had greatly changed. The early trees became greener every day. She was about to continue when she noticed a gentleman among the trees at the park’s edge. He was coming towards her. Fearing it was Mr. Darcy, she immediately began to retreat. However, he was now close enough to see her. He stepped forward eagerly and spoke her name. Elizabeth had turned away, but his voice proved it was Mr. Darcy. She moved towards the gate again. By then, he had reached it. He held out a letter, which she took without thinking. With proud calmness, he said, “I have walked among these trees for some time, hoping to meet you. Will you honour me by reading this letter?” He bowed slightly, turned back among the trees, and soon disappeared.

Elizabeth expected no pleasure but felt the strongest curiosity. She opened the letter with growing surprise. The envelope contained two sheets of paper, completely covered in small, close writing. Even the envelope itself was full. She continued along the lane and began to read. It was written at Rosings at eight that morning and said:—

“Do not fear, madam, that this letter repeats the feelings or proposal you hated last night. I do not write to hurt you or to humiliate myself. For both our happiness, those wishes cannot be forgotten too soon. Writing and reading this letter will cause effort. I would have saved us both that effort if my character did not require it. You must forgive my demand for your attention. I know your feelings will give it unwillingly, but your sense of justice must give it.

“Last night, you accused me of two very different offences, unequal in seriousness. First, you said I separated Mr. Bingley from your sister without caring about their feelings. Second, you said I ruined Mr. Wickham’s present happiness and future hopes. I had supposedly ignored his claims, honour, and humanity. Mr. Wickham was my childhood companion and my father’s known favourite. He depended almost entirely on my family’s support and had been raised to expect it. Deliberately abandoning him would have been a terrible act. Separating two young people whose love had grown for only a few weeks could not compare with it. However, you judged me severely for both matters. I hope the following account of my actions and reasons will protect me from such blame in future. To explain myself, I must describe feelings that may offend you. I can only say I am sorry. The explanation is necessary, and further apology would be foolish. Soon after reaching Hertfordshire, I noticed what others also saw. Bingley preferred your eldest sister to every other young woman nearby. However, I did not fear serious love until the Netherfield ball. I had often seen him in love before. At that ball, I had the honour of dancing with you. Sir William Lucas then accidentally told me that everyone expected Bingley to marry your sister. He spoke of it as certain, with only the date undecided. From that moment, I watched my friend closely. His love for Miss Bennet was stronger than any I had seen in him. I also watched your sister. Her face and manners were open, cheerful, and attractive as always. Yet I saw no sign of special feeling. By the end of the evening, I remained convinced of one thing. His attention pleased her, but she did not share his love. If you were not mistaken, then I was. You know your sister better, so my mistake is more likely. If that mistake led me to hurt her, your anger was reasonable. Still, I must say that your sister appeared perfectly calm. Even the sharpest observer might believe her kind heart was not easily touched. I certainly wished to believe she did not care. However, my hopes and fears do not usually control my investigations and decisions. I believed in her indifference because I judged it fairly, not only because I wanted it. I also honestly considered such indifference sensible. My objections to the marriage were not only about her family’s social position. Last night, I admitted that only the strongest love overcame this concern in my own case. Lower connections would harm my friend less than me. But other serious objections existed equally in both cases. I had tried to forget them in my own case because they were not before me. I must now state them briefly. Your mother’s family position was objectionable. However, it mattered little beside the repeated lack of proper behaviour shown by your mother and three younger sisters. Even your father sometimes showed it. Forgive me; hurting you gives me pain. The faults of your closest relations must trouble you, and my description will anger you. But remember one comfort. You and your eldest sister have avoided the same criticism. Everyone recognises this, and it honours both your understanding and character. I will add only this: the events of that evening confirmed my opinion of everyone. They strengthened every reason to save my friend from what I considered a very unhappy connection. He left Netherfield for London the next day, planning to return soon. I must now explain my part. His sisters were as worried as I was, and we soon discovered our agreement. We believed no time should be lost in separating their brother from Miss Bennet. We therefore joined him in London. There I willingly explained the certain evils of his choice and strongly pressed them upon him. My warnings might have shaken or delayed his decision. However, they probably would not have prevented the marriage without something more. I assured him without hesitation that your sister did not love him. He had believed she honestly returned his love, though perhaps less strongly. Bingley is naturally modest and trusts my judgment more than his own. It was not difficult to persuade him that he had deceived himself. Once convinced, he quickly agreed not to return to Hertfordshire. I do not blame myself for doing this. Only one part of my behaviour brings me no satisfaction. I lowered myself to using a trick and hid your sister’s presence in London. Miss Bingley and I knew it, but her brother still does not. Perhaps they could have met without harm. However, his love did not seem weak enough to make such a meeting safe. This concealment may have been beneath me. Still, it is done, and I did it for the best. I have no more to say or apologise for on this subject. If I hurt your sister, I did so unknowingly. My reasons may naturally seem insufficient to you, but I have not learned to condemn them. — Now I come to the more serious accusation of injuring Mr. Wickham. I can only answer by giving the full history of his connection with my family. I do not know his exact accusations. However, several completely honest witnesses can confirm everything I relate. Mr. Wickham is the son of a respectable man who managed all the Pemberley estates for many years. His good service naturally made my father wish to help him. George Wickham was also his godson, so my father treated him generously. He paid for his schooling and later Cambridge. This help was important because Wickham’s father was always poor. His wife’s wastefulness prevented him from educating his son as a gentleman. My father enjoyed the young man’s attractive manners and thought very highly of him. He hoped Wickham would enter the church and planned to provide a parish position. I began to think very differently of Wickham many years ago. He carefully hid his bad habits and lack of principle from his greatest friend. But he could not hide them from a young man of nearly his own age. I saw him in careless moments my father, Mr. Darcy, never saw. Here again I will hurt you; only you know how much. Whatever feelings Mr. Wickham has created, I will still reveal his true character. Those feelings give me another reason. My excellent father died about five years ago. He remained devoted to Mr. Wickham until the end. In his will, he especially asked me to support Wickham’s career. If Wickham entered the church, he should receive a valuable family parish position when it became free. He also received one thousand pounds. Wickham’s father died soon afterwards. Within six months, Mr. Wickham wrote to me. He had finally decided not to enter the church. He hoped I would instead give him some immediate financial support. He said he planned to study law and could not live on the interest from one thousand pounds. I wished to believe him sincere but did not. Still, I readily accepted his proposal because I knew he should not become a clergyman. We quickly settled the matter. He gave up every claim to a church position and accepted three thousand pounds instead. Our connection now seemed ended. I thought too badly of him to invite him to Pemberley or meet him socially in London. I believe he mainly lived in London. His supposed legal studies were only an excuse. Free from all control, he lived in idleness and wild pleasure. For about three years, I heard little of him. Then the clergyman holding the position intended for him died. Mr. Wickham again applied to me by letter for the position. His financial situation was extremely bad, which I easily believed. Studying law had brought him no profit. He now claimed he would certainly enter the church if I gave him the position. He trusted I could hardly refuse. I had nobody else to provide for and could not have forgotten my honoured father’s wishes. You will hardly blame me for refusing this request and every repeated attempt. His anger matched his desperate financial position. He certainly spoke as violently against me to others as he did to me. After this, we ended every appearance of acquaintance. I do not know how he lived. But last summer, he forced himself painfully upon my attention again. I must describe an event I would prefer to forget. No smaller duty than this could make me reveal it to anyone. After saying so much, I do not doubt your secrecy. My sister is more than ten years younger than I am. My mother’s nephew, Colonel Fitzwilliam, and I became her guardians. About a year ago, she left school and began living in London with a lady responsible for her. Last summer, they went to Ramsgate. Mr. Wickham also went there, certainly by design. He already knew Mrs. Younge, whose character had badly deceived us. With her knowledge and help, he won Georgiana’s affection. Her loving heart remembered his childhood kindness. He persuaded her that she was in love and that they should run away together to marry. She was only 15, which must excuse her. After admitting her foolishness, I can happily add that Georgiana herself told me. I arrived unexpectedly a day or two before they planned to leave. She could not bear to hurt a brother she almost regarded as a father. She confessed everything. You may imagine what I felt and did. To protect my sister’s reputation and feelings, I did not make the event public. I wrote to Mr. Wickham, who immediately left. Mrs. Younge was naturally removed from her position. Mr. Wickham’s chief object was certainly my sister’s fortune of thirty thousand pounds. I also believe revenge against me strongly encouraged him. His revenge would truly have been complete. This, madam, is a faithful account of every event involving us. Unless you reject it completely as false, I hope you will no longer accuse me of cruelty towards Mr. Wickham. I do not know what lies he used to deceive you. His success is not surprising because you knew nothing about either of us. You could not discover the truth and did not wish to suspect him. You may wonder why I said none of this last night. I was not calm enough to know what I could or should reveal. Colonel Fitzwilliam can especially confirm the truth of everything here. He is my close relation and trusted companion. More importantly, he is one of the people responsible for my father’s will. He therefore knows every detail of these events. Your hatred of me may make my word worthless. However, the same cause cannot prevent you from trusting my cousin. So that you may speak to him, I will try to give you this letter this morning. I will only add: God bless you.

“Fitzwilliam Darcy.”
