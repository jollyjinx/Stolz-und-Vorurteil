# Chapter XIII

“I hope, my dear,” Mr. Bennet told his wife at breakfast next morning, “that you ordered a good dinner. I expect someone to join our family today.”

“Who do you mean, my dear? I know no one who is coming. Perhaps Charlotte Lucas may happen to visit. My dinners are certainly good enough for her. I doubt she often sees such meals at home.”

“The person I mean is a gentleman and a stranger.”

Mrs. Bennet's eyes shone. “A gentleman and a stranger! It must be Mr. Bingley. Jane, you clever secretive girl, you said nothing! I shall be extremely glad to see Mr. Bingley. But—good Lord! How unlucky! We cannot get any fish today. Lydia, my love, ring the bell. I must speak to Hill immediately.”

“It is not Mr. Bingley,” said her husband. “I have never seen this person in my life.”

Everyone became astonished. Mr. Bennet enjoyed being eagerly questioned by his wife and five daughters together.

After entertaining himself with their curiosity, he explained. “About a month ago, I received this letter. I answered it two weeks ago. The situation seemed delicate and required quick attention. It comes from my cousin, Mr. Collins. After I die, he may throw all of you out of this house whenever he likes.”

“Oh, my dear,” cried his wife, “I cannot bear hearing that. Please do not speak about that horrible man. It is terribly cruel that the entail takes your estate away from your own children. In your place, I would have tried long ago to do something about it.”

Jane and Elizabeth tried to explain the entail. They had often tried before. But Mrs. Bennet would not listen to reason on this subject. She continued angrily attacking the cruel settlement. An estate was leaving a family of five daughters for a man nobody cared about.

“It is certainly a very wicked business,” said Mr. Bennet. “Nothing can free Mr. Collins from the guilt of inheriting Longbourn. But listen to his letter. His manner of expressing himself may soften you a little.”

“It certainly will not. Writing to you was rude and false of him. I hate such false friends. Why could he not continue quarrelling with you as his father did?”

“Indeed, he seems to have worried about his duty to his father. You will hear.”

“Hunsford, near Westerham, Kent, 15 October.

“Dear Sir,

“The disagreement between you and my late honoured father always troubled me greatly. Since I sadly lost him, I have often wished to repair the relationship. For some time, my doubts stopped me. My father had always chosen to disagree with you. I feared friendship with you might seem disrespectful to his memory.”—“There, Mrs. Bennet.”—“However, I have now made my decision. I became a clergyman at Easter. Then I received the generous patronage of the Right Honourable Lady Catherine de Bourgh, widow of Sir Lewis de Bourgh. Through her kindness, she gave me the valuable parish position here. I shall always treat her Ladyship with grateful respect. I shall also be ready to perform every ceremony required by the Church of England. As a clergyman, I consider it my duty to establish peace in every family I can influence. For these reasons, I believe my present friendly offer deserves great praise. I hope you will kindly overlook my being next in the entail of Longbourn. Please do not reject this offered olive branch of peace. I am naturally concerned about harming your pleasant daughters and apologise for it. I am ready to make every possible repayment, but more about that later. If you have no objection, I propose to visit you and your family on Monday, 18 November, at four o'clock. I shall probably trouble you with my company until the following Saturday week. This causes no difficulty. Lady Catherine does not object to my occasional absence on Sunday if another clergyman performs the day's duties. I remain, dear sir, with respectful compliments to your wife and daughters, your well-wisher and friend,

“William Collins.”

“At four o'clock, then, we may expect this peacemaking gentleman,” said Mr. Bennet, folding the letter. “He seems a very responsible and polite young man. I am sure he will become a valuable acquaintance, especially if Lady Catherine kindly lets him visit us again.”

“What he says about the girls has some sense. If he wishes to repay them, I shall not discourage him.”

“It is difficult to guess how he means to repay us,” said Jane. “Still, the wish speaks well of him.”

Elizabeth chiefly noticed his extraordinary respect for Lady Catherine. His kind plan to baptise, marry, and bury his parishioners whenever necessary also interested her.

“I think he must be strange,” she said. “I cannot understand him. His style is very pompous. Why does he apologise for being next in the entail? Even if he wanted to change it, he probably could not. Can he be a sensible man, sir?”

“No, my dear, I think not. I strongly hope he is the opposite. His letter mixes obedience with self-importance in a very promising way. I am eager to see him.”

“As writing,” said Mary, “his letter seems correct. The olive branch idea is not completely original, but he expresses it well.”

Catherine and Lydia found neither the letter nor its writer interesting. Their cousin was very unlikely to arrive in a red uniform. For several weeks, no man in another colour had pleased them. The letter had removed much of their mother's anger toward Mr. Collins. She prepared to meet him with a calmness that amazed her husband and daughters.

Mr. Collins arrived exactly on time, and the whole family received him very politely. Mr. Bennet said little, but the ladies were ready to talk. Mr. Collins needed no encouragement and did not wish to remain silent. He was a tall, heavy-looking young man of twenty-five. His appearance was serious and important, and his manners extremely formal. Soon after sitting, he praised Mrs. Bennet's fine daughters. He had heard much about their beauty, but the truth was even better than their fame. He felt sure she would see them all well married in time. Some listeners disliked this polite speech. Mrs. Bennet never quarrelled with a compliment and answered eagerly.

“You are very kind, sir. I sincerely hope it happens. Otherwise, they will have almost nothing. Matters are arranged so strangely.”

“Perhaps you mean the entail on this estate.”

“Ah, sir, I certainly do. You must admit it is a painful matter for my poor girls. I do not blame you, of course. Such things happen by chance. No one knows where an estate will go once an entail controls it.”

“I fully understand my fair cousins' difficulty, madam. I could say much more, but I must not appear too bold or quick. Still, I assure the young ladies that I come prepared to admire them. For now, I shall say no more. But perhaps, when we know each other better—”

The call to dinner interrupted him, and the girls smiled at each other. They were not the only objects of Mr. Collins's admiration. He examined and praised the hall, dining room, and every piece of furniture. His praise would have pleased Mrs. Bennet deeply. Unfortunately, she imagined him viewing it all as his future property. He greatly admired the dinner too. He asked which fair cousin deserved praise for the cooking. Mrs. Bennet corrected him sharply. They could afford an excellent cook, and her daughters did no kitchen work. He apologised for offending her. In a gentler voice, she said she was not offended. Still, he continued apologising for about fifteen minutes.
