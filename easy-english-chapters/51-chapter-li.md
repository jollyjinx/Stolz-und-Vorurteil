# Chapter LI

Their sister’s wedding day arrived. Jane and Elizabeth probably felt more for Lydia than she felt herself. The carriage went to meet the couple at —— and bring them back by dinner. The elder Miss Bennets feared their arrival, especially Jane. She imagined Lydia must feel as Jane would after such wrongdoing. Thinking about what her sister must suffer made Jane miserable.

They arrived. The family waited in the breakfast room to receive them. Mrs. Bennet smiled as the carriage reached the door. Her husband looked completely serious. Her daughters felt alarmed, anxious, and uncomfortable.

They heard Lydia’s voice in the entrance hall. The door opened, and she ran into the room. Her mother stepped forward, embraced her, and welcomed her with delight. Wickham followed his wife. Mrs. Bennet gave him her hand with a loving smile. She eagerly wished them joy, showing no doubt about their happiness.

Then they turned to Mr. Bennet. His welcome was much less warm. His face became even more severe, and he hardly spoke. The young couple’s easy confidence was enough to anger him.

Elizabeth felt disgusted, and even Miss Bennet was shocked. Lydia remained exactly herself: uncontrolled, unashamed, wild, loud, and fearless. She turned from one sister to another and demanded their congratulations. At last, everyone sat down. Lydia eagerly looked around the room and noticed a small change. Then she laughed and said she had been away a very long time.

Wickham felt no more shame than Lydia. Yet his manners were always pleasant. If his character and marriage had been proper, everyone would have enjoyed his smiles and easy conversation. He warmly claimed their new family relationship. Elizabeth had not thought even he could show such confidence. She sat down and made a decision. In future, she would never limit what she believed a shameless man could do. Elizabeth and Jane blushed. The two people causing their shame did not change colour.

There was plenty of conversation. The bride and her mother could not speak fast enough. Wickham sat near Elizabeth and easily asked about his local friends. She found it impossible to answer with the same good humour. Lydia and Wickham seemed to have the happiest memories in the world. Nothing in the past caused them pain. Lydia freely mentioned subjects her sisters would never discuss.

“Just imagine, I left three months ago!” she cried. “It feels like only two weeks. Yet so many things have happened. Good gracious! When I left, I never expected to return married. Still, I thought marriage would be great fun.”

Her father looked up. Jane was distressed, and Elizabeth gave Lydia a meaningful look. But Lydia never saw or heard anything she chose to ignore. She continued happily:

“Oh, Mama, do people here know I married today? I feared they might not. We passed William Goulding in his small carriage, so I made sure he knew. I lowered the side window next to him. Then I removed my glove and rested my hand on the window. That let him see the ring. Then I bowed and smiled with all my strength.”

Elizabeth could bear no more. She got up and ran from the room. She did not return until they crossed the hall for dinner. She arrived in time to see Lydia proudly take the place beside her mother. Then Lydia said to her eldest sister:

“Ah, Jane, I take your place now. You must sit lower because I am a married woman.”

Time could not give Lydia the shame she had lacked from the beginning. Her confidence and high spirits only increased. She longed to see Mrs. Philips, the Lucases, and every other neighbour. She wanted each of them to call her “Mrs. Wickham.” After dinner, she showed her ring to Mrs. Hill and the two housemaids. She proudly told them she was married.

“Well, Mama,” she said when everyone returned to the breakfast room, “what do you think of my husband? Is he not charming? I am sure all my sisters envy me. I only hope they receive half my good luck. They must all go to Brighton. That is where one finds husbands. What a shame we did not all go, Mama!”

“Very true. We would have gone if I had decided. But, my dear Lydia, I dislike your moving so far away. Must you go?”

“Oh, Lord! Yes, but it does not matter. I will enjoy it greatly. You, Papa, and my sisters must visit us. We will spend the whole winter in Newcastle. I expect there will be balls, and I will find good partners for them all.”

“I would like that more than anything!” said her mother.

“When you leave, you can let one or two sisters stay with me. I will probably find husbands for them before winter ends.”

“Thank you for my share of the favour,” said Elizabeth. “But I do not particularly like your way of finding husbands.”

Their visitors would stay no more than ten days. Mr. Wickham had received his officer’s position before leaving London. He had to join his regiment at the end of two weeks.

Only Mrs. Bennet regretted their short visit. She used the time by making visits with Lydia and holding frequent parties at home. Everyone welcomed the parties. Thoughtful people wanted to avoid private family time even more than the thoughtless ones.

Wickham’s love for Lydia was exactly as Elizabeth expected. It was weaker than Lydia’s love for him. She hardly needed to observe them to know this. The facts already showed that Lydia’s love, not his, had caused their elopement. Elizabeth might have wondered why he ran away with a woman he did not deeply love. But financial trouble had forced him to escape. In such a position, he would not refuse the chance to have a companion.

Lydia was extremely fond of him. She called him her dear Wickham on every occasion. No one could compare with him. He did everything better than anyone else. She was sure he would shoot more birds on the first of September than any man in the country.

One morning soon after their arrival, Lydia sat with her two elder sisters. She said to Elizabeth:

“Lizzy, I believe I never told you about my wedding. You were absent when I told Mama and the others everything. Are you not curious to hear how we arranged it?”

“No, truly,” replied Elizabeth. “I think we should say as little as possible about it.”

“Oh, you are so strange! But I must tell you what happened. We married at St. Clement’s because Wickham’s rooms were in that parish. We all had to be there by eleven o’clock. My aunt, my uncle, and I would go together. The others would meet us at the church.

“Then Monday morning came, and I was so excited! I feared something would delay the wedding. I would have completely lost my mind. While I dressed, my aunt talked and lectured like someone reading a sermon. But I heard only one word in ten. Of course, I was thinking about my dear Wickham. I longed to know whether he would marry in his blue coat.

“We ate breakfast at ten as usual. I thought it would never end. By the way, my aunt and uncle were terribly unpleasant during my whole visit. Believe me, I never left the house once during those two weeks. There was no party, plan, or anything! Of course, London was rather empty, but the Little Theatre was open.

“Just as the carriage reached the door, business called my uncle to that horrible Mr. Stone. Once those men meet, they never finish. I was terribly frightened because my uncle had to give me away. If we missed the proper hour, we could not marry that day. Luckily, he returned after ten minutes, and we all left. Later, I remembered the wedding did not need him. Mr. Darcy could have given me away instead.”

“Mr. Darcy!” repeated Elizabeth in complete astonishment.

“Oh, yes! He was coming with Wickham. But good gracious, I completely forgot! I should not have said anything. I promised them so faithfully! What will Wickham say? It had to remain a great secret!”

“If it must remain secret,” said Jane, “do not say another word. You can trust me not to ask more.”

“Oh, certainly,” said Elizabeth, though burning with curiosity. “We will ask no questions.”

“Thank you,” said Lydia. “If you asked, I would certainly tell you everything. Then Wickham would be so angry.”

Such encouragement made Elizabeth want to ask. She had to remove the possibility by running away.

But remaining ignorant was impossible. At least, she had to try to learn more. Mr. Darcy had attended her sister’s wedding. That place, event, and group seemed to offer him no duty or attraction. Many quick and wild guesses rushed into her mind, but none satisfied her. She liked the guesses that made his behaviour most noble. Yet these seemed least likely. She could not bear the uncertainty. She quickly took paper and wrote a short letter to her aunt. She asked for an explanation of Lydia’s words if the intended secrecy allowed it.

“You can easily understand my curiosity,” she added. “This person is not connected with us and hardly knows our family. Why was he among you at such a time? Please write immediately and help me understand. Perhaps very strong reasons require the secrecy Lydia mentioned. If so, I must try to accept my ignorance.”

“Though I will not accept it,” she added to herself while finishing the letter. “My dear aunt, tell me honestly. Otherwise, I will use tricks and plans to discover the truth.”

Jane’s careful sense of honour prevented a private discussion about Lydia’s words. Elizabeth was glad. Until she knew whether her questions would receive answers, she preferred to have no confidante.
