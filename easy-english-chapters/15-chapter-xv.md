# Chapter XV

Mr. Collins was not a sensible man. Neither education nor society had done much to improve his natural weakness. He had spent most of his life under an uneducated and miserly father. He had attended a university, but only for the required time. He had made no useful connections there. His strict father had first made him seem very humble. Now, however, his foolish self-confidence weakened that humility. He had lived quietly, then found success early and unexpectedly. By good fortune, Lady Catherine de Bourgh had noticed him when the parish position at Hunsford became available. He deeply respected her high rank and worshipped her as his patron. At the same time, he thought very highly of himself. He valued his authority as a clergyman and his rights as rector. He was therefore a strange mixture of pride and obedience, self-importance and humility.

He now had a good house and a large enough income, so he planned to marry. When he tried to make peace with the Longbourn family, he already had a wife in mind. He meant to choose one of the daughters, if they were as pretty and pleasant as people said. This would be his way of making amends for inheriting their father's estate. He thought it an excellent and suitable plan. In his own opinion, it was also extremely generous and unselfish.

Seeing the daughters did not change his plan. Miss Bennet's lovely face confirmed his hopes. As the eldest daughter, she also suited his strict ideas about proper order. He therefore chose her during the first evening. The next morning, however, changed his mind. Before breakfast, he spent fifteen minutes alone with Mrs. Bennet. They began by discussing his rectory. Then he naturally admitted that he hoped to find its future mistress at Longbourn. Mrs. Bennet smiled warmly and encouraged him in general. Yet she warned him against choosing Jane. “I cannot speak for my younger daughters,” she said. “I cannot give you a definite answer. But I do not know of any special attachment among them. I must only mention something about my eldest daughter. I feel I should warn you that she will probably become engaged very soon.”

Mr. Collins only had to change his choice from Jane to Elizabeth. He did so while Mrs. Bennet stirred the fire. Elizabeth came directly after Jane in both age and beauty, so she naturally replaced her.

Mrs. Bennet remembered his hint and hoped that two daughters might soon marry. The man she could hardly mention the day before was now a great favourite.

Lydia had not forgotten her plan to walk to Meryton. Every sister except Mary agreed to go. Mr. Bennet asked Mr. Collins to accompany them. He badly wanted to get rid of him and have the library to himself. After breakfast, Mr. Collins had followed him there. He had pretended to read one of the largest books in the room. In truth, he had talked almost without stopping to Mr. Bennet about his house and garden at Hunsford. This greatly disturbed Mr. Bennet. In his library, he could usually depend on peace and quiet. He told Elizabeth that he expected foolish pride in every other room. But he was used to being free from it there. He therefore quickly and politely invited Mr. Collins to join the young women. Mr. Collins was much better suited to walking than reading. He happily closed the large book and went with them.

Mr. Collins filled the walk with grand but empty remarks. His cousins answered politely. This continued until they entered Meryton. Then he could no longer hold the younger girls' attention. They immediately searched the street for officers. Only a very fashionable bonnet or truly new muslin in a shop window could distract them.

Soon, however, every young woman's attention turned to a stranger. He was a young man with the appearance of a gentleman. He walked with an officer on the other side of the street. The officer was Mr. Denny, whose return from London Lydia had come to ask about. He bowed as they passed. Everyone admired the stranger's manner and wondered who he was. Kitty and Lydia were determined to find out. They crossed the street, pretending that they needed something from a shop there. Luckily, just as they reached the pavement, the two men turned back and met them. Mr. Denny immediately spoke to the young women. He asked permission to introduce his friend, Mr. Wickham. Wickham had returned with him from London the previous day. Denny was pleased to say that Wickham had accepted a position as an officer in their regiment. This was exactly right. The young man needed only a uniform to become completely charming. His appearance strongly supported him. He had a handsome face, a fine figure, and very pleasant manners. After the introduction, he spoke easily, properly, and without seeming proud. The whole group stood talking very pleasantly. Then they heard horses, and Darcy and Bingley came riding down the street. When they recognized the young women, both men came directly towards them. They began the usual polite conversation. Bingley spoke most, and his attention was mainly on Miss Bennet. He said he was riding to Longbourn especially to ask about her health. Mr. Darcy confirmed this with a bow. He was deciding not to look at Elizabeth when the stranger suddenly caught his eye. Elizabeth happened to watch both men as they recognized each other. Their reaction amazed her. Both changed colour: one became pale and the other red. After a few moments, Mr. Wickham touched his hat in greeting. Mr. Darcy only just chose to return the greeting. What could this mean? Elizabeth could not imagine, but she desperately wanted to know.

A minute later, Mr. Bingley said goodbye and rode on with his friend. He did not seem to have noticed what had happened.

Mr. Denny and Mr. Wickham walked with the young women to Mr. Philips's house. There they bowed and prepared to leave. Miss Lydia begged them to come inside, but they refused. Even Mrs. Philips opened the parlour window and loudly repeated the invitation, without success.

Mrs. Philips was always pleased to see her nieces. The two eldest were especially welcome after being away. She eagerly expressed surprise at their sudden return. Their own carriage had not collected them, so she would not have known. Luckily, she had seen Mr. Jones's shop boy in the street. He had said they should send no more medicine to Netherfield because the Miss Bennets had left. Then Jane introduced Mr. Collins, and Mrs. Philips had to welcome him politely. She used her finest manners, and he answered with even greater ceremony. He apologized for entering her home before they were acquainted. Still, he hoped his relationship to the young women excused him. Mrs. Philips was almost frightened by such extreme politeness. But she soon stopped thinking about one stranger because everyone asked about the other. She could only tell her nieces what they already knew. Mr. Denny had brought Wickham from London, and he would become a lieutenant in the ——shire regiment. She had watched him walking along the street for the past hour. If he had appeared again, Kitty and Lydia would certainly have kept watching too. Unfortunately, only a few officers now passed the windows. Compared with the stranger, they had become “stupid, disagreeable fellows.” Some would dine with the Philipses the next day. Their aunt promised that her husband would visit Mr. Wickham and invite him too. The Longbourn family only had to come that evening. Everyone agreed. Mrs. Philips promised a pleasant, noisy game of lottery tickets and a little hot supper afterwards. These delights cheered them all, and they separated in excellent spirits. As he left, Mr. Collins again apologized. With endless politeness, Mrs. Philips assured him that no apology was needed.

On the way home, Elizabeth told Jane what she had seen between the two men. Jane would have defended either man if someone accused him. But she could explain their behaviour no better than Elizabeth.

When Mr. Collins returned, he greatly pleased Mrs. Bennet by praising Mrs. Philips's manners and politeness. Apart from Lady Catherine and her daughter, he had never seen a more elegant woman. She had not only welcomed him with perfect politeness. She had also clearly included him in the invitation for the next evening, although they had never met. Perhaps his connection with the family explained some of this attention. Still, he said he had never received so much attention in his whole life.
