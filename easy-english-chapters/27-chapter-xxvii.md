# Chapter XXVII

January and February passed without greater events in the Longbourn family. Walks to Meryton, sometimes muddy and sometimes cold, provided most of the change. March would take Elizabeth to Hunsford. At first, she had not seriously planned to go. However, Charlotte depended on the visit. Elizabeth gradually looked forward to it with more pleasure and certainty. Their separation had increased her wish to see Charlotte and reduced her dislike of Mr. Collins. The plan offered something new. With such a mother and such unfriendly sisters, life at home was not perfect. A change was welcome for its own sake. The journey would also let her see Jane. As the time approached, Elizabeth would have been very sorry about any delay. Everything went smoothly and followed Charlotte’s first plan. Elizabeth would travel with Sir William and his second daughter. They later added a night in London, making the plan as perfect as possible.

Her only pain was leaving her father, who would certainly miss her. When the time came, he disliked her departure greatly. He told her to write and almost promised to answer.

Elizabeth and Mr. Wickham said goodbye with perfect friendliness, especially on his side. His present interest in Miss King did not make him forget Elizabeth. She had first attracted and deserved his attention. She had first listened, pitied, and been admired. He wished her every pleasure and reminded her what to expect from Lady Catherine de Bourgh. He trusted that their opinions of her—and everyone else—would always agree. His concern and interest touched Elizabeth. She felt she must always regard him with sincere affection. She left convinced that, married or single, he would remain her model of a pleasant and charming man.

The next day, her fellow travellers did not make Wickham seem less agreeable. Sir William Lucas and his daughter Maria had nothing worth hearing. Maria was good-humoured but as empty-headed as her father. Their talk was about as delightful as the carriage’s rattling. Elizabeth enjoyed absurd people, but she had known Sir William’s absurdities too long. He had no new story about the wonder of meeting the king and receiving his title. His information and his polite remarks were equally worn out.

The journey was only 24 miles, and they started early. They reached Gracechurch Street by noon. As their carriage approached Mr. Gardiner’s door, Jane watched from a drawing-room window. She came to the hall to welcome them. Elizabeth studied her face and was pleased to see it healthy and lovely as ever. A crowd of small boys and girls waited on the stairs. They were too eager to wait in the drawing room. However, after not seeing Elizabeth for a year, they were too shy to come farther down. Everything was joyful and loving. The day passed very pleasantly. The morning brought business and shopping, and they spent the evening at a theatre.

Elizabeth managed to sit beside her aunt. They first spoke about Jane. Mrs. Gardiner answered Elizabeth’s detailed questions. Jane always tried to stay cheerful, but sometimes became deeply sad. This upset Elizabeth more than it surprised her. Still, they could reasonably hope the sadness would not last long. Mrs. Gardiner also described Miss Bingley’s visit to Gracechurch Street. She repeated several conversations with Jane. They proved that Jane had sincerely given up the friendship.

Mrs. Gardiner then teased her niece about Wickham leaving her. She praised Elizabeth for bearing it so well.

“But, my dear Elizabeth,” she added, “what kind of girl is Miss King? I would be sorry to think our friend only wants her money.”

“My dear aunt, what is the difference between wanting money and being sensible when choosing a husband or wife? Where does careful judgment end and greed begin? Last Christmas, you feared his marrying me because it would be unwise. Now he is seeking a girl with only ten thousand pounds, and you call him greedy.”

“If you tell me what kind of girl Miss King is, I will know what to think.”

“I believe she is a very good sort of girl. I know nothing bad about her.”

“But he gave her no attention until her grandfather’s death gave her this fortune?”

“No—why should he? He could not seek my love because I had no money. Why should he court another poor girl whom he did not care about?”

“But turning his attention towards her so soon after her grandfather’s death seems rather improper.”

“A man in financial difficulty has no time for all the fine social rules others follow. If she does not object, why should we?”

“Her lack of objection does not excuse him. It only shows she lacks something herself—sense or feeling.”

“Well,” cried Elizabeth, “choose your own answer. He is greedy, and she is foolish.”

“No, Lizzy, I do not choose that. I would hate to think badly of a young man who lived so long in Derbyshire.”

“Oh, if that is all, I think very badly of young men from Derbyshire. Their close friends in Hertfordshire are not much better. I am tired of them all. Thank heaven! Tomorrow I am going to a man with no pleasant quality. Neither his manners nor his sense recommends him. After all, foolish men are the only ones worth knowing.”

“Be careful, Lizzy. That speech sounds strongly like disappointment.”

Before the play ended and separated them, Elizabeth received an unexpected pleasure. Her uncle and aunt invited her on a summer tour they were planning.

“We have not decided how far we will travel,” said Mrs. Gardiner, “but perhaps we will visit the Lakes.”

No plan could have pleased Elizabeth more. She accepted the invitation at once with great gratitude. “My dear, dear aunt,” she cried joyfully, “what delight! What happiness! You give me new life and energy. Goodbye to disappointment and bad temper. What are men beside rocks and mountains? Oh, what wonderful hours we will spend! When we return, we will not be like other travellers. We will be able to give accurate descriptions. We will know where we went and remember what we saw. Lakes, mountains, and rivers will not become confused in our minds. When we describe a view, we will not argue about where it was. Let our first descriptions be less unbearable than those of most travellers.”
