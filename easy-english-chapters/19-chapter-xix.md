# Chapter XIX

The next day brought a new scene at Longbourn. Mr. Collins made his formal proposal. He decided not to delay because his leave lasted only until the following Saturday. He felt no shyness, so even the moment itself caused him no pain. He proceeded in an orderly way, following every rule he believed belonged to such business. Soon after breakfast, he found Mrs. Bennet, Elizabeth, and one younger girl together. He addressed their mother.

“Madam, may I hope for your support with your beautiful daughter Elizabeth? I wish to request the honour of speaking with her privately this morning.”

Elizabeth only had time to blush with surprise before Mrs. Bennet answered immediately.

“Oh, dear! Yes, certainly. I am sure Lizzy will be very happy. I am sure she cannot object. Come, Kitty, I need you upstairs.” She gathered her sewing and hurried away. Then Elizabeth called out—

“Dear madam, please do not go. I beg you to stay. Mr. Collins must excuse me. He cannot have anything to say that others should not hear. I shall leave myself.”

“No, no, Lizzy. What nonsense. I order you to stay.” Elizabeth looked upset and embarrassed and seemed ready to escape. Mrs. Bennet added, “Lizzy, I insist that you stay and listen to Mr. Collins.”

Elizabeth would not disobey such an order. After thinking briefly, she realized that ending the matter quickly and quietly was best. She sat down again. Constant work with her hands hid feelings divided between distress and amusement. Mrs. Bennet and Kitty left. As soon as they were gone, Mr. Collins began.

“My dear Miss Elizabeth, believe me, your modesty only adds to your other perfect qualities. I would like you less without this small unwillingness. Let me assure you that your respected mother permits me to speak. Your natural modesty may make you pretend not to understand my purpose. Yet my attentions have been too clear to mistake. Almost as soon as I entered this house, I chose you as my future life's companion. But before my feelings carry me away, I should explain my reasons for marriage. I should also explain why I came to Hertfordshire intending to select a wife, because that was certainly my purpose.”

The thought of calm, serious Mr. Collins being carried away by emotion almost made Elizabeth laugh. She could not use his short pause to stop him. He continued.

“My first reason for marrying is that every comfortable clergyman like me should set an example in his parish. Second, I believe marriage will greatly increase my happiness. Third—and perhaps I should have mentioned this first—my noble patron especially advises it. Twice she has kindly given her opinion, although I did not ask! On the Saturday evening before I left Hunsford, we were playing quadrille. Mrs. Jenkinson was arranging Miss De Bourgh's footstool. Lady Catherine said, ‘Mr. Collins, you must marry. A clergyman like you must marry. Choose properly. For my sake and yours, choose a gentlewoman. She should be active and useful, without an expensive education. She must make a small income provide a great deal. This is my advice. Find such a woman quickly, bring her to Hunsford, and I shall visit her.’ My beautiful cousin, Lady Catherine de Bourgh's attention and kindness are important advantages I can offer you. Her manners are greater than I can describe. I believe she will enjoy your intelligence and lively character. Naturally, her rank will make you quieter and more respectful. Those are my general reasons for marriage. Now I must explain why I looked to Longbourn instead of my own neighbourhood. I assure you that many pleasant young women live there. But after your respected father's death, I shall inherit this estate. Of course, he may live many more years. I therefore decided to choose one of his daughters as my wife. Then your family's loss will be smaller when that sad event happens—though, as I said, it may be years away. That is my reason, beautiful cousin. I hope it will not lower your opinion of me. Now I must use the strongest language to assure you of my violent love. Money means nothing to me. I shall ask your father for none because I know he cannot give it. You may receive only one thousand pounds invested at four percent, after your mother's death. Therefore, I shall remain silent about money. Once we are married, I shall never blame you unfairly for it.”

Elizabeth absolutely had to interrupt him now.

“You are moving too quickly, sir,” she cried. “You forget that I have not answered. Let me answer without further delay. Thank you for the compliment. I fully understand the honour of your proposal. But I must refuse it.”

With a formal movement of his hand, Mr. Collins replied, “I already know young women commonly refuse the man they secretly plan to accept. They do so when he first asks for their favour. Sometimes they refuse a second or even a third time. Therefore, your words do not discourage me. I still hope to lead you to the altar soon.”

“Honestly, sir,” Elizabeth cried, “that hope is surprising after my answer. I am not one of those young women, if they exist. I would never risk my happiness by hoping for a second proposal. My refusal is completely serious. You could not make me happy. I am certain I am the last woman in the world who could make you happy. If your friend Lady Catherine knew me, she would surely consider me entirely unsuitable.”

“If we could be certain Lady Catherine thought so,” Mr. Collins said very seriously. “But I cannot imagine that her Ladyship would disapprove of you. When I next have the honour of seeing her, I shall praise your modesty, careful spending, and other pleasant qualities.”

“Mr. Collins, there is no need to praise me. You must let me judge for myself. Please show me the respect of believing my words. I wish you happiness and wealth. By refusing you, I do everything possible to protect them. Your offer has satisfied your delicate feelings about my family. You may inherit Longbourn without blaming yourself when the time comes. We can therefore consider this matter finally settled.” She rose and meant to leave. But Mr. Collins spoke again.

“Next time I have the honour of discussing this subject, I hope for a kinder answer. I do not accuse you of cruelty now. I know women traditionally refuse a man the first time. Perhaps you have already said as much as a truly modest woman can say to encourage me.”

“Really, Mr. Collins,” Elizabeth cried with some anger, “you confuse me completely. If my words sound encouraging, I do not know how to make you believe that I refuse.”

“My dear cousin, let me believe your refusal is only a polite custom. I have several short reasons. My offer does not seem unworthy of you. The home and position I can offer are highly desirable. My place in society, my connection with the De Bourgh family, and my relationship to you all support me. You should also consider something else. Despite your many charms, you may never receive another marriage proposal. Sadly, your share of money is very small. It will probably defeat the effect of your beauty and pleasant qualities. I must therefore conclude that your refusal is not serious. I shall believe you want uncertainty to increase my love. Elegant women often behave this way.”

“Sir, I assure you that I claim none of the elegance that means troubling a respectable man. I would prefer the compliment of being believed. Again and again, I thank you for the honour of your proposal. But accepting is completely impossible. Every feeling I have prevents it. Can I speak more clearly? Do not see me as an elegant woman trying to annoy you. See me as a reasonable person speaking honestly from her heart.”

“You are always charming!” he cried with awkward politeness. “When both your excellent parents clearly support me, I am certain you will accept my proposal.”

Elizabeth would not answer such determined self-deception. She immediately left in silence. If he continued to treat her repeated refusals as encouragement, she would ask her father for help. His refusal would be final. At least no one could mistake his behaviour for the pretended modesty and flirting of an elegant woman.
