# Chapter VII

Mr. Bennet's property was almost entirely an estate bringing two thousand pounds a year. Unfortunately for his daughters, an entail controlled it. Without a son, Longbourn had to pass to a distant male relative. Mrs. Bennet's fortune was large enough for her position but could not replace this loss. Her father had been a lawyer in Meryton and left her four thousand pounds.

Her sister was married to Mr. Philips. He had worked as their father's clerk and later took over his business. Her brother lived in London and worked in a respectable trade.

Longbourn village was only one mile from Meryton. This was a convenient distance for the young ladies. Three or four times a week, they visited their aunt and the hat shop opposite her. Catherine and Lydia went especially often. They had fewer thoughts and activities than their sisters. When nothing better appeared, a walk to Meryton entertained their morning and provided evening conversation. The countryside might have little news, but their aunt always had some. At present, they had plenty of news and happiness. A militia regiment had recently arrived nearby. It would stay all winter, with its headquarters in Meryton.

Visits to Mrs. Philips now produced the most exciting information. Every day, they learned more officers' names and family connections. Soon they knew where the officers lived and then knew the men themselves. Mr. Philips visited all of them, opening a new source of joy for his nieces. They spoke of nothing but officers. Their mother became excited by Mr. Bingley's fortune. But to the girls, it was worthless beside the uniform of even the lowest officer.

One morning, Mr. Bennet listened to them speak warmly about the officers. Then he calmly observed:

“From the way you talk, I understand that you are two of the silliest girls in the country. I have suspected this for some time. Now I am certain.”

Catherine was embarrassed and said nothing. Lydia did not care. She continued praising Captain Carter and hoping to see him that day. He was leaving for London the next morning.

“I am amazed, my dear,” said Mrs. Bennet, “that you so readily call your own children silly. If I wanted to think badly of anyone's children, I would not choose mine.”

“If my children are silly, I hope I will always recognise it.”

“Yes, but they are actually all very clever.”

“This is the only subject on which we disagree. I hoped our opinions were always the same. But I must believe that our two youngest daughters are unusually foolish.”

“My dear Mr. Bennet, you cannot expect such young girls to have their parents' sense. At our age, they will care as little about officers as we do. I remember when I liked a red uniform very much. In my heart, I still do. If a handsome young colonel with five or six thousand pounds a year wanted one daughter, I would agree. Colonel Forster looked very fine in uniform at Sir William's the other night.”

“Mama,” cried Lydia, “my aunt says Colonel Forster and Captain Carter visit Miss Watson less often now. She often sees them standing in Clarke's bookshop instead.”

Before Mrs. Bennet could answer, a servant entered with a note for Miss Bennet. It came from Netherfield, and the messenger waited for an answer. Mrs. Bennet's eyes shone with pleasure. While Jane read, she called eagerly:

“Well, Jane, who sent it? What is it about? What does he say? Hurry and tell us, Jane. Hurry, my love.”

“It is from Miss Bingley,” said Jane. Then she read it aloud.

“My dear friend,

“If you do not dine with Louisa and me today, we may hate each other forever. A whole day alone between two women must end in an argument. Come as soon as you receive this. My brother and the gentlemen will dine with the officers. Yours always,

“Caroline Bingley.”

“With the officers!” cried Lydia. “I wonder why my aunt did not tell us.”

“Dining away from home,” said Mrs. Bennet. “That is very unlucky.”

“May I have the carriage?” asked Jane.

“No, my dear. You should ride there because it seems likely to rain. Then you will have to stay all night.”

“That would be a good plan,” said Elizabeth, “if they certainly would not offer to send her home.”

“Oh, but the gentlemen will take Mr. Bingley's carriage to Meryton. The Hursts have no horses for their own.”

“I would much rather travel in our carriage.”

“But, my dear, your father cannot spare the horses. They are needed on the farm. Mr. Bennet, are they not?”

“The farm needs them much more often than I can get them for myself.”

“But if you have them today,” said Elizabeth, “my mother's plan will succeed.”

At last, Mrs. Bennet forced her husband to admit the horses were busy. Jane had to ride. Her mother followed her to the door, cheerfully predicting bad weather. Her hopes came true. Soon after Jane left, heavy rain began. Her sisters worried, but her mother was delighted. It rained without stopping all evening. Jane certainly could not return.

“That was a very lucky idea of mine!” Mrs. Bennet said several times. She spoke as if she had caused the rain herself. However, she did not know the full success of her plan until morning. Breakfast had hardly ended when a Netherfield servant brought Elizabeth this note:

“My dearest Lizzie,

“I feel very ill this morning, probably because I became completely wet yesterday. My kind friends will not let me return home until I recover. They also insist that Mr. Jones examines me. Do not worry if you hear that he has visited. Apart from a sore throat and headache, there is not much wrong with me.

“Yours, etc.”

“Well, my dear,” Mr. Bennet said after Elizabeth read it aloud, “perhaps your daughter will become dangerously ill. If she dies, it will comfort us to know she did it while pursuing Mr. Bingley under your orders.”

“Oh, I am not afraid she will die. People do not die from small colds. They will care for her well. Everything is fine while she remains there. I would visit her if I could have the carriage.”

Elizabeth was truly worried and decided to go to Jane. No carriage was available, and she did not ride. Her only choice was walking, and she announced her decision.

“How can you be foolish enough to walk through all this mud?” cried her mother. “You will not be fit to be seen when you arrive.”

“I will be fit to see Jane. That is all I want.”

“Is that a hint, Lizzy,” said her father, “that I should send for the horses?”

“No, indeed. I do not want to avoid the walk. Distance is nothing when you have a reason. It is only three miles. I will return by dinner.”

“I admire the energy of your kindness,” Mary observed. “But reason should guide every feeling. In my opinion, effort should always match what is required.”

“We will walk with you as far as Meryton,” said Catherine and Lydia. Elizabeth accepted their company, and the three left together.

“If we hurry,” Lydia said as they walked, “perhaps we will see Captain Carter before he leaves.”

They separated in Meryton. The two youngest went to the home of an officer's wife. Elizabeth continued alone. She quickly crossed field after field, climbed over stiles, and jumped across puddles. At last, the house came into view. Her ankles were tired, her stockings muddy, and exercise had made her face glow.

She entered the breakfast room, where everyone except Jane had gathered. Her appearance caused great surprise. Mrs. Hurst and Miss Bingley could hardly believe she had walked three miles alone in such weather. Elizabeth was sure they despised her for it. Still, they received her very politely. Their brother showed more than politeness: he showed kindness and good humour. Mr. Darcy said little, and Mr. Hurst said nothing. Darcy admired the brightness exercise had given her face. He also wondered whether concern for Jane justified walking so far alone. Mr. Hurst thought only about his breakfast.

The answers to her questions about Jane were not encouraging. Miss Bennet had slept badly and now had a fever. Though awake, she was too ill to leave her room. Elizabeth was glad to go to her immediately. Jane had not admitted in her note how much she wanted Elizabeth's visit. She feared worrying or troubling anyone. Now she was delighted to see her. However, she was too ill for much conversation. When Miss Bingley left, Jane could say little except grateful praise for everyone's extraordinary kindness. Elizabeth quietly cared for her.

After breakfast, the sisters joined them. Elizabeth began to like them when she saw their affection and concern for Jane. The local medical man came and examined her. As expected, he said she had caught a severe cold and they must overcome it. He advised bed and promised to send medicine. Jane gladly followed the advice because her fever and headache were growing worse. Elizabeth never left her room, and the other ladies were rarely away. The gentlemen were out, so they had nothing else to do.

At three o'clock, Elizabeth felt she must leave and unwillingly said so. Miss Bingley offered her a carriage. Elizabeth needed only a little urging to accept. Then Jane showed such unhappiness at losing her that Miss Bingley changed the offer. Instead, she invited Elizabeth to remain at Netherfield for now. Elizabeth accepted gratefully. A servant went to Longbourn to report her stay and bring back clothes.
