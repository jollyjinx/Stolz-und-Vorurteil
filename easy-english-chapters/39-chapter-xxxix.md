# Chapter XXXIX

In the second week of May, the three young women left Gracechurch Street together. They travelled to the town of —— in Hertfordshire. Near the inn, Mr. Bennet’s carriage was supposed to meet them. They soon saw proof that the driver had arrived on time. Kitty and Lydia were watching from an upstairs dining-room window. They had already spent over an hour there. They happily visited the hat shop opposite, watched the soldier on guard, and prepared salad and cucumber.

After welcoming their sisters, they proudly showed a table covered with an inn’s usual cold meat. “Isn’t this nice?” they cried. “Isn’t it a wonderful surprise?”

“And we plan to pay for you all,” Lydia added. “But you must lend us the money. We have just spent ours at that shop.” Then she showed them her purchases. “Look, I bought this hat. I do not think it is very pretty. But I thought I might as well buy it. I will take it apart when I get home. Then I will see if I can make it look better.”

Her sisters called it ugly, but Lydia was not troubled. “Oh, there were two or three much uglier ones in the shop. I will buy prettier satin and decorate it again. Then I think it will look quite acceptable. Besides, what we wear this summer will hardly matter. The ——shire regiment is leaving Meryton in two weeks.”

“Are they really?” cried Elizabeth with the greatest satisfaction.

“They will camp near Brighton. I want papa to take us all there for the summer! It would be a wonderful plan and would hardly cost anything, I am sure. Mamma would love to go too! Just imagine what a miserable summer we will have otherwise!”

“Yes,” Elizabeth thought, “that would indeed be a wonderful plan. It would ruin us completely and immediately. Good heavens! Brighton and a whole camp full of soldiers! One poor militia regiment and Meryton’s monthly dances have already unsettled us!”

“Now I have some news for you,” Lydia said as they sat at the table. “What do you think? It is excellent news, wonderful news, about someone we all like.”

Jane and Elizabeth looked at each other. They told the waiter that he did not need to remain. Lydia laughed and said—

“Yes, that is just like your careful, proper behaviour. You thought the waiter must not hear, as if he cared! I am sure he often hears worse things than my news. But he is an ugly man! I am glad he has gone. I have never seen such a long chin. Well, now for my news. It concerns dear Wickham, so it was too good for the waiter, wasn’t it? Wickham will not marry Mary King—there! She has gone to stay with her uncle in Liverpool. Wickham is safe.”

“And Mary King is safe!” Elizabeth added. “Safe from a financially unwise marriage.”

“If she liked him, she is very foolish to leave.”

“But I hope neither of them is deeply in love,” said Jane.

“I am certain he is not. I promise he never cared even slightly about her. Who could care about such a nasty little freckled creature?”

Elizabeth was shocked. She herself could never have expressed such an unkind thought so roughly. Yet she had once held nearly the same opinion and had believed it generous!

When everyone had eaten and the older sisters had paid, they called for the carriage. After some planning, the whole group managed to fit inside. So did every box, sewing bag, and package. Kitty and Lydia’s unwelcome new purchases joined them too.

“How wonderfully crowded we are!” cried Lydia. “I am glad I brought my hat. Another hatbox makes the crowd even funnier! Now let us be warm and comfortable. We can talk and laugh all the way home. First, tell us everything that happened while you were away. Did you meet any pleasant men? Did you flirt with anyone? I truly hoped one of you would return with a husband. Jane will soon be an old maid, I declare. She is almost twenty-three! Lord! I would be so ashamed not to marry before twenty-three! You cannot imagine how much Aunt Philips wants you to find husbands. She says Lizzy should have accepted Mr. Collins. But I do not think that marriage would have been any fun. Lord! I would love to marry before all of you! Then I could take you to every dance as your married companion. Goodness! We had such fun at Colonel Forster’s the other day! Kitty and I spent the day there. Mrs. Forster promised a little dance that evening. By the way, Mrs. Forster and I are very close friends! So she invited the two Harringtons. But Harriet was ill, and Pen had to come alone. Then what do you think we did? We dressed Chamberlayne in women’s clothes so everyone would think he was a lady. Just imagine the fun! Nobody knew except Colonel and Mrs. Forster, Kitty, me, and my aunt. We had to borrow one of her dresses. You cannot imagine how good he looked! Denny, Wickham, Pratt, and two or three other men came in. They did not recognise him at all. Lord, how I laughed! Mrs. Forster laughed too. I thought I would die. This made the men suspect something, and they soon discovered the trick.”

Lydia tried to entertain everyone all the way to Longbourn with such party stories and jokes. Kitty helped with reminders and extra details. Elizabeth listened as little as possible. Still, she could not escape hearing Wickham’s name again and again.

Their family welcomed them very warmly. Mrs. Bennet was delighted that Jane remained as beautiful as ever. During dinner, Mr. Bennet spoke to Elizabeth more than once without being asked—

“I am glad you have returned, Lizzy.”

The dinner group was large because almost every Lucas came. They wished to welcome Maria and hear her news. Many different conversations filled the room. Across the table, Lady Lucas asked Maria about her eldest daughter and her chickens. Mrs. Bennet managed two tasks at once. She collected information about current fashions from Jane, who sat farther down the table. At the same time, she repeated it all to the younger Miss Lucases. Lydia spoke louder than anyone else. She listed the morning’s pleasures to anyone willing to listen.

“Oh, Mary,” she said, “I wish you had come because we had such fun! On the journey, Kitty and I pulled down every window cover. We pretended nobody was inside the carriage. I would have done it all the way, but Kitty became sick. When we reached the George, I think we behaved very generously. We treated the other three to the nicest cold lunch in the world. If you had come, we would have treated you too. Then leaving was such fun! I thought we would never fit inside the carriage. I nearly died with laughter. We were so cheerful all the way home! We talked and laughed so loudly that people could hear us ten miles away!”

Mary replied very seriously, “My dear sister, I would never wish to speak badly of such pleasures. Most women would certainly enjoy them. But I admit they would give me no pleasure. I would greatly prefer a book.”

Lydia did not hear one word of this answer. She rarely listened to anyone for half a minute. She never listened to Mary at all.

That afternoon, Lydia strongly urged the other girls to walk to Meryton. She wanted to see how everyone was doing, but Elizabeth firmly opposed the plan. People must not say the Miss Bennets chased officers after only half a day at home. Elizabeth had another reason too. She feared meeting Wickham again and planned to avoid him as long as possible. The regiment’s coming departure gave her enormous comfort. They would leave in two weeks. Once they were gone, she hoped Wickham could no longer trouble her.

Elizabeth soon learned that her parents often discussed Lydia’s Brighton plan from the inn. She immediately saw that her father had no intention of agreeing. But his answers were unclear and could be understood in different ways. Therefore, although Mrs. Bennet was often discouraged, she still hoped to succeed eventually.
