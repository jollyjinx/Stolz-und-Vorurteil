# Chapter XVIII

Elizabeth entered the drawing room at Netherfield and searched the crowd of red uniforms. Until that moment, she had never doubted that Mr. Wickham would be there. She had ignored every memory that might reasonably have worried her. She had dressed with special care and arrived in excellent spirits. She hoped to win whatever part of his heart remained unwon that evening. Then a terrible thought struck her. Perhaps the Bingleys had left him off the officers' invitation to please Mr. Darcy. This was not quite true, but Wickham was certainly absent. Lydia eagerly asked his friend Mr. Denny about him. Denny said Wickham had gone to London on business the day before and had not returned. With a meaningful smile, he added—

“I do not think business would have called him away now, unless he wished to avoid a certain gentleman here.”

Lydia did not hear this, but Elizabeth did. It made Darcy seem just as responsible as she had first imagined. Her disappointment greatly increased her dislike of him. Soon afterwards, he approached and politely asked how she was. She could hardly answer with ordinary politeness. Giving Darcy attention, patience, or tolerance would be an injury to Wickham. She decided not to speak with him at all and turned away angrily. Even Mr. Bingley could not completely improve her mood. His blind loyalty to Darcy annoyed her.

But Elizabeth was not naturally bad-tempered. Her own hopes for the evening were destroyed, yet she did not remain unhappy for long. She told Charlotte Lucas all her troubles, since they had not met for a week. Soon she could describe her cousin's strange behaviour and point him out. The first two dances brought her misery back. They were deeply embarrassing. Mr. Collins was awkward and serious. He apologized instead of paying attention and often moved wrongly without noticing. A bad partner for two dances could hardly cause more shame and discomfort. Escaping him felt wonderful.

Next, she danced with an officer. Talking about Wickham refreshed her, especially when she heard that everyone liked him. Afterwards, she returned to Charlotte Lucas and began speaking with her. Suddenly, Mr. Darcy asked for her hand in the next dance. His request surprised her so much that she accepted without knowing what she was doing. He immediately walked away. Elizabeth remained behind, annoyed by her slow reaction. Charlotte tried to comfort her.

“I am sure you will find him very pleasant.”

“Heaven forbid! That would be the worst possible misfortune. Imagine liking a man whom one is determined to hate! Do not wish such evil on me.”

When dancing began again, Darcy came to claim her hand. Charlotte quietly warned Elizabeth not to be foolish. Her feelings for Wickham must not make her unpleasant to a man far more important than him. Elizabeth did not answer and took her place in the dance. She was amazed by the honour of standing opposite Mr. Darcy. Their neighbours' looks showed equal amazement. For some time, neither spoke. Elizabeth began to imagine that silence would last through both dances. At first, she decided not to break it. Then she thought forcing him to talk would punish him more. She made a small remark about the dance. He answered and became silent again. After several minutes, she spoke a second time.

“It is your turn to say something now, Mr. Darcy. I mentioned the dance. You should comment on the room's size or the number of couples.”

He smiled and promised to say anything she wished.

“Very well. That answer will do for now. Later, perhaps, I shall say private balls are much pleasanter than public ones. But now we may be silent.”

“Do you follow rules for conversation while dancing?”

“Sometimes. We must speak a little, you know. Complete silence for half an hour would look strange. Yet conversation should help some people by making them say as little as possible.”

“Are you thinking of your own feelings now, or trying to please me?”

“Both,” Elizabeth replied playfully. “I have always noticed how similar our minds are. We are both unfriendly and silent. We speak only when we can amaze the whole room. Then future generations will repeat our words like famous sayings.”

“I am sure that does not describe you very well,” he said. “I cannot say how closely it describes me. You certainly believe it is accurate.”

“I must not judge my own work.”

He did not answer. They remained silent until they reached the end of the dance. Then he asked whether she and her sisters often walked to Meryton. She said yes. Unable to resist, she added, “When you met us there recently, we had just made a new acquaintance.”

The effect was immediate. His face became more proud, but he said nothing. Elizabeth blamed herself for her weakness and could not continue. At last, Darcy spoke in a controlled voice.

“Mr. Wickham has such pleasant manners that he easily makes friends. Whether he can keep them is less certain.”

“He was unlucky enough to lose your friendship,” Elizabeth replied with emphasis. “He will probably suffer from the way he lost it throughout his life.”

Darcy did not answer and seemed eager to change the subject. At that moment, Sir William Lucas came near them. He meant to cross the dance to the other side. When he saw Mr. Darcy, however, he stopped and bowed with special politeness. Then he praised Darcy's dancing and his partner.

“My dear sir, I have been greatly delighted. One rarely sees such excellent dancing. You clearly belong to the highest society. Yet your beautiful partner does not make you look worse. I hope to see this pleasure repeated often. That will be especially true when a certain happy event occurs, my dear Miss Eliza.” He looked at Jane and Bingley. “What congratulations we shall offer then! I ask Mr. Darcy to agree. But I must not interrupt you, sir. You will not thank me for keeping you from this charming young lady's conversation. Her bright eyes are already blaming me.”

Darcy hardly heard the final part. Sir William's reference to Bingley affected him strongly. He looked very seriously towards Bingley and Jane, who danced together. Soon he recovered and turned back to Elizabeth.

“Sir William's interruption has made me forget our subject.”

“I do not think we were speaking at all. Sir William could not have interrupted two people with less to say. We have already tried two or three subjects without success. I cannot imagine what we should discuss next.”

“What do you think of books?” he asked with a smile.

“Books—oh, no! I am sure we never read the same ones. Or we do not feel the same way about them.”

“I am sorry you think so. But if that is true, we certainly have a subject. We can compare our different opinions.”

“No. I cannot discuss books in a ballroom. My head is always full of something else.”

“At such events, the present moment always fills your thoughts—does it?” His expression showed doubt.

“Yes, always,” she replied without thinking. Her mind had already left their subject. Soon she suddenly said, “Mr. Darcy, I remember hearing you say that you hardly ever forgive. Once you become angry, your anger cannot be ended. I suppose you are very careful before you become angry?”

“I am,” he said firmly.

“And you never let prejudice blind you?”

“I hope not.”

“People who never change their opinion must be especially certain that their first judgment is correct.”

“May I ask where these questions are leading?”

“I only want to understand your character,” she said, trying to become less serious. “I am attempting to discover it.”

“And are you succeeding?”

She shook her head. “Not at all. I hear such different reports about you that I am completely confused.”

“I can easily believe that reports about me differ greatly,” he answered seriously. “Miss Bennet, I wish you would not draw my character at present. I fear the result would make neither of us look good.”

“But if I do not draw you now, I may never have another chance.”

“I would never stop you from enjoying yourself,” he replied coldly. She said nothing more. They completed the second dance and separated in silence. Both were unhappy, though not equally. Darcy felt strongly enough about her to forgive her soon. All his anger turned towards another person.

They had not been apart long when Miss Bingley approached Elizabeth. With polite contempt, she said—

“So, Miss Eliza, I hear that George Wickham delights you? Your sister has asked me a thousand questions about him. Among his other information, the young man apparently forgot one fact. He is old Wickham's son, and old Wickham managed the late Mr. Darcy's estate. As a friend, I advise you not to believe all his claims. His story that Mr. Darcy treated him badly is completely false. Mr. Darcy has always been unusually kind to him. George Wickham, however, has treated Mr. Darcy shamefully. I do not know the details. But I know that Mr. Darcy is entirely innocent. He cannot bear hearing George Wickham's name. My brother thought he had to include Wickham in the officers' invitation. Yet he was very pleased that Wickham removed himself. Wickham's decision to come here at all is extremely rude. I cannot imagine how he dared. I pity you for discovering your favourite's guilt, Miss Eliza. But considering his family background, one could hardly expect better.”

“According to you, his guilt and his family background are the same thing,” Elizabeth said angrily. “You accuse him only of being Mr. Darcy's estate manager's son. I assure you that he told me that himself.”

“I beg your pardon,” Miss Bingley replied with a cruel smile as she turned away. “Forgive me for interfering. I meant to be kind.”

“Rude girl!” Elizabeth said to herself. “You are badly mistaken if you expect such a weak attack to influence me. It shows only your chosen ignorance and Mr. Darcy's spite.” Then she looked for Jane, who had promised to ask Bingley about the same matter. Jane met her with a gentle smile and a happy glow. Her expression clearly showed how much she enjoyed the evening. Elizabeth understood her feelings immediately. At that moment, her worries about Wickham and anger at his enemies disappeared. She could think only of Jane's excellent chance for happiness.

“I want to know what you learned about Mr. Wickham,” she said, smiling as brightly as Jane. “But perhaps you were too pleasantly occupied to think of anyone else. If so, I shall certainly forgive you.”

“No,” Jane replied, “I have not forgotten him. But I have nothing satisfying to tell you. Mr. Bingley does not know his whole history. He knows nothing about the events that chiefly offended Mr. Darcy. Still, he promises that his friend behaves honestly and honourably. He is certain that Mr. Wickham deserved much less help from Mr. Darcy than he received. I am sorry to say that both he and his sister consider Mr. Wickham an unrespectable young man. I fear Mr. Wickham behaved very carelessly and deserved to lose Mr. Darcy's friendship.”

“Mr. Bingley does not know Mr. Wickham himself.”

“No. He first saw him in Meryton the other morning.”

“Then Mr. Darcy gave him this story. That completely satisfies me. But what does he say about the clerical living?”

“He does not remember the exact details, although Mr. Darcy told him more than once. But he believes Wickham received only a conditional promise.”

“I do not doubt Mr. Bingley's honesty,” Elizabeth said warmly. “But you must forgive me for needing more than promises. I am sure Mr. Bingley defended his friend very skilfully. Yet he does not know several parts of the story. He learned the rest from that same friend. I shall therefore continue to think of both men exactly as before.”

She changed to a subject that pleased them both and caused no disagreement. Elizabeth happily heard Jane's hopeful but modest thoughts about Bingley's feelings. She did everything possible to increase Jane's confidence. Then Mr. Bingley joined them, and Elizabeth returned to Miss Lucas. Charlotte asked whether Elizabeth had enjoyed her last partner. Before she could fully answer, Mr. Collins approached. With great excitement, he announced an important discovery.

“By a remarkable accident, I have learned that my patron's close relation is in this room,” he said. “I heard the gentleman mention his cousin Miss De Bourgh and her mother, Lady Catherine. He was speaking to the young woman who welcomes guests here. How wonderfully such things happen! Who could imagine that I might meet Lady Catherine de Bourgh's nephew at this public ball? Thankfully, I discovered it in time to offer him my respects. I shall do so now. I trust he will forgive me for not speaking earlier. My complete ignorance of the connection must excuse me.”

“You are not going to introduce yourself to Mr. Darcy?”

“Indeed, I am. I shall beg his forgiveness for not doing so earlier. I believe he is Lady Catherine's nephew. I can assure him that her Ladyship was perfectly well a week ago yesterday.”

Elizabeth tried hard to stop him. She explained that Mr. Darcy would consider an introduction without permission extremely rude, not a compliment to his aunt. Neither man needed to notice the other. If an introduction was needed, Mr. Darcy was more important and must begin it. Mr. Collins listened with the determined expression of a man following his own wishes. When she stopped, he replied.

“My dear Miss Elizabeth, I have the highest opinion of your excellent judgment in matters you understand. But the rules for ordinary people differ greatly from those for the clergy. The position of a clergyman equals the highest rank in the country—if he also behaves with proper humility. Therefore, you must let me follow my conscience. It tells me that this is my duty. Forgive me for not following your advice. On every other subject, it will always guide me. But education and regular study make me better able to judge this question than a young woman like you.” He bowed deeply and left her to approach Mr. Darcy. Elizabeth watched Darcy's response carefully. His amazement at being addressed was obvious. Her cousin began with a serious bow. She could not hear him, but felt she knew every word. His lips clearly formed “apology,” “Hunsford,” and “Lady Catherine de Bourgh.” Seeing him embarrass himself before Darcy upset her. Darcy watched him with open surprise. When Mr. Collins finally allowed him to speak, Darcy answered with distant politeness. This did not stop Mr. Collins from speaking again. Darcy's contempt grew as the second speech continued. At its end, he gave only a slight bow and moved away. Mr. Collins then returned to Elizabeth.

“I assure you that I am completely satisfied with my welcome,” he said. “Mr. Darcy seemed very pleased by my attention. He answered with perfect politeness. He even paid me a compliment. He said he trusted Lady Catherine's judgment so fully that she could never help an unworthy person. That was a very fine thought. Overall, I am greatly pleased with him.”

Elizabeth had no more personal hopes for the evening. She now watched Jane and Mr. Bingley almost completely. Her pleasant thoughts made her nearly as happy as Jane. She imagined her sister living in that very house. A loving marriage would give Jane perfect happiness. In those circumstances, Elizabeth might even try to like Bingley's two sisters. She saw that her mother's thoughts followed the same path. Elizabeth decided not to go near her because she might hear too much. At supper, however, terrible bad luck placed them close together. Her mother spoke freely and loudly to Lady Lucas. She discussed nothing except her expectation that Jane would soon marry Mr. Bingley. Mrs. Bennet found the subject exciting and never tired of listing the marriage's advantages. Bingley was charming and rich, and lived only three miles away. She praised herself for all these facts. His sisters loved Jane, which was another comfort. Surely they wanted the marriage as much as Mrs. Bennet did. Jane's excellent marriage would also introduce her younger sisters to other rich men. Finally, Mrs. Bennet could place her unmarried daughters in Jane's care. Then she would not need to attend society more often than she wished. Proper manners required her to describe this as a pleasure. In truth, no woman was less likely to enjoy staying home at any age. She ended by wishing that Lady Lucas might soon be equally fortunate. Yet she clearly and proudly believed that this was impossible.

Elizabeth tried unsuccessfully to slow her mother's words and make her whisper more quietly. To her terrible embarrassment, Mr. Darcy sat opposite and heard most of it. Her mother only scolded her for being foolish.

“What is Mr. Darcy to me? Why should I fear him? We do not owe him enough special politeness to avoid saying things he dislikes.”

“For heaven's sake, madam, speak more quietly. What do you gain by offending Mr. Darcy? That will never make his friend think better of you.”

Nothing Elizabeth said had any effect. Her mother continued discussing her plans in the same clear voice. Elizabeth repeatedly blushed with shame and annoyance. She often looked towards Mr. Darcy, and every look confirmed her fear. He did not always watch her mother. Yet Elizabeth knew he listened constantly. His expression slowly changed from angry contempt to calm and serious thought.

At last, Mrs. Bennet had nothing more to say. Lady Lucas had long been yawning at joys she did not expect to share. She was finally left to enjoy cold ham and chicken. Elizabeth began to recover. But the peace did not last long. After supper, people discussed singing. Elizabeth was embarrassed to see Mary agree after very little encouragement. She tried to stop her with meaningful looks and silent requests. It was useless. Mary refused to understand. She loved the chance to perform and began her song. Elizabeth watched with painful feelings. She waited impatiently through several verses, but the ending brought no reward. Among the thanks, someone suggested that Mary might sing again. After half a minute's pause, Mary began another song. Her abilities did not suit such a performance. Her voice was weak and her manner unnatural. Elizabeth suffered terribly. She looked at Jane, but Jane calmly talked to Bingley. Bingley's sisters made mocking signs to each other. Darcy remained completely serious. Elizabeth looked at her father, silently begging him to interfere before Mary sang all night. He understood. After Mary's second song, he said loudly—

“That was very good, child. You have delighted us long enough. Now let the other young women perform.”

Mary pretended not to hear, but was somewhat embarrassed. Elizabeth felt sorry for her and regretted her father's words. She feared her own worry had made matters worse. The guests now asked others to perform.

“If I could sing,” Mr. Collins said, “I would gladly perform a song for the company. Music is an innocent amusement and perfectly suitable for a clergyman. However, I do not mean that we should spend too much time on it. Other duties certainly need attention. A parish rector has much work. First, he must arrange his tithes in a way that helps him without offending his patron. He must write his own sermons. The remaining time is needed for parish duties and care of his home. He must make that home as comfortable as possible. It is also important that he behave attentively and peacefully towards everyone. This is especially true of those who helped him gain his position. I cannot excuse him from that duty. Nor could I respect a man who missed a chance to honour anyone connected with such a family.” He finished with a bow to Mr. Darcy. Half the room had heard his loud speech. Many stared and many smiled. Mr. Bennet looked more amused than anyone. Mrs. Bennet seriously praised Mr. Collins for speaking so wisely. She quietly told Lady Lucas that he was a remarkably clever and good young man.

Elizabeth thought her family could not have embarrassed themselves more successfully if they had planned it. Luckily, Bingley missed some of their performance. His feelings also seemed unlikely to suffer much from the foolishness he did see. Still, his sisters and Mr. Darcy had received an excellent chance to mock her relations. Elizabeth could not decide what was worse: Darcy's silent contempt or the women's rude smiles.

The rest of the evening gave her little pleasure. Mr. Collins remained close beside her and troubled her. He could not persuade her to dance again, but prevented her from dancing with anyone else. She begged him to dance with another woman and offered introductions to anyone present. He said he did not really care about dancing. His main purpose was to win her through gentle attention. Therefore, he intended to stay near her all evening. There was no way to argue with such a plan. Her friend Miss Lucas provided the greatest relief. Charlotte often joined them and kindly drew Mr. Collins into conversation herself.

At least Mr. Darcy paid Elizabeth no more attention. He often stood nearby with no partner but never came close enough to speak. She believed her comments about Mr. Wickham had caused this and felt glad.

The Longbourn group was the last to leave. Through Mrs. Bennet's plan, their carriage arrived fifteen minutes after every other guest had gone. This gave them time to see how strongly some of the family wanted them to leave. Mrs. Hurst and Miss Bingley hardly spoke except to complain of tiredness. They clearly wanted the house to themselves. They rejected every attempt by Mrs. Bennet to talk. The whole group became dull and uncomfortable. Mr. Collins's long speeches offered little relief. He praised Mr. Bingley and his sisters for their elegant entertainment, welcome, and politeness to the guests. Darcy said nothing. Mr. Bennet silently enjoyed the scene. Mr. Bingley and Jane stood slightly apart and spoke only to each other. Elizabeth remained as silent as Mrs. Hurst and Miss Bingley. Even Lydia was too tired to say more than, “Lord, how tired I am!” She gave a huge yawn each time.

At last, they rose to say goodbye. Mrs. Bennet strongly expressed her hope of seeing the whole family soon at Longbourn. She especially told Mr. Bingley that he could dine with them at any time. A formal invitation was unnecessary, and his presence would make them very happy. Bingley was delighted and grateful. He promised to visit at his first opportunity after returning from London. He had to go there briefly the next day.

Mrs. Bennet was completely satisfied when she left. Marriage settlements, new carriages, and wedding clothes would need some preparation. Still, she confidently expected Jane to live at Netherfield within three or four months. She felt equally certain that another daughter would marry Mr. Collins, though that pleased her less. Elizabeth was her least beloved child. Mr. Collins and his position were quite good enough for her. But Mr. Bingley and Netherfield made them seem unimportant.
