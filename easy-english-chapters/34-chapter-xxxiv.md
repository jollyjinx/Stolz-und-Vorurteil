# Chapter XXXIV

After the others left, Elizabeth chose an activity that would increase her anger with Mr. Darcy. She examined every letter Jane had written since Elizabeth came to Kent. Jane made no direct complaint. She did not discuss past events or describe present suffering. Yet every letter and almost every line lacked her usual cheerfulness. Jane’s peaceful mind and kindness towards everyone had once made her writing cheerful. Very little had ever clouded it. Elizabeth now noticed every sentence suggesting unhappiness. She had hardly noticed them during the first reading. Mr. Darcy had shamefully boasted of the misery he could cause. His words made Elizabeth feel Jane’s suffering more sharply. One thought offered some comfort. His Rosings visit would end the day after tomorrow. Even better, Elizabeth would be with Jane again in less than two weeks. Her love could then help restore Jane’s spirits.

Thinking of Darcy’s departure from Kent reminded her that his cousin would leave too. However, Colonel Fitzwilliam had made clear that he had no intentions towards her. He was pleasant, but Elizabeth did not mean to become unhappy about him.

While settling this in her mind, the doorbell suddenly rang. Elizabeth became slightly nervous. Colonel Fitzwilliam had once visited late in the evening. Perhaps he had come to ask especially about her health. This idea quickly disappeared. To her complete amazement, Mr. Darcy entered the room. He hurriedly asked about her health and said he hoped she felt better. Elizabeth answered with cold politeness. He sat for a few moments, then rose and walked around the room. She was surprised but said nothing. After several minutes of silence, he approached her in great emotion and began:—

“I have struggled against my feelings, but in vain. It is useless. I cannot control them. You must allow me to tell you how passionately I admire and love you.”

Elizabeth’s astonishment was impossible to express. She stared, blushed, doubted, and remained silent. Darcy considered this enough encouragement. He immediately declared everything he felt and had long felt for her. He spoke well, but described more than the feelings of his heart. He was as eloquent about pride as about tenderness. He spoke warmly of her lower social position and the shame of such a marriage. He also described the family objections that his judgment had placed against his desire. He spoke as warmly as if he were injuring his own importance. This was unlikely to help his proposal.

Elizabeth had deeply disliked him for a long time. Still, she understood the compliment of such a man’s love. She did not consider accepting him for one moment. At first, she felt sorry for the pain she would cause. Then his later words made her angry, and all pity disappeared. She tried to control herself and answer patiently when he finished. At the end, he described the strength of his love. Despite every effort, he had found it impossible to defeat. He now hoped she would reward it by accepting his hand. Elizabeth easily saw that he expected a favourable answer. He spoke of fear and anxiety, but his face showed real confidence. This only made her more angry. When he stopped, colour rose in her face, and she said,—

“In such cases, it is usual to express gratitude for the feelings declared. This is true even when they are not equally returned. Such gratitude is natural. If I could feel it, I would thank you now. But I cannot. I never wanted your good opinion, and you gave it most unwillingly. I am sorry to cause anyone pain. I did it without knowing and hope the pain will be brief. You say certain feelings long prevented you from admitting your love. After my answer, those same feelings should easily defeat it.”

Mr. Darcy leaned against the fireplace and watched her face. He received her words with as much anger as surprise. His face became pale with rage, and every feature showed his inner struggle. He fought to appear calm and did not speak until he believed he had succeeded. The silence was terrible for Elizabeth. At last, he said with forced calm,—

“And this is the whole answer I have the honour of receiving! Perhaps I might ask why you reject me with so little effort at politeness. But it is not important.”

“I might equally ask,” she replied, “why you clearly meant to offend and insult me. Why did you say you loved me against your will, reason, and even character? If I was rude, does that not excuse it? But I have other reasons for anger, and you know them. Imagine that my own feelings had not decided against you. Suppose they had been indifferent or even favourable. Could anything make me accept the man who destroyed my beloved sister’s happiness, perhaps forever?”

At these words, Mr. Darcy changed colour. His emotion lasted only a moment. He listened without trying to interrupt as she continued,—

“I have every reason to think badly of you. Nothing can excuse your unjust and ungenerous part in that affair. You cannot and dare not deny being the chief cause of their separation. Perhaps you were the only cause. You exposed one person to criticism for changing his mind. You exposed the other to laughter because her hopes failed. You involved both in the deepest misery.”

She paused. With fresh anger, she saw that he listened without any sign of regret. He even looked at her with a smile of pretended disbelief.

“Can you deny doing it?” she repeated.

With forced calm, he replied, “I do not wish to deny it. I did everything possible to separate my friend from your sister. I am pleased with my success. I was kinder to him than to myself.”

Elizabeth refused to notice this polite reference to his own love. She understood its meaning, but it did not calm her.

“But my dislike is not based only on this,” she continued. “Long before it happened, I had decided what you were like. Mr. Wickham’s story many months ago revealed your character. What can you say about that? What imaginary act of friendship will defend you? What false story will you use to deceive others?”

“You take a strong interest in that gentleman’s concerns,” Darcy said less calmly, his face growing redder.

“Who could know his misfortunes without caring about him?”

“His misfortunes!” Darcy repeated with contempt. “Yes, his misfortunes have truly been great.”

“And you caused them!” Elizabeth cried. “You reduced him to his present poverty—poverty compared with his former hopes. You kept back the advantages you knew were intended for him. You took away the independence he deserved during the best years of his life. You did all this! Yet you speak of his misfortunes with contempt and laughter.”

“And this,” Darcy cried, walking quickly across the room, “is your opinion of me! This is how you judge me! Thank you for explaining it fully. According to your calculation, my faults are very serious. But perhaps,” he added, stopping and turning towards her, “you would have ignored them if I had protected your pride. I honestly admitted the objections that long prevented any serious intention. Perhaps you would have hidden these bitter accusations if I had hidden my struggles. With greater cleverness, I could have flattered you. I could have pretended that pure desire, reason, thought, and everything else drove me. But I hate every kind of disguise. I am not ashamed of the feelings I described. They were natural and fair. Could you expect me to celebrate the lower position of your relations? Should I congratulate myself on gaining family so clearly below my own?”

Elizabeth became angrier every moment. Still, she tried her hardest to speak calmly.

“You are mistaken, Mr. Darcy, if you believe your manner of proposing affected me greatly. It only saved me the regret I might have felt in refusing you. I might have regretted it if you had behaved in a more gentlemanlike manner.”

She saw him start but he said nothing. She continued,—

“You could not have proposed in any way that would have tempted me to accept you.”

Again, his astonishment was clear. He looked at her with disbelief and humiliation. She continued,—

“From the beginning—almost from our first meeting—your manners revealed your pride, self-importance, and selfish contempt for others’ feelings. They formed the foundation of my disapproval. Later events built an unchanging dislike upon it. I had not known you for a month when I knew one thing. You were the last man in the world I could ever marry.”

“You have said quite enough, madam. I understand your feelings completely. I can only be ashamed of my own. Forgive me for taking so much of your time. Please accept my best wishes for your health and happiness.”

With these words, he hurried from the room. A moment later, Elizabeth heard the front door open and close. Her mind was now in painful confusion. She felt too weak to stand, sat down, and cried for half an hour. Each review increased her astonishment. Mr. Darcy had proposed to her! He had loved her for many months! He loved her enough to marry despite every objection. Those same objections had made him prevent his friend from marrying her sister. They must be at least as strong in his own case. It was almost unbelievable! Elizabeth was pleased to have inspired such powerful love without knowing it. However, his terrible pride soon overcame her brief pity. He had shamelessly admitted what he did to Jane. He could not justify it but confessed with unforgivable confidence. He had spoken cruelly about Mr. Wickham and had not denied his cruelty towards him.

Elizabeth continued these painful thoughts until she heard Lady Catherine’s carriage. She could not face Charlotte’s watchful attention. She hurried away to her room.
