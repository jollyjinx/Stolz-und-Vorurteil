# Chapter XXV

Mr. Collins spent a week declaring his love and planning future happiness. Then Saturday called him away from his dear Charlotte. He could comfort himself by preparing a home for his bride. He hoped they would choose the wedding day soon after his next return to Hertfordshire. That day would make him the happiest of men. He said goodbye to his Longbourn relations with the same seriousness as before. He again wished his fair cousins health and happiness. He also promised their father another letter of thanks.

The following Monday brought Mrs. Bennet the pleasure of a visit from her brother and his wife. As usual, they came to spend Christmas at Longbourn. Mr. Gardiner was sensible and gentlemanlike. Both his nature and education made him far superior to his sister. The Netherfield ladies would hardly have believed such a man possible. He earned his money through trade and lived within sight of his warehouses. Yet he was very polite and pleasant. Mrs. Gardiner was several years younger than Mrs. Bennet and Mrs. Philips. She was kind, intelligent, and elegant, and her Longbourn nieces loved her. She was especially close to the two eldest girls. They had often stayed with her in London.

On arriving, Mrs. Gardiner first gave out her presents and described the newest fashions. When this was finished, she had a less active duty. It was her turn to listen. Mrs. Bennet had many troubles to describe and many complaints to make. Everyone had treated them badly since her sister’s last visit. Two of her daughters had almost married, and in the end neither marriage happened.

“I do not blame Jane,” she continued, “because she would have married Mr. Bingley if she could. But Lizzy! Oh, sister, it is very hard. She could already be Mr. Collins’s wife, except for her own stubbornness. He proposed to her in this very room, and she refused him. Now Lady Lucas will have a married daughter before I do. Longbourn is still controlled by the entail exactly as before. The Lucases are very clever people, sister. They only think about what they can gain. I am sorry to say it, but it is true. My own family opposes me, and my neighbours think of themselves first. It makes me very nervous and unwell. Still, your visit at this time is my greatest comfort. I am also very glad to hear what you said about long sleeves.”

Mrs. Gardiner already knew most of this news from Jane’s and Elizabeth’s letters. She gave her sister a brief answer. Then, out of pity for her nieces, she changed the subject.

Later, when she was alone with Elizabeth, she spoke more about Jane. “It seems the marriage would have been good for her,” she said. “I am sorry it failed. But such things happen very often! A young man like Mr. Bingley easily loves a pretty girl for a few weeks. Then chance separates them, and he easily forgets her. Such changes of feeling are very common.”

“An excellent kind of comfort,” said Elizabeth, “but it does not fit our case. Chance did not cause our trouble. Friends do not often persuade a rich, independent young man to forget a girl. Only a few days earlier, he had been deeply in love with her.”

“But people use the words ‘deeply in love’ so often and so carelessly. They tell me very little. The words may describe feelings from half an hour’s acquaintance or a truly strong love. How deep was Mr. Bingley’s love?”

“I never saw a love with better promise. He stopped noticing other people and thought only of her. Every meeting made it clearer. At his own ball, he offended two or three young ladies by not asking them to dance. I spoke to him twice and received no answer. Could there be better signs? Is general rudeness not the true heart of love?”

“Oh yes, of the kind of love I think he felt. Poor Jane! I am sorry for her. With her nature, she may not recover quickly. It would have been better if this happened to you, Lizzy. You would have laughed yourself out of it sooner. Do you think she could return to London with us? A different place might help her. A little escape from home may be as useful as anything.”

Elizabeth was extremely pleased with this suggestion. She felt certain that Jane would readily agree.

“I hope,” Mrs. Gardiner added, “that thoughts of this young man will not affect her decision. We live in a very different part of London and know completely different people. As you know, we go out very little. They are very unlikely to meet unless he truly comes to visit her.”

“And that is completely impossible! His friend now guards him, and Mr. Darcy would never allow such a visit. Jane lives in such a part of London! My dear aunt, how could you imagine it? Mr. Darcy may have heard of Gracechurch Street. But after entering it, he would think a month of washing could not make him clean. And you may depend upon it: Mr. Bingley never moves without him.”

“So much the better. I hope they do not meet at all. But does Jane not exchange letters with his sister? Caroline will have to visit.”

“She will end the friendship completely.”

Elizabeth pretended to feel certain about this. She also acted sure that Bingley was being prevented from seeing Jane. However, her own concern proved that she was not completely without hope. Perhaps his love would return. Sometimes she even thought it likely. Jane’s natural attractions might successfully fight the influence of his friends.

Miss Bennet gladly accepted her aunt’s invitation. Her only thought about the Bingleys was a small hope. Caroline did not live in the same house as her brother. Jane might therefore spend an occasional morning with Caroline without meeting him.

The Gardiners stayed at Longbourn for a week. Visits with the Philipses, Lucases, and officers filled every day. Mrs. Bennet had planned so much entertainment that they never had a family dinner alone. When people dined at Longbourn, some officers always came. Mr. Wickham was certain to be one of them. Elizabeth’s warm praise of him had made Mrs. Gardiner watchful. She carefully observed them both. She did not think they were seriously in love. However, their clear preference for each other made her slightly uneasy. Before leaving Hertfordshire, she decided to warn Elizabeth. Encouraging such a love would be unwise.

Wickham could please Mrs. Gardiner for a reason beyond his usual charms. About ten or twelve years earlier, before her marriage, she had spent much time in his part of Derbyshire. They therefore knew many of the same people. Wickham had visited the area rarely since Mr. Darcy’s father died five years earlier. Still, he could give her more recent news of old friends than she had received elsewhere.

Mrs. Gardiner had seen Pemberley and knew the late Mr. Darcy’s reputation very well. This gave them an endless subject. She compared her memories of Pemberley with Wickham’s detailed descriptions. She also praised its late owner’s character. These conversations delighted both of them. Then she learned how the present Mr. Darcy had treated Wickham. She tried to remember reports of Darcy’s character as a boy that agreed with the story. At last, she felt certain she had heard of Mr. Fitzwilliam Darcy as a proud, unpleasant boy.
