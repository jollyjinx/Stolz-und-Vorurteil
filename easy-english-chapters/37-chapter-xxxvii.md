# Chapter XXXVII

The two gentlemen left Rosings the next morning. Mr. Collins had waited near the gates to offer his final respects. He returned home with the pleasing news that both men looked very healthy. Their spirits were as good as anyone could expect after the sad farewell at Rosings. Then he hurried there to comfort Lady Catherine and her daughter. He came back very pleased with a message from her Ladyship. She felt so bored that she strongly wished them all to dine with her.

Elizabeth could not see Lady Catherine without remembering what might have happened. If Elizabeth had chosen differently, she might now have been introduced as her future niece. She smiled as she imagined her Ladyship’s anger. “What would she have said? How would she have behaved?” These questions amused her.

Their first subject was the smaller party now at Rosings. “I assure you, I feel it deeply,” said Lady Catherine. “I believe nobody feels the loss of friends as much as I do. But I am especially fond of these young men. And I know how fond they are of me! They were terribly sorry to leave! But they always are. The dear Colonel kept his spirits up fairly well until the very end. But Darcy seemed to feel it most deeply—more than last year, I think. His love for Rosings is certainly growing.”

Mr. Collins added a compliment and a meaningful hint. Both mother and daughter kindly smiled at them.

After dinner, Lady Catherine noticed that Miss Bennet seemed unhappy. She immediately decided that Elizabeth did not want to return home so soon. Then she added—

“But if so, you must ask your mother to let you stay longer. Mrs. Collins will be very glad of your company, I am sure.”

“I am very grateful to your Ladyship for your kind invitation,” Elizabeth replied. “But I cannot accept it. I must be in London next Saturday.”

“Why, then you will have stayed here only six weeks. I expected you to stay two months. I told Mrs. Collins so before you came. There can be no reason for leaving so soon. Mrs. Bennet could certainly manage without you for another two weeks.”

“But my father cannot. He wrote last week and urged me to return.”

“Oh, of course your father can manage without you if your mother can. Daughters are never so important to a father. If you stay one more complete month, I can take one of you to London. I am going there for a week in early June. Dawson does not mind sitting on the outside seat, so one of you can fit comfortably. Indeed, if the weather is cool, I would even take you both. Neither of you is large.”

“You are extremely kind, madam, but I believe we must follow our original plan.”

Lady Catherine seemed to accept this. “Mrs. Collins, you must send a servant with them. You know I always speak plainly. I cannot bear two young women travelling alone in a hired carriage. It is highly improper. You must find someone to send. I dislike that sort of thing more than anything. Young women should always be properly guarded and attended, according to their social position. When my niece Georgiana went to Ramsgate last summer, I insisted on two male servants. Miss Darcy is the daughter of Mr. Darcy of Pemberley and Lady Anne. She could not properly have travelled in any other way. I pay the closest attention to all such matters. You must send John with the young ladies, Mrs. Collins. I am glad I remembered to mention it. Letting them travel alone would truly bring shame on you.”

“My uncle is sending a servant for us.”

“Oh! Your uncle! He employs a male servant, does he? I am very glad someone considers these things. Where will you change horses? Oh, Bromley, of course. Mention my name at the Bell, and they will attend to you.”

Lady Catherine had many more questions about their journey. Since she did not answer every question herself, Elizabeth had to pay attention. Elizabeth thought this was lucky. Her mind was so busy that otherwise she might have forgotten where she was. She had to save her thoughts for quiet hours alone. Whenever she was alone, thinking gave her the greatest relief. Every day she took a lonely walk and enjoyed all the pain of unpleasant memories.

Elizabeth was quickly learning Mr. Darcy’s letter by heart. She studied every sentence, and her feelings towards him often changed greatly. When she remembered his manner during the proposal, she still felt angry. But when she considered how unfairly she had judged and blamed him, she became angry with herself. She felt pity for his disappointed feelings. His love made her grateful, and his general character earned her respect. Yet she could not approve of him. She did not regret refusing him or wish to see him again. Her own past behaviour constantly caused shame and regret. The unhappy faults of her family troubled her even more, and seemed impossible to correct. Her father was satisfied with laughing at them. He would never try to control his youngest daughters’ wild foolishness. Her mother’s own manners were so poor that she could not see the harm. Elizabeth and Jane had often tried to stop Catherine and Lydia from behaving carelessly. But their mother encouraged them, so how could they improve? Catherine was weak, easily annoyed, and completely guided by Lydia. She always felt insulted by their advice. Lydia was determined and careless, and hardly listened. Both girls were uneducated, lazy, and vain. While an officer remained in Meryton, they would flirt with him. While Meryton was within walking distance of Longbourn, they would always go there.

Elizabeth also worried constantly about Jane. Mr. Darcy’s explanation restored all her former good opinion of Bingley. This made Jane’s loss seem even greater. Bingley’s love had been sincere, and his actions were free from blame. His only possible fault was trusting his friend completely. Jane had lost a situation that offered every advantage and every hope of happiness. How painful that her own family’s foolish and improper behaviour had caused it!

She also now understood Wickham’s true character. These thoughts deeply affected her usually cheerful spirits. It became almost impossible for her to seem reasonably happy.

During her final week, they visited Rosings as often as at the beginning. They spent their very last evening there. Her Ladyship again asked every detail about their journey and explained the best packing methods. She insisted that dresses must be placed in the one correct way. Afterwards, Maria felt forced to undo the morning’s work and pack her trunk again.

At parting, Lady Catherine kindly lowered herself enough to wish them a good journey. She invited them to visit Hunsford again next year. Miss de Bourgh made the great effort of curtseying and offering each woman her hand.
