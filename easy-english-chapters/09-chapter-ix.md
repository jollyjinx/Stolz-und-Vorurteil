# Chapter IX

Elizabeth spent most of the night in Jane's room. Early in the morning, Mr. Bingley sent a housemaid to ask about Jane. Elizabeth was pleased to give a fairly hopeful answer. Later, the two elegant women who served his sisters also asked. Despite Jane's improvement, Elizabeth sent a note to Longbourn. She wanted her mother to visit and judge Jane's condition herself. The note went immediately, and Mrs. Bennet quickly obeyed it. With her two youngest daughters, she reached Netherfield soon after the family's breakfast.

If Jane had appeared dangerously ill, Mrs. Bennet would have been miserable. But she saw that Jane was not in danger. Therefore, she did not want an immediate recovery, which might send Jane away from Netherfield. She refused to consider taking Jane home. The medical man arrived at about the same time and also advised against moving her. Mrs. Bennet sat with Jane for a while. Then Miss Bingley appeared and invited them downstairs. The mother and three daughters followed her into the breakfast room. Bingley met them and hoped Mrs. Bennet had not found Miss Bennet worse than expected.

“Indeed I have, sir,” she answered. “She is far too ill to move. Mr. Jones says we must not even consider it. We must trouble you with her a little longer.”

“Move her!” cried Bingley. “No one should consider it. I am sure my sister will not allow her to leave.”

“You may depend on it, madam,” Miss Bingley said with cold politeness. “Miss Bennet will receive every possible care while she remains here.”

Mrs. Bennet thanked her at great length.

“Without such good friends, I do not know what would happen to her,” she added. “She is very ill and suffers greatly, though with perfect patience. She always behaves that way. She has the sweetest temper I have ever known. I often tell my other girls that they are nothing beside her. This is a beautiful room, Mr. Bingley, with a charming view across the gravel path. I know no country house equal to Netherfield. I hope you will not leave quickly, though your rental agreement is short.”

“Everything I do, I do quickly,” he replied. “If I decided to leave Netherfield, I would probably go in five minutes. At present, however, I consider myself firmly settled here.”

“That is exactly what I would have expected of you,” said Elizabeth.

“You are beginning to understand me?” he cried, turning toward her.

“Oh yes. I understand you perfectly.”

“I wish I could take that as a compliment. But being so easy to understand may be rather sad.”

“That depends. A deep, complicated character is not necessarily better or worse than yours.”

“Lizzy,” cried her mother, “remember where you are. Do not speak wildly as your family lets you do at home.”

“I did not know you studied character,” Bingley immediately continued. “It must be an entertaining study.”

“Yes, but complicated characters are the most entertaining. At least they have that advantage.”

“The countryside usually offers few people for such study,” said Darcy. “In a country neighbourhood, society is small and changes little.”

“But people themselves change so much that there is always something new to observe.”

“Yes, indeed,” cried Mrs. Bennet, offended by his comment about the country. “I assure you that people change just as much in the country as in London.”

Everyone was surprised. Darcy looked at her for a moment and silently turned away. Mrs. Bennet imagined that she had completely defeated him and continued her triumph.

“I do not see any great advantage in London except shops and public entertainment. The country is much pleasanter, is it not, Mr. Bingley?”

“When I am in the country, I never want to leave,” he replied. “When I am in London, I feel almost the same. Each has advantages, and I can be equally happy in either.”

“Yes, because you have the right nature. But that gentleman,” she said, looking at Darcy, “seemed to think the country was worthless.”

“Indeed, Mama, you are mistaken,” said Elizabeth, blushing for her mother. “You misunderstood Mr. Darcy. He only meant that the country has less variety of people than London. You must admit that is true.”

“Certainly, my dear. No one denied it. But this neighbourhood has many people. Few neighbourhoods can be larger. We dine with twenty-four families.”

Only concern for Elizabeth helped Bingley avoid laughing. His sister was less careful and gave Mr. Darcy a meaningful smile. Elizabeth wanted to change her mother's thoughts. She asked whether Charlotte Lucas had visited Longbourn since Elizabeth left.

“Yes, she came yesterday with her father. What a pleasant man Sir William is, Mr. Bingley! Do you not agree? He is so fashionable, elegant, and relaxed. He always has something to say to everyone. That is good breeding. People who think themselves important and never speak understand nothing about it.”

“Did Charlotte dine with you?”

“No, she insisted on going home. I suppose they needed her to make the mince pies. I always keep servants who can do their own work, Mr. Bingley. My daughters were raised differently. But everyone must judge for herself. The Lucas girls are very good, I assure you. It is a pity they are not handsome! I do not think Charlotte is very plain, of course, because she is our close friend.”

“She seems a very pleasant young woman,” said Bingley.

“Oh yes, but you must admit she is very plain. Lady Lucas often says so herself and envies Jane's beauty. I do not like praising my own child, but one rarely sees anyone prettier than Jane. Everyone says so, so this is not only a mother's opinion. When she was fifteen, a gentleman at my brother Gardiner's in London loved her deeply. My sister-in-law was sure he would propose before we left. But he did not. Perhaps he thought her too young. Still, he wrote some very pretty poems about her.”

“And that ended his love,” Elizabeth said impatiently. “Many men have probably been cured the same way. I wonder who first discovered that poetry drives away love?”

“I have always considered poetry the food of love,” said Darcy.

“It may feed a fine, strong, healthy love. Everything feeds what is already strong. But if the feeling is weak and thin, one good poem will starve it completely.”

Darcy only smiled. The silence afterward made Elizabeth fear her mother would embarrass herself again. She wanted to speak but found no subject. After a short pause, Mrs. Bennet again thanked Mr. Bingley for his kindness to Jane. She also apologised for troubling him with Lizzy. Mr. Bingley answered with natural politeness. He made his younger sister say the polite words expected from her too. Miss Bingley did so without warmth, but Mrs. Bennet was satisfied. Soon she ordered her carriage. At this signal, Lydia came forward. The two youngest girls had whispered throughout the visit. They had decided Lydia should challenge Mr. Bingley about his promise to give a ball at Netherfield.

Lydia was a strong, well-grown girl of fifteen, with clear skin and a cheerful face. She was her mother's favourite. Mrs. Bennet's affection had brought Lydia into society at an early age. Lydia had great energy and natural confidence. The officers' attention had increased that confidence into boldness. Her uncle's good dinners and her easy manners had made her popular with them. She was therefore quite ready to speak to Mr. Bingley about the ball. She suddenly reminded him of his promise. Not keeping it, she added, would be the most shameful thing in the world. His answer delighted her mother.

“I am completely ready to keep my promise. When your sister recovers, you may choose the exact day of the ball. But you would not wish to dance while she is ill?”

Lydia declared herself satisfied. “Oh yes, waiting until Jane is well would be much better. Captain Carter will probably be back in Meryton by then. After you give your ball,” she added, “I shall demand one from the officers too. I will tell Colonel Forster he should be ashamed if he refuses.”

Mrs. Bennet and her daughters then left. Elizabeth immediately returned to Jane. She left the two ladies and Mr. Darcy to discuss her family's behaviour. However, despite all Miss Bingley's jokes about fine eyes, Darcy refused to criticise Elizabeth.
