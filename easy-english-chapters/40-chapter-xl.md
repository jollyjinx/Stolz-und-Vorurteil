# Chapter XL

Elizabeth could no longer resist telling Jane what had happened. She decided to hide every detail concerning her sister. After warning Jane to expect a surprise, she described the main events between Mr. Darcy and herself.

Miss Bennet’s surprise soon became weaker because of her loving opinion of Elizabeth. To Jane, anyone’s admiration of Elizabeth seemed perfectly natural. Other feelings then replaced all surprise. She regretted that Mr. Darcy had expressed himself in such an unattractive manner. But she felt even more sorrow for the pain Elizabeth’s refusal must have caused him.

“He was wrong to be so certain of success,” she said. “And he certainly should not have shown it. But think how much that confidence must increase his disappointment.”

“Indeed,” Elizabeth replied, “I am deeply sorry for him. But he has other feelings that will probably soon end his love for me. Still, you do not blame me for refusing him?”

“Blame you! Oh, no.”

“But you blame me for speaking so strongly in Wickham’s defence?”

“No—I do not know that you were wrong to say those things.”

“But you will know after I tell you what happened the next day.”

She then described the letter and repeated everything it said about George Wickham. What a terrible shock for poor Jane! She would gladly have lived without believing one person could contain so much wickedness. Darcy’s defence pleased her, but could not comfort her after such a discovery. She worked hard to prove that there might be some mistake. She tried to clear one man without accusing the other.

“That will not work,” said Elizabeth. “You can never make both men good. Choose one, because you must be satisfied with only one. Together they have just enough goodness to make one decent man. Recently, that goodness has moved between them quite often. I now believe it all belongs to Mr. Darcy, but you may choose differently.”

Still, some time passed before Jane could be made to smile.

“I cannot remember ever feeling more shocked,” she said. “Wickham so very bad! It is almost impossible to believe. And poor Mr. Darcy! Dear Lizzy, think what he must have suffered. Such disappointment! And he knew you thought badly of him! He also had to reveal something so painful about his sister. It is truly too upsetting. I am certain you must feel that too.”

“Oh no, watching you feel so much regret and pity has ended all of mine. I know you will give him more than enough justice. So every moment, I become less concerned and more indifferent. Your great generosity makes me careful with mine. If you mourn him much longer, my heart will become as light as a feather.”

“Poor Wickham! His face looks so good! And his manner seems so open and gentle.”

“Someone certainly educated those two young men very badly. One received all the goodness. The other received only its appearance.”

“I never thought Mr. Darcy looked as completely without goodness as you once did.”

“Yet I meant to be unusually clever by firmly disliking him without any reason. Such dislike encourages cleverness and creates many chances for jokes. You can constantly insult someone without saying anything fair. But you cannot always laugh at a man without sometimes accidentally saying something clever.”

“Lizzy, when you first read that letter, I am certain you could not joke about it.”

“Indeed, I could not. I felt deeply uncomfortable—very uncomfortable. I might even say unhappy. And I had nobody to tell about my feelings. Jane was not there to comfort me. You could not say I was less weak, vain, and foolish than I knew I was! Oh, how I wanted you!”

“How unfortunate that you spoke so strongly about Wickham to Mr. Darcy. Now your words seem completely undeserved.”

“Certainly. But speaking bitterly naturally followed from the unfair beliefs I had encouraged. There is one matter where I want your advice. Should I tell everyone we know about Wickham’s true character?”

Miss Bennet paused before replying. “Surely there is no reason to expose him so terribly. What do you think?”

“I think we should not try. Mr. Darcy did not permit me to share his information. In fact, he meant every detail about his sister to remain as private as possible. And if I explain the rest of Wickham’s behaviour, who will believe me? The dislike of Mr. Darcy in Meryton is so strong. Showing him as a good man would almost kill half the good people there. I cannot manage that task. Wickham will soon leave, so his true character will not matter to anyone here. People will discover everything later. Then we can laugh because they were too foolish to see it before. For now, I will say nothing.”

“You are completely right. Making his faults public might ruin him forever. Perhaps he now regrets his actions and wants to rebuild his good name. We must not make him lose all hope.”

This conversation calmed Elizabeth’s troubled mind. She had shared two secrets that had weighed on her for two weeks. Whenever she wanted to discuss them again, Jane would willingly listen. But another secret remained, and good sense prevented her from revealing it. She could not share the other half of Mr. Darcy’s letter. She could not explain how sincerely Bingley had valued Jane. This was knowledge that nobody else could share. Only a complete understanding between Jane and Bingley could allow Elizabeth to end this last mystery. “And then,” she thought, “I could only tell what Bingley himself could explain much more pleasantly. I cannot be free to speak until the information has lost all its value!”

Now that Elizabeth was settled at home, she could observe Jane’s true feelings. Jane was not happy. She still loved Bingley very tenderly. She had never before even imagined herself in love. So her feelings had all the warmth of first love. Because of her age and character, they were also steadier than first love often is. She warmly treasured his memory and preferred him to every other man. She needed all her good sense and concern for her friends to control her sorrow. Otherwise, it could damage her health and disturb their peace.

“Well, Lizzy,” Mrs. Bennet said one day, “what do you now think of Jane’s sad affair? I have decided never to speak about it again to anybody. I told my sister Philips so recently. But I cannot discover that Jane saw him at all in London. Well, he is a very undeserving young man. I suppose there is no chance in the world that she will ever marry him now. Nobody says he will return to Netherfield this summer. I have even asked everyone who might know.”

“I do not believe he will ever live at Netherfield again.”

“Oh, well! He may choose as he likes. Nobody wants him to come. Still, I will always say that he treated my daughter terribly. If I were Jane, I would not accept it. My comfort is that Jane will certainly die of a broken heart. Then he will regret what he has done.”

Elizabeth found no comfort in this expectation, so she did not answer.

“Well, Lizzy,” her mother soon continued, “the Collinses live very comfortably, do they? Well, well, I only hope it continues. What sort of meals do they provide? Charlotte is an excellent manager, I am sure. If she is half as clever as her mother, she saves enough money. I am certain their household spends nothing unnecessary.”

“No, nothing at all.”

“They manage everything very well, depend upon it. Yes, yes. They will make sure not to spend beyond their income. They will never have money troubles. Well, I hope they enjoy it! I suppose they often discuss owning Longbourn after your father dies. They probably already consider it theirs whenever that happens.”

“They could not discuss that subject in front of me.”

“No, that would have been strange. But I am sure they often discuss it privately. Well, perhaps they can comfortably accept an estate that is not legally theirs. I would be ashamed to own one only because it was legally promised to me.”
