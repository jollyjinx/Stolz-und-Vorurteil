# Chapter XXXVI

When Mr. Darcy gave Elizabeth the letter, she did not expect another proposal. She had formed no expectation about its contents. Still, she read it eagerly, and it caused strongly opposing feelings. She could hardly understand her emotions. At first, she was amazed that he believed he could defend himself. She felt certain that shame should prevent any explanation he might offer. She began his Netherfield account with strong prejudice against every word. She read so eagerly that she could hardly understand it. She was too impatient for the next sentence to attend to the present one. She immediately decided his belief in Jane’s indifference was false. His worst objections to the marriage made her too angry to judge him fairly. He expressed no regret that satisfied her. His style was proud, not sorry. Everything was pride and insulting confidence.

Then came his account of Mr. Wickham, and Elizabeth read more carefully. If true, these events would destroy every valued belief about Wickham’s goodness. They also had a frightening similarity to his own story. Her feelings became more painful and harder to understand. Surprise, fear, and even horror overcame her. She wanted to reject it completely. “This must be false! This cannot be! It must be the worst lie!” she repeatedly cried. She reached the end while hardly understanding the final page or two. Then she quickly put it away. She declared that she would ignore it and never look at it again.

She walked on with a disturbed mind that could settle on nothing. But this was impossible. After half a minute, she opened the letter again. Controlling herself as well as possible, she reread the painful account of Wickham. This time, she forced herself to examine every sentence. The history of his connection with the Pemberley family exactly matched his own story. The late Mr. Darcy’s kindness also agreed with Wickham’s words, although Elizabeth had not known its full extent. So far, each account supported the other. However, they differed greatly about the will. Elizabeth clearly remembered Wickham’s exact words about the parish position. One side had to be completely dishonest. For a few moments, she persuaded herself that her wishes were correct. Then she carefully reread the details that followed. Wickham had given up every claim to the position and received three thousand pounds instead. Again, Elizabeth had to hesitate. She lowered the letter and tried to judge every detail fairly. She considered the likelihood of each statement but achieved little. Both sides had only made claims. She read on again. Every line increasingly proved something she had considered impossible. The facts could make Mr. Darcy completely innocent throughout the whole affair.

Mr. Darcy openly accused Mr. Wickham of wastefulness and years of bad behaviour. This greatly shocked Elizabeth, especially because she could not prove it unfair. She had never heard of Wickham before he joined the ——shire militia. A young man had met him by chance in London, renewed a slight acquaintance, and encouraged him to join. Nobody in Hertfordshire knew anything about Wickham’s earlier life except what he himself told them. Elizabeth could have sought information about his true character, but she had never wanted it. His face, voice, and manners had immediately given him every good quality in her mind. She tried to remember one truly good or honest action that could defend him. At least, his goodness might outweigh occasional errors. She wished to describe Mr. Darcy’s account of years of laziness and vice as only such errors. But no memory helped her. She could clearly imagine all Wickham’s charming appearance and manner. Yet she remembered no real goodness beyond his general popularity. His social skills had also won the officers’ affection. After thinking for a long time, she continued reading. Unfortunately, his plan against Miss Darcy received some support from Colonel Fitzwilliam. Only the previous morning, he had spoken of his role in his cousin’s affairs. Mr. Darcy also named him as a witness to every detail. Elizabeth had no reason to question Colonel Fitzwilliam’s character. For a moment, she almost decided to ask him. Then she considered how awkward it would be. Finally, she knew Mr. Darcy would never offer such proof unless his cousin would confirm it.

Elizabeth perfectly remembered her first evening with Wickham at Mr. Philips’s. Many of his expressions remained clear. Now she saw how improper it was to tell such personal matters to a stranger. She wondered why she had not noticed before. He had pushed forward his own story without good feeling. His claims also disagreed with his behaviour. He had boasted that he did not fear meeting Mr. Darcy. Mr. Darcy could leave the county, but Wickham would stand his ground. Yet the next week, Wickham avoided the Netherfield ball. Until the Netherfield family left, he told nobody except Elizabeth. Afterwards, everyone heard and discussed the story. Then he had no hesitation about destroying Mr. Darcy’s character. Yet he had said respect for the father would always prevent him from exposing the son.

How differently everything about Wickham now appeared! His attention to Miss King now came from a hateful desire for money alone. Her moderate fortune no longer proved that his wishes were modest. It showed his eagerness to take anything. His behaviour towards Elizabeth could have no good reason. He had misunderstood her fortune, or he had encouraged her preference to satisfy his pride. Elizabeth believed she had shown that preference too carelessly. Every remaining effort to defend him weakened. She also had to admit further reasons for Mr. Darcy’s innocence. When Jane questioned Mr. Bingley long ago, he said Darcy was blameless. Darcy’s manners were proud and unpleasant. Still, throughout their acquaintance, Elizabeth had never seen him behave without principle or justice. Recently, they had often met, and she knew his ways fairly well. Nothing suggested immoral or godless habits. His own relations respected and valued him. Even Wickham had admitted Darcy was a good brother. Elizabeth often heard Darcy speak lovingly about his sister, proving he could feel affection. If Wickham’s claims were true, Darcy’s terrible injustice could hardly remain hidden. Friendship between such a man and the pleasant Mr. Bingley made no sense.

Elizabeth became completely ashamed of herself. She could not think of Darcy or Wickham without feeling blind, unfair, prejudiced, and foolish.

“How badly I have acted!” she cried. “I was proud of my judgment and valued my abilities! I often looked down on my sister’s generous fairness. I pleased my pride with useless and mistaken distrust. How humiliating this discovery is! Yet I deserve it! Even love could not have made me more blindly foolish. But pride, not love, caused my error. One man’s preference pleased me, and the other’s neglect offended me. From the beginning, I chose prejudice and ignorance and drove away reason where either man was concerned. Until this moment, I never knew myself.”

Her thoughts moved from herself to Jane, and then from Jane to Bingley. She remembered that Mr. Darcy’s explanation had seemed very weak and read it again. The second reading had a completely different effect. She had been forced to trust his statements about Wickham. How could she refuse them trust about Jane? Darcy said he had never suspected her sister’s love. Elizabeth remembered Charlotte had always held the same opinion. She also had to accept the justice of his description. Jane’s feelings were strong but little displayed. Her face and manner always showed a calm friendliness not usually connected with deep emotion.

Then Elizabeth reached the part about her family. Darcy’s criticism was humiliating but deserved, and she felt deep shame. The accusation was too just to deny. He had especially referred to events at the Netherfield ball. They confirmed all his first objections and had affected Elizabeth as strongly as they affected him.

She noticed his compliment to herself and Jane, and it gave some comfort. Still, it could not remove the shame her family had brought upon itself. Jane’s disappointment was truly the work of her closest relations. Their improper behaviour had also badly damaged both sisters’ reputation. Elizabeth felt more depressed than ever before.

For two hours, Elizabeth wandered along the lane. She allowed every kind of thought, reconsidered events, and decided what was likely. She tried to accept this sudden, important change. At last, tiredness and the memory of her long absence made her return. She entered wishing to appear cheerful as usual. She decided to control thoughts that made conversation impossible.

She immediately learned that both gentlemen from Rosings had visited. Mr. Darcy had stayed only a few minutes to say goodbye. Colonel Fitzwilliam had remained for at least an hour, hoping Elizabeth would return. He had almost decided to walk after her until he found her. Elizabeth could barely pretend regret at missing him. She was truly glad. Colonel Fitzwilliam no longer interested her. She could think only of the letter.
