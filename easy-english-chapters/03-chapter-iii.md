# Chapter III

Mrs. Bennet and her five daughters asked Mr. Bennet every possible question about Mr. Bingley. Still, they could not make him give them a useful description. They tried direct questions, clever guesses, and quiet suggestions. He escaped them all. At last, they had to accept news from their neighbour Lady Lucas. Her report was extremely positive. Sir William had liked Mr. Bingley very much. He was young, remarkably handsome, and extremely pleasant. Best of all, he planned to bring a large party to the next public ball. Nothing could be more delightful. A man who loved dancing could easily fall in love. They had great hopes for Mr. Bingley's heart.

“If I can see one daughter happily living at Netherfield,” Mrs. Bennet told her husband, “and all the others equally well married, I will want nothing more.”

A few days later, Mr. Bingley returned Mr. Bennet's visit. He sat with him in the library for about ten minutes. He had hoped to see the young ladies, because he had heard much about their beauty. However, he saw only their father. The ladies had slightly better luck. From an upstairs window, they discovered that he wore a blue coat and rode a black horse.

Soon afterward, they sent him a dinner invitation. Mrs. Bennet had already planned dishes that would show her skill as a housekeeper. Then his reply arrived and delayed everything. Mr. Bingley had to be in London the next day, so he could not accept their kind invitation. Mrs. Bennet was deeply troubled. She could not imagine what business took him to London so soon after arriving in Hertfordshire. Perhaps he would always rush from place to place and never properly settle at Netherfield. Lady Lucas calmed her by suggesting he had gone to London to gather guests for the ball. Soon people reported that he would bring twelve ladies and seven gentlemen. The girls were unhappy about so many ladies. The day before the ball, better news comforted them. Instead of twelve ladies, he had brought only six people from London: his five sisters and a cousin. When the party entered the ballroom, there were only five people altogether. They were Mr. Bingley, his two sisters, the older sister's husband, and another young man.

Mr. Bingley was handsome and behaved like a gentleman. His face was pleasant, and his manner was relaxed and natural. His sisters were elegant and clearly fashionable. His brother-in-law, Mr. Hurst, simply looked like a gentleman. But Mr. Darcy quickly drew everyone's attention. He was tall and handsome, with a noble appearance. Within five minutes, everyone knew that he had ten thousand pounds a year. The men called him an impressive figure. The women said he was much handsomer than Mr. Bingley. People admired him for half the evening. Then his behaviour destroyed his popularity. He seemed proud, above the company, and impossible to please. Even his great estate in Derbyshire could not save him. People decided his face was cold and unpleasant, and that he could not compare with his friend.

Mr. Bingley soon knew all the important people in the room. He was lively and open, danced every dance, and disliked the ball ending early. He even spoke about giving a ball at Netherfield. Such pleasant qualities needed no explanation. What a contrast with his friend! Mr. Darcy danced once with Mrs. Hurst and once with Miss Bingley. He refused introductions to every other lady. For the rest of the evening, he walked around and sometimes spoke to his own party. Everyone had decided his character. He was the proudest, most unpleasant man alive, and they hoped he would never return. Mrs. Bennet disliked him most violently. Her general dislike became personal because he had treated one of her daughters badly.

There were too few gentlemen, so Elizabeth Bennet had to sit out two dances. During that time, Mr. Darcy stood near enough for her to hear him. Mr. Bingley left the dance briefly and urged his friend to join it.

“Come, Darcy,” he said. “You must dance. I hate seeing you stand alone in this foolish way. You would be much better dancing.”

“I certainly will not. You know I hate dancing unless I know my partner well. At a public ball like this, it would be unbearable. Your sisters already have partners. Dancing with any other woman here would be a punishment.”

“I would not be as difficult to please as you, even for a kingdom!” cried Bingley. “I have never met so many pleasant girls in one evening. Several of them are unusually pretty.”

“You are dancing with the only handsome girl here,” said Mr. Darcy, looking at the eldest Miss Bennet.

“Oh, she is the most beautiful woman I have ever seen! But one of her sisters sits just behind you. She is very pretty and probably very pleasant. Let me ask my partner to introduce you.”

“Which one?” Darcy turned and looked at Elizabeth for a moment. Their eyes met, and he looked away. Then he said coldly, “She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me. I will not honour young ladies whom other men have ignored. Return to your partner and enjoy her smiles. You are wasting your time with me.”

Mr. Bingley followed his advice, and Mr. Darcy walked away. Elizabeth did not feel warmly toward Darcy after that. However, she told the story to her friends with great energy. She was lively and playful and enjoyed anything ridiculous.

The whole family had a pleasant evening. Mrs. Bennet saw the Netherfield party admire her eldest daughter. Mr. Bingley danced with Jane twice, and his sisters gave her special attention. Jane was as pleased as her mother, though she showed it quietly. Elizabeth understood Jane's happiness. Mary heard someone call her the most accomplished girl in the neighbourhood while speaking to Miss Bingley. Catherine and Lydia always had partners. So far, that was all they cared about at a ball. They returned happily to Longbourn, the village where they lived as its leading family. Mr. Bennet was still awake. When reading, he forgot the time. Tonight, he also wanted to hear about the evening that had raised such hopes. He had rather hoped his wife's plans for the stranger would fail. He soon learned that she had a very different story.

“Oh, my dear Mr. Bennet,” Mrs. Bennet said as she entered, “we had a wonderful evening and an excellent ball. I wish you had been there. Everyone admired Jane. They all said she looked beautiful. Mr. Bingley thought so too, and he danced with her twice. Imagine that, my dear—twice! She was the only woman he asked again. First, he danced with Miss Lucas. I hated seeing that, but he did not admire her at all. No one can, you know. Then he noticed Jane during the dance. He asked who she was, got an introduction, and requested the next two dances. His third pair was with Miss King, his fourth with Maria Lucas, his fifth with Jane again, and his sixth with Lizzy. Then came the Boulanger—”

“If he had any pity for me,” her husband cried impatiently, “he would have danced much less! For God's sake, say no more about his partners. I wish he had sprained his ankle in the first dance!”

“Oh, my dear,” Mrs. Bennet continued, “I am delighted with him. He is extremely handsome, and his sisters are charming. I have never seen dresses more elegant than theirs. The lace on Mrs. Hurst's gown—”

He interrupted her again. Mr. Bennet refused to hear any description of fine clothes. Therefore, she had to choose another subject. With bitter feeling and some exaggeration, she described Mr. Darcy's shocking rudeness.

“But I assure you,” she added, “Lizzy loses nothing by failing to please him. He is a horrible, unpleasant man and not worth pleasing. He was so proud and conceited that no one could bear him. He walked here and there, believing himself terribly important. Lizzy was not handsome enough for him to dance with! I wish you had been there to silence him with one of your answers. I truly hate that man.”
