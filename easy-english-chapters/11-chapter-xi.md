# Chapter XI

After dinner, the ladies left the dining room. Elizabeth ran to Jane and made sure she was warmly protected. Then she helped Jane into the drawing room. Her two friends welcomed her with many expressions of pleasure. During the hour before the gentlemen arrived, Elizabeth had never found them so pleasant. They had real conversational skill. They could accurately describe an event, tell a funny story, and laugh energetically about their acquaintances.

When the gentlemen entered, Jane was no longer Miss Bingley's main interest. Her eyes turned immediately to Darcy, and she spoke before he had crossed the room. Mr. Darcy went directly to Miss Bennet and politely congratulated her. Mr. Hurst gave a small bow and said he was “very glad.” But only Bingley's greeting had warmth and length. He was full of joy and care. For the first half-hour, he built up the fire so Jane would not become cold. At his request, she moved farther from the door. Then he sat beside her and spoke to almost no one else. Elizabeth worked in the opposite corner and watched with delight.

After tea, Mr. Hurst reminded Miss Bingley about cards, but without success. She had privately learned that Mr. Darcy did not want to play. Mr. Hurst soon found his open request refused. She said nobody planned to play, and everyone's silence seemed to support her. Therefore, Mr. Hurst stretched out on a sofa and went to sleep. Darcy took a book, and Miss Bingley did the same. Mrs. Hurst played mainly with her rings and bracelets. Sometimes she joined her brother's conversation with Miss Bennet.

Miss Bingley watched Mr. Darcy read as much as she read her own book. She continually asked questions or looked at his page. She could not draw him into conversation. He only answered briefly and continued reading. At last, her own book had completely failed to entertain her. She had chosen it only because it was the second volume of his. She gave a great yawn and said, “What a pleasant way to spend an evening! There is truly no enjoyment like reading. One becomes tired of everything else much sooner than a book. When I own a house, I shall be miserable without an excellent library.”

Nobody answered. She yawned again, threw away her book, and looked around for entertainment. Then she heard her brother mention a ball to Miss Bennet. She suddenly turned and said:

“By the way, Charles, are you serious about holding a ball at Netherfield? Before deciding, you should ask what this group wants. Unless I am mistaken, some people here would find a ball a punishment rather than a pleasure.”

“If you mean Darcy,” cried her brother, “he may go to bed before it begins. But the ball is settled. As soon as Nicholls has made enough white soup, I shall send the invitations.”

“I would like balls far more if people organised them differently,” she replied. “The usual process is unbearably dull. It would be much more sensible if everyone conversed instead of dancing.”

“Much more sensible, my dear Caroline, certainly. But it would be much less like a ball.”

Miss Bingley did not answer. Soon she stood and walked around the room. She had an elegant figure and walked well. But Darcy, for whose benefit she did it, continued firmly reading. In desperation, she tried once more. Turning to Elizabeth, she said:

“Miss Eliza Bennet, let me persuade you to walk around the room with me. It is very refreshing after sitting in one position so long.”

Elizabeth was surprised but immediately agreed. Miss Bingley also achieved her real purpose: Mr. Darcy looked up. Such behaviour interested him as much as it surprised Elizabeth. Without realising it, he closed his book. They immediately invited him to join them, but he refused. He could imagine only two reasons for their walking together. His presence would interfere with either reason. What could he mean? Miss Bingley desperately wanted to know and asked whether Elizabeth understood him.

“Not at all,” Elizabeth answered. “But he certainly means to judge us harshly. The surest way to disappoint him is to ask nothing.”

Miss Bingley could not bear to disappoint Mr. Darcy in anything. She continued demanding an explanation of his two reasons.

“I have no objection to explaining,” he said when she let him speak. “Either you are close friends with secrets to discuss, or you know walking displays your figures well. In the first case, I would be in your way. In the second, I can admire you better from my seat by the fire.”

“Oh, shocking!” cried Miss Bingley. “I have never heard anything so horrible. How shall we punish him for that speech?”

“Nothing is easier if you truly wish to,” said Elizabeth. “We can all trouble and punish one another. Tease him and laugh at him. You know him well, so you must know how.”

“Honestly, I do not. Our friendship has not taught me that. Tease such calmness and control? No, he can defeat us there. As for laughter, let us not embarrass ourselves by laughing without a reason. Mr. Darcy may enjoy his victory.”

“Mr. Darcy cannot be laughed at!” cried Elizabeth. “That is an unusual advantage. I hope it remains unusual because knowing many such people would be a great loss. I dearly love to laugh.”

“Miss Bingley,” he said, “gives me too much credit. Even the wisest and best men can seem ridiculous. Even their wisest actions can be mocked by someone who lives only to make jokes.”

“Certainly,” Elizabeth replied, “such people exist, but I hope I am not one. I hope I never mock anything wise or good. But foolishness, nonsense, strange habits, and inconsistency amuse me. I laugh at them whenever I can. I suppose you have none of these faults.”

“Perhaps no one can be completely free of them. But I have always tried to avoid weaknesses that make strong minds appear ridiculous.”

“Such as vanity and pride.”

“Yes, vanity is a weakness. But when someone truly has a superior mind, pride will always be properly controlled.”

Elizabeth turned away to hide a smile.

“Your examination of Mr. Darcy is finished, I suppose,” said Miss Bingley. “What is your result?”

“I am completely convinced that Mr. Darcy has no fault. He openly says so himself.”

“No,” said Darcy, “I have never claimed that. I have enough faults, though I hope not in my understanding. I cannot promise anything good about my temper. It is too unwilling to bend, certainly too much for the world's comfort. I cannot quickly forget other people's foolishness, wickedness, or injuries against me. Attempts to influence me do not easily change my feelings. People might call my temper resentful. Once I lose my good opinion, it is lost forever.”

“That is a real fault!” cried Elizabeth. “Never-ending anger is a dark mark in a character. But you chose your fault carefully. I cannot laugh at it. You are safe from me.”

“Every character naturally leans toward some particular evil. Even the best education cannot overcome that natural fault.”

“And your fault is a tendency to hate everybody.”

“And yours,” he replied with a smile, “is choosing to misunderstand them.”

“Let us have some music,” cried Miss Bingley, tired of a conversation that excluded her. “Louisa, you will not mind if I wake Mr. Hurst.”

Her sister did not object, and they opened the piano. After thinking for a few moments, Darcy was not sorry. He had begun to feel the danger of giving Elizabeth too much attention.
