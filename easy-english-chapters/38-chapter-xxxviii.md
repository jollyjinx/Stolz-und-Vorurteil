# Chapter XXXVIII

On Saturday morning, Elizabeth and Mr. Collins reached breakfast a few minutes before the others. He used the chance to offer the farewell politeness he considered completely necessary.

“I do not know, Miss Elizabeth,” he said, “whether Mrs. Collins has thanked you for visiting us. But I am certain you will not leave without receiving her thanks. I assure you, we have greatly valued your company. We know how little our humble home can offer anyone. Our simple life, small rooms, few servants, and limited society must make Hunsford extremely dull to you. But I hope you believe we are grateful that you lowered yourself to visit. We have done everything possible to prevent you from having an unpleasant time.”

Elizabeth quickly thanked him and assured him that she had been happy. She had greatly enjoyed the six weeks. Charlotte’s company and the kind attention she received made her truly grateful. Mr. Collins was pleased, and replied with a more smiling seriousness—

“It gives me the greatest pleasure to hear that your time was not unpleasant. We have certainly done our best. Fortunately, we could introduce you to very important society. Our connection with Rosings often allowed us to escape our humble home. So I think we may congratulate ourselves that your Hunsford visit was not entirely tiresome. Our position with Lady Catherine’s family is an extraordinary advantage and blessing. Few people can claim such a thing. You see how close we are to them and how often they invite us. Truly, despite all this humble parish house’s disadvantages, nobody living here deserves pity. Not while they share our close relationship with Rosings.”

His feelings were too grand for words, so he had to walk around the room. Meanwhile, Elizabeth tried to combine politeness and truth in a few short sentences.

“In fact, my dear cousin, you can give Hertfordshire a very favourable report of us. At least, I hope you can. Every day you have witnessed Lady Catherine’s great attention to Mrs. Collins. Altogether, I hope your friend does not appear to have made an unfortunate—but silence is wiser here. Let me only assure you, my dear Miss Elizabeth, of my sincere hope for you. I wish you equal happiness in marriage. My dear Charlotte and I share one mind and one way of thinking. Our characters and ideas are remarkably alike in every matter. We seem to have been made for each other.”

Elizabeth could safely say that such a marriage was a great happiness. She could also sincerely say she believed in and celebrated his comfortable home life. Still, she welcomed the interruption when the lady responsible for those comforts entered. Poor Charlotte! Elizabeth felt sad to leave her with such company. But Charlotte had chosen it with full understanding. She clearly regretted her visitors’ departure, yet did not seem to ask for pity. Her home and household, her parish and chickens, and all their concerns still pleased her.

At last the carriage arrived. The trunks were fastened on, the packages placed inside, and everything was ready. The friends said an affectionate goodbye, and Mr. Collins walked Elizabeth to the carriage. As they crossed the garden, he gave her messages of respect for her entire family. He remembered his thanks for their kindness at Longbourn during winter. He also sent compliments to Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner, though he had never met them. He helped Elizabeth inside, and Maria followed. The door was almost closed when he suddenly looked worried. He reminded them that they had forgotten to leave any message for the Rosings ladies.

“But,” he added, “of course you wish me to give them your humble respects. I shall also offer your grateful thanks for their kindness during your stay.”

Elizabeth did not object. The door was finally allowed to close, and the carriage drove away.

“Good heavens!” cried Maria after a few silent minutes. “It seems only a day or two since we arrived! Yet so many things have happened!”

“A great many indeed,” her companion said with a sigh.

“We dined at Rosings nine times, and drank tea there twice! I will have so much to tell!”

Elizabeth privately added, “And I will have so much to hide!”

They travelled with little conversation and no frightening event. Four hours after leaving Hunsford, they reached Mr. Gardiner’s house. They would remain there for a few days.

Jane looked well, but Elizabeth had little chance to study her mood. Their aunt’s kindness had arranged many activities for them. But Jane would return home with her. At Longbourn, Elizabeth would have plenty of time to observe her.

Meanwhile, Elizabeth struggled to wait until Longbourn before telling Jane about Mr. Darcy’s proposals. She could reveal something that would greatly surprise Jane. It would also please the vanity that Elizabeth had not yet reasoned away. The temptation to speak openly was very strong. Only two concerns stopped her. She still did not know how much she should reveal. Once she began, she feared saying something about Bingley that would cause Jane more pain.
