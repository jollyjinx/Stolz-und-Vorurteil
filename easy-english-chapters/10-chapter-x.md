# Chapter X

The day passed much like the previous one. Mrs. Hurst and Miss Bingley spent several morning hours with Jane, who slowly improved. In the evening, Elizabeth joined the group in the drawing room. This time, there was no loo table. Mr. Darcy was writing a letter. Miss Bingley sat near him and watched its progress. She repeatedly interrupted him with messages for his sister. Mr. Hurst and Mr. Bingley played piquet, while Mrs. Hurst watched.

Elizabeth took up some needlework and enjoyed listening to Darcy and Miss Bingley. She continually praised his handwriting, straight lines, or long letter. He received every compliment with complete indifference. Their strange conversation perfectly matched Elizabeth's opinion of them both.

“How delighted Miss Darcy will be to receive such a letter!”

He did not answer.

“You write unusually fast.”

“You are mistaken. I write rather slowly.”

“How many letters you must write in a year! Business letters too! I would hate them.”

“Then it is fortunate that I must write them instead of you.”

“Please tell your sister that I long to see her.”

“I have already told her once because you asked me.”

“I fear you dislike your pen. Let me sharpen it for you. I sharpen pens remarkably well.”

“Thank you, but I always sharpen my own.”

“How do you manage to write such straight lines?”

He remained silent.

“Tell your sister I am delighted by her improvement on the harp. Also tell her I adore her beautiful little table design. I think it far better than Miss Grantley's.”

“May I delay your excitement until my next letter? I have no room to describe it properly now.”

“Oh, it does not matter. I shall see her in January. But do you always write such charming long letters, Mr. Darcy?”

“They are usually long. I cannot decide whether they are always charming.”

“I have a rule: anyone who can easily write a long letter cannot write badly.”

“That does not compliment Darcy, Caroline,” cried her brother, “because he does not write easily. He thinks too hard about words with four syllables. Do you not, Darcy?”

“My writing style is very different from yours.”

“Oh,” cried Miss Bingley, “Charles writes more carelessly than anyone. He leaves out half his words and covers the rest with ink marks.”

“My ideas move so quickly that I cannot express them in time. Therefore, my letters sometimes express no ideas at all.”

“Your modesty, Mr. Bingley,” said Elizabeth, “makes criticism impossible.”

“Nothing is more misleading than apparent modesty,” said Darcy. “It is often careless thinking and sometimes a hidden boast.”

“Which of the two was my recent little piece of modesty?”

“The hidden boast. You are actually proud of your writing faults. You believe they come from quick thought and careless action. You consider those qualities interesting, even if they are not admirable. People often value their ability to do things quickly. They ignore the poor result. This morning you told Mrs. Bennet you could leave Netherfield five minutes after deciding. You meant this as praise of yourself. But what is admirable about such speed? It must leave important work unfinished and helps no one.”

“No,” cried Bingley, “this is too much. You remember at night every foolish thing said in the morning. Still, I honestly believed my words and believe them now. At least I did not pretend to make quick decisions merely to impress the ladies.”

“You probably believed it. But I doubt you would leave so quickly. Chance controls your actions as much as anyone's. Imagine you were mounting your horse and a friend said, ‘Bingley, stay until next week.’ You would probably stay and not leave at all. With one more request, you might stay a month.”

“You have only proved,” cried Elizabeth, “that Mr. Bingley described himself unfairly. You have praised him much more than he praised himself.”

“I am extremely pleased,” said Bingley, “that you changed my friend's words into praise of my sweet temper. But I fear he intended nothing of the kind. He would respect me more if I firmly refused and rode away as fast as possible.”

“Would Mr. Darcy think your stubborn loyalty to the plan corrected your original foolish speed?”

“Honestly, I cannot explain it exactly. Darcy must speak for himself.”

“You expect me to explain opinions you call mine but which I never admitted. Still, let us accept your description. Remember, Miss Bennet, the friend supposedly asking Bingley to delay has only asked. He has offered no argument showing that delay is wise.”

“So easily agreeing with a friend's request has no value for you.”

“Agreeing without being convinced shows little respect for either person's intelligence.”

“You seem to give no importance to friendship and affection, Mr. Darcy. Caring about the person asking often makes us agree without demanding reasons. I am not speaking especially about your example concerning Mr. Bingley. We should perhaps wait until it happens before judging his behaviour. But consider an ordinary matter between friends. One asks the other to change an unimportant decision. Would you think badly of that person for agreeing before hearing an argument?”

“Before continuing, should we not define exactly how important the request is? We should also define how close the two people are.”

“Certainly,” cried Bingley. “Let us hear every detail, including their different heights and sizes. That matters more than you realise, Miss Bennet. If Darcy were not so tall compared with me, I would respect him only half as much. At certain times and places, Darcy is the most frightening person I know. This is especially true in his own house on Sunday evenings when he has nothing to do.”

Mr. Darcy smiled, but Elizabeth thought he looked slightly offended and stopped herself from laughing. Miss Bingley angrily defended him and scolded her brother for speaking such nonsense.

“I see your plan, Bingley,” said his friend. “You dislike an argument and want to silence this one.”

“Perhaps I do. Arguments are too similar to quarrels. If you and Miss Bennet wait until I leave, I will be grateful. Then you may say whatever you like about me.”

“Your request costs me nothing,” said Elizabeth. “Mr. Darcy should finish his letter.”

Mr. Darcy followed her advice and completed his letter.

Afterward, he asked Miss Bingley and Elizabeth to provide some music. Miss Bingley quickly moved to the piano. She politely asked Elizabeth to play first. Elizabeth refused equally politely but more firmly, so Miss Bingley sat down.

Mrs. Hurst sang with her sister. Elizabeth stood by the piano, turning pages in music books. She noticed how often Mr. Darcy watched her. She could hardly believe such an important man admired her. Yet looking because he disliked her seemed even stranger. At last, she decided he noticed her because he found more faults in her than anyone else. This idea did not hurt her. She liked him too little to care about his approval.

After some Italian songs, Miss Bingley played a lively Scottish tune. Mr. Darcy soon came near Elizabeth and spoke.

“Do you not feel a great wish, Miss Bennet, to use this chance to dance a reel?”

She smiled but did not answer. Surprised by her silence, he repeated the question.

“Oh,” she said, “I heard you the first time. I could not immediately decide how to answer. You wanted me to say yes, so you could despise my taste. But I enjoy defeating such plans and preventing expected contempt. Therefore, I have decided to say I do not want to dance a reel. Now despise me if you dare.”

“Indeed, I do not dare.”

Elizabeth had expected to offend him and was amazed by his polite answer. But her manner mixed sweetness with playful cleverness, making it difficult for her to offend anyone. Darcy had never been so enchanted by any woman. He truly believed he would be in danger if her family connections were not socially beneath his.

Miss Bingley saw or suspected enough to become jealous. Her desire for dear Jane's recovery now had extra help. She wanted Elizabeth gone.

She often tried to make Darcy dislike Elizabeth. She joked about their imagined marriage and planned his happiness in such a family.

“When this desirable event happens,” she said as they walked in the garden next day, “I hope you teach your mother-in-law to remain silent. If possible, cure the younger girls of chasing officers. May I mention a delicate subject? Try to control the small amount of pride and rudeness in your lady.”

“Do you have any other plans for my married happiness?”

“Oh yes. Put portraits of your uncle and aunt Philips in the Pemberley gallery. Place them beside the portrait of your great-uncle, the judge. Mr. Philips and the judge work in the same profession, only at different levels. Do not try to have Elizabeth's portrait painted. What artist could copy those beautiful eyes?”

“Capturing their expression would indeed be difficult. But an artist could copy their colour, shape, and remarkably fine eyelashes.”

At that moment, Mrs. Hurst and Elizabeth came toward them along another path.

“I did not know you planned to walk,” said Miss Bingley. She was embarrassed and feared they had heard her.

“You treated us horribly,” Mrs. Hurst answered. “You ran away without telling us you were going out.”

She took Mr. Darcy's free arm, leaving Elizabeth to walk alone. The path had room for only three. Mr. Darcy noticed their rudeness and immediately said:

“This path is not wide enough for our group. We should go into the avenue.”

Elizabeth had no wish to remain with them. She answered with a laugh.

“No, stay where you are. You make a charming group and look unusually fine. Adding a fourth would spoil the beautiful picture. Goodbye.”

She ran cheerfully away. As she wandered, she happily hoped to return home in a day or two. Jane had recovered enough to plan two hours outside her room that evening.
