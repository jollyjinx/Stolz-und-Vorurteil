# Chapter XLVIII

Everyone hoped for a letter from Mr. Bennet the next morning. But the post brought no message from him. His family knew he was usually a careless and slow letter writer. At such a time, however, they had expected him to make an effort. They concluded that he had no good news. Still, they would have preferred to know this for certain. Mr. Gardiner had waited only for the post before leaving.

After he left, they could at least expect regular reports. Their uncle promised to persuade Mr. Bennet to return to Longbourn soon. This greatly comforted Mrs. Bennet. She thought his return was the only way to prevent his death in a duel.

Mrs. Gardiner and the children planned to remain in Hertfordshire for several more days. She thought her presence might help her nieces. She helped them care for Mrs. Bennet and comforted them during their free hours. Aunt Philips also visited often, always claiming that she meant to cheer them. Yet each visit brought a new story about Wickham’s wasteful or improper behaviour. Therefore, she usually left them more unhappy than before.

All Meryton tried to darken the character of a man they had called an angel three months earlier. People said he owed money to every tradesman in town. They also claimed he had tried to attract women in every tradesman’s family. Every such affair was called a seduction. Everyone now called him the wickedest young man in the world. Everyone also discovered that they had always distrusted his appearance of goodness. Elizabeth believed less than half of these stories. But she believed enough to feel even more certain of Lydia’s ruin. Jane believed even fewer stories, but she too became almost hopeless. If the couple had gone to Scotland, they should have heard something by now. Until then, Jane had never completely abandoned that hope.

Mr. Gardiner left Longbourn on Sunday. His wife received a letter from him on Tuesday. He had found Mr. Bennet immediately and persuaded him to stay at Gracechurch Street. Before Mr. Gardiner arrived, Mr. Bennet had visited Epsom and Clapham without learning anything useful. He now planned to question people at all the main London hotels. He thought the couple might have stayed in one before finding private rooms. Mr. Gardiner expected no success from this plan. But his brother was eager, so he would help. Mr. Bennet still refused to leave London. Mr. Gardiner promised to write again very soon. He also added:

“I have written to Colonel Forster. I asked him to question Wickham’s close friends in the regiment. Perhaps Wickham has relations or connections who know where he is hiding in London. Finding someone who could give us such a clue would be extremely important. At present, nothing guides us. I am sure Colonel Forster will do everything possible. On further thought, however, perhaps Lizzy knows more about Wickham’s living relations than anyone else.”

Elizabeth understood why her uncle respected her knowledge. But she could not provide information as useful as his compliment deserved.

She had never heard of any relations except Wickham’s father and mother. Both had been dead for many years. Still, some companions in the ——shire regiment might know more. Elizabeth did not strongly expect success, but the inquiry gave them something to hope for.

Every day at Longbourn was full of anxiety. The most anxious time came when they expected the post. Letters were the main object of each impatient morning. Any good or bad news would arrive in them. Every new day was expected to bring something important.

Before Mr. Gardiner wrote again, a different letter arrived for Mr. Bennet. It was from Mr. Collins. Jane had instructions to open her father’s letters during his absence, so she read it. Elizabeth knew Mr. Collins’s letters were always remarkable and read over Jane’s shoulder. It said:

“My dear Sir,

“Our family relationship and my position in life require me to express my sympathy. We learned yesterday from a Hertfordshire letter about your terrible suffering. Be certain, dear sir, that Mrs. Collins and I sincerely feel for you and your respectable family. Your distress must be especially bitter because time can never remove its cause. I will offer every possible argument to reduce this severe misfortune. I will try to comfort you in a situation that must be most painful to a parent. Your daughter’s death would have been a blessing beside this. The matter is even sadder for another reason. My dear Charlotte tells me that too much freedom probably led your daughter to this immoral behaviour. Still, you and Mrs. Bennet may find comfort in one thought. Her natural character must be bad, or she could not commit such a terrible act at so young an age. Whatever the truth, you deserve great pity. Mrs. Collins, Lady Catherine, and her daughter all share this opinion. I have told them the whole affair. They agree that one daughter’s false step will harm all her sisters’ chances. As Lady Catherine kindly says, who would now marry into such a family? This thought makes me even more satisfied about a certain event last November. If that event had ended differently, I would now share all your sorrow and disgrace. Let me advise you, dear sir, to comfort yourself as much as possible. Remove your unworthy daughter from your love for ever. Leave her to suffer the results of her terrible offence.

“I am, dear sir,” and so on.

Mr. Gardiner did not write again until Colonel Forster answered him. Then he had no pleasant news. Wickham had no known relations with whom he remained in contact. He certainly had no close living relation. He had once known many people. Since joining the militia, however, he had formed no especially close friendship. No one seemed likely to know where he was. His terrible financial position also gave him a strong reason to hide. He feared discovery by Lydia’s relations, but he also had large gambling debts. Colonel Forster believed it would take more than a thousand pounds to pay his Brighton expenses. Wickham owed much money in the town, and his gambling debts were even worse. Mr. Gardiner told the Longbourn family all these facts. Jane heard them with horror. “A gambler!” she cried. “I never expected this. I had no idea.”

Mr. Gardiner added that their father should return the following day, Saturday. Every attempt had failed, and Mr. Bennet had lost hope. He finally accepted his brother-in-law’s request to return to his family. Mr. Gardiner would continue the search and do whatever seemed wise. When Mrs. Bennet heard this, she showed less pleasure than her children expected. Earlier, she had feared greatly for her husband’s life.

“What! Is he coming home without poor Lydia?” she cried. “Surely he will not leave London before finding them. If he returns, who will fight Wickham and make him marry her?”

Mrs. Gardiner now wished to return home. They decided that she and her children would travel to London as Mr. Bennet returned. The family coach took them through the first part of their journey. Then it brought its master back to Longbourn.

Mrs. Gardiner left with all her questions about Elizabeth and her Derbyshire friend. Those questions had followed her from Derbyshire. Elizabeth had never freely mentioned his name. Mrs. Gardiner had half expected a letter from him, but none came. Since returning, Elizabeth had received no letter that could have come from Pemberley.

The family’s unhappy state fully explained Elizabeth’s low spirits. Therefore, no one could fairly guess anything from her sadness. But Elizabeth now understood her own feelings fairly well. Without knowing Darcy, she could have faced the fear of Lydia’s disgrace more easily. She thought it would have saved her one sleepless night in every two.

When Mr. Bennet arrived, he showed his usual calm and thoughtful appearance. He spoke as little as usual and did not mention his business in London. Some time passed before his daughters dared to discuss it.

That afternoon, he joined them at tea. Elizabeth finally dared to introduce the subject. She briefly expressed sorrow for what he had suffered. He answered, “Say nothing about that. Who should suffer except me? My own actions caused this, so I should feel it.”

“You must not judge yourself too severely,” replied Elizabeth.

“You are right to warn me against that danger. Human nature so easily becomes too hard on itself! No, Lizzy, let me understand my blame once in my life. I do not fear the feeling will defeat me. It will pass soon enough.”

“Do you think they are in London?”

“Yes. Where else could they hide so well?”

“And Lydia always wanted to go to London,” added Kitty.

“Then she is happy,” said her father dryly. “And she will probably stay there for some time.”

After a short silence, he continued, “Lizzy, I am not angry that your advice last May proved right. Considering what happened, forgiving me shows a generous spirit.”

Miss Bennet interrupted them when she came to collect her mother’s tea.

“This performance does us good,” he cried. “It gives misfortune such elegance! Another day I will do the same. I will sit in my library wearing my nightcap and dressing gown. I will cause as much trouble as possible. Or perhaps I will wait until Kitty runs away.”

“I am not going to run away, Papa,” said Kitty crossly. “If I ever went to Brighton, I would behave better than Lydia.”

“You go to Brighton! I would not trust you as close to it as Eastbourne for fifty pounds! No, Kitty, I have finally learned caution. You will suffer the results. No officer may ever enter my house again or even pass through the village. Balls are completely forbidden unless one of your sisters dances with you. And you may never leave the house until you prove something. You must spend ten minutes of every day in a sensible way.”

Kitty believed all these threats and began to cry.

“Well, well,” he said, “do not be unhappy. If you are a good girl for the next ten years, I will take you to a military review at the end.”
