# Chapter XLIX

Two days after Mr. Bennet returned, Jane and Elizabeth walked in the garden behind the house. They saw the housekeeper coming towards them. They expected her to call them to their mother and went to meet her. Instead, she said to Miss Bennet, “Please excuse my interruption, madam. I hoped you had received good news from London, so I came to ask.”

“What do you mean, Hill? We have heard nothing from London.”

“Dear madam,” cried Mrs. Hill in great surprise, “do you not know an express messenger came? Mr. Gardiner sent him to the master. He arrived half an hour ago, and the master has a letter.”

The girls ran away without taking time to speak. They hurried through the entrance into the breakfast room and then the library. Their father was in neither place. They were about to look upstairs with their mother when the butler met them. He said:

“If you are looking for my master, madam, he is walking towards the little wood.”

They immediately ran through the hall again and crossed the lawn. Their father was walking calmly towards a small wood beside the field.

Jane was less quick and less used to running than Elizabeth, so she fell behind. Elizabeth reached her father while struggling for breath and cried eagerly:

“Oh, Papa, what news? Have you heard from my uncle?”

“Yes, an express messenger brought a letter from him.”

“And what news does it contain? Is it good or bad?”

“What good news could we expect?” he said, taking the letter from his pocket. “But perhaps you would like to read it.”

Elizabeth impatiently took it from his hand. Jane now reached them.

“Read it aloud,” said their father. “I hardly know what it says myself.”

“Gracechurch Street, Monday, August 2.

“My dear Brother,

“At last I can send news of my niece. On the whole, I hope it will satisfy you. Soon after you left on Saturday, I was fortunate enough to discover their part of London. I will save the details until we meet. It is enough to know they have been found. I have seen them both—”

“Then it is as I always hoped!” cried Jane. “They are married!”

Elizabeth continued reading: “I have seen them both. They are not married, and I cannot find that they planned to marry. But I hope they will marry soon if you accept the promises I made for you. You must secure your daughter’s equal share of the five thousand pounds. This money will be divided among your children after your death and my sister’s. You must also promise her one hundred pounds a year during your life. Considering everything, I did not hesitate to accept these conditions for you. I went only as far as I believed I had authority to go. I am sending this by express so your answer will arrive quickly. These details show that Mr. Wickham’s position is not as hopeless as people believe. People have been mistaken about that. I am glad to say some money will remain after all his debts are paid. This will be settled on my niece in addition to her own fortune. I expect you will give me full authority to act for you in this business. I will then tell Haggerston to prepare a proper legal settlement immediately. You have no reason to return to London. Stay quietly at Longbourn and trust my care and effort. Send your answer as soon as possible and write very clearly. We think my niece should marry from this house, and I hope you approve. She comes to us today. I will write again when anything more is decided. Yours and so on.

“Edw. Gardiner.”

“Is it possible?” cried Elizabeth when she finished. “Can he truly mean to marry her?”

“Then Wickham is not as unworthy as we thought,” said her sister. “My dear father, I congratulate you.”

“Have you answered the letter?” asked Elizabeth.

“No, but I must answer soon.”

She earnestly begged him not to delay any longer.

“Oh, my dear father,” she cried, “come back and write at once. Every moment is important in such a matter.”

“Let me write for you,” said Jane, “if you dislike the trouble.”

“I dislike it very much,” he replied, “but it must be done.”

With these words, he turned and walked back towards the house with them.

“And may I ask?” said Elizabeth. “I suppose you must accept the conditions.”

“Accept them! I am only ashamed that he asks so little.”

“And they must marry! Yet Wickham is such a man.”

“Yes, yes, they must marry. Nothing else can be done. But I greatly want to know two things. First, how much money has your uncle paid to arrange it? Second, how can I ever repay him?”

“Money! My uncle!” cried Jane. “What do you mean, sir?”

“No sensible man would marry Lydia for such a small reward. I will give her one hundred pounds a year while I live and fifty pounds after my death.”

“That is true,” said Elizabeth. “I had not thought of it before. His debts will be paid, and money will remain! Oh, my uncle must have arranged this! What a good and generous man. I fear he has caused himself financial trouble. A small sum could not achieve all this.”

“No,” said her father. “Wickham is a fool if he accepts less than ten thousand pounds. I would dislike thinking badly of him at the beginning of our family relationship.”

“Ten thousand pounds! Heaven forbid! How could we repay even half that amount?”

Mr. Bennet did not answer. All three remained silent and thoughtful until they reached the house. Their father went to write in the library. The girls entered the breakfast room.

“And they truly will marry!” cried Elizabeth when they were alone. “How strange this is, and we must be grateful for it. They have little chance of happiness, and his character is terrible. Yet we must be glad they will marry! Oh, Lydia!”

“One thought comforts me,” replied Jane. “He surely would not marry Lydia without real affection for her. Our kind uncle may have helped to clear his debts. But I cannot believe he paid ten thousand pounds or anything close to it. He has children and may have more. How could he spare five thousand pounds?”

“If we learn the size of Wickham’s debts, we will know what Mr. Gardiner did,” said Elizabeth. “We must also learn how much Wickham settles on Lydia. Wickham does not own sixpence. We can never repay our aunt and uncle’s kindness. They have taken her home and given her their protection and support. Years of gratitude cannot repay such a sacrifice for her sake. She is actually with them now! If such goodness does not make her ashamed, she will never deserve happiness. Imagine her first meeting with my aunt!”

“We must try to forget everything that happened on both sides,” said Jane. “I hope and trust they will still be happy. His agreement to marry proves that he now thinks correctly. Their love will make them steadier. I hope they will settle quietly and live sensibly. In time, people may forget their past foolishness.”

“Neither you nor I nor anyone else can ever forget their behaviour,” replied Elizabeth. “There is no use discussing it.”

The girls now realised that their mother probably knew nothing about the news. They went to the library and asked if their father wanted them to tell her. He was writing and answered calmly without lifting his head:

“As you please.”

“May we take my uncle’s letter and read it to her?”

“Take whatever you like and go away.”

Elizabeth took the letter from his desk, and they went upstairs together. Mary and Kitty were both with Mrs. Bennet, so one reading would inform them all. They gently prepared everyone for good news and then read the letter aloud. Mrs. Bennet could hardly control herself. Jane read Mr. Gardiner’s hope that Lydia would soon marry. At once, her mother’s joy burst out. Every sentence made it stronger. Delight now made her as wildly excited as fear and anger had before. Knowing that her daughter would marry was enough. She had no fear for Lydia’s happiness and no shame about her behaviour.

“My dear, dear Lydia!” she cried. “This is wonderful! She will marry! I will see her again! She will marry at sixteen! My good, kind brother! I knew he would manage everything. I long to see her and dear Wickham too! But the clothes—the wedding clothes! I will write to my sister Gardiner at once. Lizzy, run downstairs and ask your father how much he will give her. Wait, I will go myself. Kitty, ring for Hill. I will dress in a moment. My dear, dear Lydia! How happy we will be when we meet!”

Her eldest daughter tried to calm this wild joy. She reminded her mother how much they all owed Mr. Gardiner.

“We owe this happy ending largely to his kindness,” she added. “We believe he has promised to help Mr. Wickham with money.”

“Well,” cried her mother, “that is completely right. Who should help except her own uncle? If he had no children, my children and I would receive all his money. This is the first thing he has given us except a few presents. Oh, I am so happy! Soon I will have a married daughter. Mrs. Wickham! How good it sounds! She was only sixteen last June. My dear Jane, I am too excited to write. I will speak, and you can write for me. We will discuss the money with your father later. But we must order the clothes at once.”

She began describing cotton, muslin, and fine linen. She would soon have ordered a great amount. With some difficulty, Jane persuaded her to wait until her father could be consulted. One day’s delay would make little difference. Her mother was too happy to be as stubborn as usual. Then more plans entered her mind.

“I will go to Meryton as soon as I am dressed,” she said. “I will tell my sister Philips the good, good news. On the way back, I can visit Lady Lucas and Mrs. Long. Kitty, run downstairs and order the carriage. Fresh air will do me great good. Girls, can I bring you anything from Meryton? Oh, here is Hill. My dear Hill, have you heard the good news? Miss Lydia will marry. You will all have a large bowl of punch to celebrate her wedding.”

Mrs. Hill immediately expressed her joy. Elizabeth received her congratulations with everyone else. Then she became sick of such foolishness and escaped to her room to think freely. Poor Lydia’s position must still be bad, even at best. Yet Elizabeth had to be thankful it was not worse. She truly felt this. Looking forward, she could expect neither sensible happiness nor financial success for Lydia. But only two hours earlier, they had feared far worse. Looking back at that fear, Elizabeth saw every advantage they had gained.
