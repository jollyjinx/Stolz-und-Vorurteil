# Chapter XX

Mr. Collins did not spend long quietly considering his successful love. Mrs. Bennet had waited in the hall for the conversation to end. She saw Elizabeth open the door, pass quickly, and climb the stairs. Then Mrs. Bennet entered the breakfast room. She warmly congratulated both Mr. Collins and herself on their closer family connection. He received and returned these congratulations with equal pleasure. Then he described the conversation. He believed its result was completely satisfying. His cousin's firm refusal naturally came from shy modesty and the true delicacy of her character.

This news shocked Mrs. Bennet. She would have liked to share his confidence that Elizabeth's protests encouraged him. But she did not dare believe it and said so.

“But depend upon this, Mr. Collins,” she added. “Lizzy will be made reasonable. I shall speak to her immediately. She is a stubborn, foolish girl and does not understand her own interests. But I shall make her understand.”

“Forgive my interruption, madam,” Mr. Collins cried. “But if she is truly stubborn and foolish, I question whether she would suit me as a wife. A man in my position naturally expects marriage to bring happiness. If she continues refusing me, perhaps we should not force her to accept. With such faults of character, she could not add much to my happiness.”

“Sir, you completely misunderstand me,” Mrs. Bennet said with alarm. “Lizzy is stubborn only in matters like this. In every other way, no girl is kinder. I shall go directly to Mr. Bennet. I am sure we shall settle the matter with her very soon.”

She gave him no time to answer. She hurried to her husband and called as she entered the library—

“Oh, Mr. Bennet, you are needed immediately! Everything is in confusion. You must make Lizzy marry Mr. Collins because she swears she will not. If you do not hurry, he will change his mind and refuse her.”

Mr. Bennet looked up from his book. He calmly watched her face. Her news did not change his expression at all.

“I do not have the pleasure of understanding you,” he said when she finished. “What are you discussing?”

“Mr. Collins and Lizzy. Lizzy says she will not accept Mr. Collins. Now Mr. Collins begins to say he will not accept Lizzy.”

“And what should I do? The situation seems hopeless.”

“Speak to Lizzy yourself. Tell her you insist that she marry him.”

“Call her downstairs. She shall hear my opinion.”

Mrs. Bennet rang the bell, and someone called Miss Elizabeth to the library.

“Come here, child,” her father said when she appeared. “I sent for you about an important matter. I understand Mr. Collins has offered to marry you. Is that true?”

Elizabeth said it was.

“Very well. And have you refused this marriage offer?”

“I have, sir.”

“Very well. Now we reach the main point. Your mother insists that you accept. Is that not true, Mrs. Bennet?”

“Yes, or I shall never see her again.”

“You face an unhappy choice, Elizabeth. From today, you must become a stranger to one parent. Your mother will never see you again if you do not marry Mr. Collins. I shall never see you again if you do.”

Elizabeth had to smile at this ending after such a beginning. Mrs. Bennet had convinced herself that her husband shared her wishes. She was terribly disappointed.

“What do you mean by speaking this way, Mr. Bennet? You promised to insist that she marry him.”

“My dear,” her husband replied, “I have two small requests. First, let me use my own understanding on this matter. Second, let me use my own room. I would like the library to myself as soon as possible.”

Despite her disappointment in her husband, Mrs. Bennet did not give up. She spoke to Elizabeth again and again. First she begged, then threatened her. She tried to persuade Jane to take her side. With perfect gentleness, Jane refused to interfere. Elizabeth sometimes answered seriously and sometimes with playful cheerfulness. Her manner changed, but her decision never did.

Meanwhile, Mr. Collins considered the event alone. He thought too well of himself to understand why Elizabeth might refuse. His pride was hurt, but he felt no other pain. His feelings for her were completely imaginary. Perhaps she deserved her mother's criticism. That possibility prevented any regret.

During this family confusion, Charlotte Lucas came to spend the day. Lydia met her in the hall and rushed towards her. In a half-whisper, she cried, “I am glad you came because we have such amusement! Guess what happened this morning. Mr. Collins proposed to Lizzy, and she refuses him.”

Charlotte hardly had time to answer before Kitty joined them with the same news. Then they entered the breakfast room, where Mrs. Bennet was alone. She immediately began discussing the subject too. She asked Miss Lucas for sympathy and begged her to persuade Lizzy to obey her family. “Please do, my dear Miss Lucas,” she added sadly. “Nobody supports me or takes my side. Everyone treats me cruelly. Nobody cares about my poor nerves.”

Jane and Elizabeth entered before Charlotte had to answer.

“Yes, here she comes,” Mrs. Bennet continued. “She looks perfectly calm. She cares no more for us than if we were in York, as long as she gets her own way. But listen, Miss Lizzy. If you continue refusing every marriage offer, you will never find a husband. I do not know who will support you after your father dies. I cannot keep you, so I warn you. From this very day, I am finished with you. In the library, I said I would never speak to you again. You will see that I keep my word. I find no pleasure in speaking to disobedient children. Indeed, speaking to anyone gives me little pleasure. People with nervous illnesses like mine have no great desire to talk. Nobody can understand my suffering! But it is always the same. People who do not complain never receive sympathy.”

Her daughters listened silently. They knew that reasoning with her or comforting her would only increase her anger. She continued without interruption until Mr. Collins entered. He looked more grand and formal than usual. When Mrs. Bennet saw him, she said to the girls—

“Now I insist that all of you remain silent. Let Mr. Collins and me speak together.”

Elizabeth quietly left the room, and Jane and Kitty followed. Lydia stayed because she wanted to hear everything possible. Charlotte was also delayed. First, Mr. Collins politely asked detailed questions about her and her whole family. Then curiosity kept her there. She walked to the window and pretended not to listen. Mrs. Bennet sadly began the planned conversation.

“Oh, Mr. Collins!”

“My dear madam,” he replied, “let us never discuss this matter again. I would never be angry with your daughter.” His voice then showed his displeasure. “We must all calmly accept troubles we cannot avoid. This is especially the duty of a young man who gained a good position as early as I did. I trust that I have accepted the situation. Perhaps this is easier because I doubt my beautiful cousin would truly have made me happy. I often notice that acceptance becomes perfect when a denied blessing loses some value in our eyes. My dear madam, I hope I do not disrespect your family by ending my request for your daughter's favour. I have not asked you and Mr. Bennet to use your authority for me. Perhaps I was wrong to accept my dismissal from your daughter instead of her parents. But everyone makes mistakes. I certainly meant well throughout the matter. I wished to find a pleasant companion for myself while also helping your whole family. If my behaviour deserves criticism, I ask permission to apologize.”
