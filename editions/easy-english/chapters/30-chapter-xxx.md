# Chapter XXX

Sir William stayed only a week at Hunsford. That was long enough to satisfy him completely about his daughter. She had a comfortable home, an unusual husband, and an unusual neighbour. During his visit, Mr. Collins spent each morning driving him around the country in his small carriage. After Sir William left, the household returned to its usual activities. Elizabeth was thankful that this did not give her more time with her cousin. Between breakfast and dinner, he usually worked in the garden or stayed in his study. There he read, wrote, and looked from the window facing the road. The ladies’ sitting room was at the back. At first, Elizabeth wondered why Charlotte did not use the dining room instead. It was larger and had a more pleasant position. She soon understood Charlotte’s excellent reason. Mr. Collins would spend far less time in his study if they used an equally lively room. Elizabeth respected Charlotte’s arrangement.

The sitting-room windows showed them nothing in the lane. Mr. Collins therefore reported every passing carriage. He especially announced each time Miss de Bourgh drove past in her small open carriage. He never failed to tell them, although it happened almost daily. She often stopped at the Parsonage and spoke with Charlotte for a few minutes. However, she could hardly ever be persuaded to leave her carriage.

Mr. Collins walked to Rosings on almost every day. His wife also often thought it necessary to go. Elizabeth did not understand the loss of so many hours at first. Then she remembered that Lady Catherine might control other parish positions. Sometimes her Ladyship honoured them with a formal visit. During these visits, she noticed everything happening in the room. She asked about their activities, examined their work, and advised a different method. She criticised the furniture or discovered that the housemaid had been careless. If she accepted food, she seemed to have only one purpose. It let her discover that Mrs. Collins bought pieces of meat too large for her household.

Elizabeth soon noticed something about this great lady. Lady Catherine was not an official local judge, but she actively judged her own parish. Mr. Collins brought her every small concern. Whenever poor villagers quarrelled, complained, or became too poor, she hurried into the village. She settled their disagreements, silenced their complaints, and scolded them into peace and plenty.

They dined at Rosings about twice each week. Sir William was gone, and only one card table appeared after dinner. Otherwise, every visit exactly repeated the first. They had few other invitations because most neighbours lived too richly for the Collinses. Elizabeth did not mind. In general, she spent her time comfortably. She enjoyed half-hours of pleasant conversation with Charlotte. The weather was unusually fine, and Elizabeth often enjoyed the outdoors. Her favourite walk followed the open grove along the park. A sheltered path ran there, and nobody else seemed to value it. She often used it while the others visited Lady Catherine. There she felt safely beyond Lady Catherine’s questions.

The first two weeks of her visit passed quickly in this quiet way. Easter was approaching. The week before it would bring new guests to Rosings, an important event in such a small circle. Soon after arriving, Elizabeth had heard that Mr. Darcy was expected within a few weeks. She preferred almost everyone else she knew. Still, he would provide a comparatively new face at their Rosings parties. His behaviour towards his cousin might also amuse her. She could see how hopeless Miss Bingley’s plans were. Lady Catherine clearly intended him for Miss de Bourgh. She spoke of his visit with great pleasure and the highest praise. She seemed almost angry that Miss Lucas and Elizabeth had already seen him many times.

The Parsonage quickly learned of his arrival. Mr. Collins spent the whole morning where he could watch the gates into Hunsford Lane. He wanted the earliest possible news. When the carriage entered the park, he bowed and hurried home with the great information. The next morning, he rushed to Rosings to pay his respects. Lady Catherine now had two nephews there. Mr. Darcy had brought Colonel Fitzwilliam, the younger son of his uncle, Lord ——. To everyone’s great surprise, both gentlemen returned with Mr. Collins. Charlotte saw them crossing the road from her husband’s room. She immediately ran to the other room and announced the coming honour. She added,—

“I may thank you for this politeness, Eliza. Mr. Darcy would never have visited me so soon otherwise.”

Elizabeth barely had time to deny deserving the compliment before the doorbell announced them. Soon, the three gentlemen entered. Colonel Fitzwilliam came first. He was about 30 and not handsome, but his appearance and manners were truly gentlemanlike. Mr. Darcy looked exactly as he had in Hertfordshire. He greeted Mrs. Collins with his usual reserve. Whatever he felt about her friend, he met Elizabeth with complete calm. She only gave him a small bow and said nothing.

Colonel Fitzwilliam immediately began an easy, pleasant conversation. His cousin made one brief remark about the house and garden to Mrs. Collins. Then he sat silently for some time. At last, politeness led him to ask Elizabeth about her family’s health. She gave the usual answer. After a short pause, she added,—

“My eldest sister has been in London for three months. Have you never happened to meet her there?”

Elizabeth knew perfectly well that he had not. However, she wanted to see whether he knew about Jane and the Bingleys. She thought he looked slightly confused. He answered that he had never been fortunate enough to meet Miss Bennet. They did not continue the subject, and the gentlemen soon left.
