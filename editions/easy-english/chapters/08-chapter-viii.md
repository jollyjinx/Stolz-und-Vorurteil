# Chapter VIII

At five o'clock, the two ladies went to dress. At half past six, Elizabeth was called to dinner. Everyone politely asked about Jane. Mr. Bingley's concern was clearly much stronger than the others'. Elizabeth could give no good news, because Jane was no better. The sisters repeated three or four times how sorry they were. They said a bad cold was shocking and that they hated being ill. Then they forgot the matter. Their lack of concern when Jane was absent restored Elizabeth's original dislike of them.

Their brother was the only person she could view with pleasure. His worry about Jane was clear, and his attention to Elizabeth was very pleasing. He kept her from feeling completely unwelcome, though she believed the others considered her so. Hardly anyone except Bingley noticed her. Miss Bingley was occupied with Mr. Darcy, and Mrs. Hurst almost equally so. Elizabeth sat beside Mr. Hurst. He was a lazy man who lived only to eat, drink, and play cards. When she preferred a plain dish to a rich stew, he had nothing to say to her.

After dinner, Elizabeth returned directly to Jane. As soon as she left, Miss Bingley began attacking her. They declared Elizabeth's manners very bad—a mixture of pride and rudeness. She had no conversation, elegance, taste, or beauty. Mrs. Hurst agreed and added:

“In short, she has nothing to recommend her except being an excellent walker. I shall never forget her appearance this morning. She looked almost wild.”

“She certainly did, Louisa. I could hardly stop myself from laughing. Coming at all was so foolish! Why should she rush across the countryside because her sister had a cold? And her hair was so untidy and wild!”

“Yes, and her petticoat! I hope you saw it. I am certain the bottom six inches were covered in mud. Her lowered gown did not hide it at all.”

“Your description may be exact, Louisa,” said Bingley, “but I noticed none of it. Miss Elizabeth Bennet looked remarkably well when she entered this morning. I did not see her dirty petticoat.”

“You noticed it, Mr. Darcy, I am sure,” said Miss Bingley. “You would not want your sister to display herself that way.”

“Certainly not.”

“She walked three, four, or five miles, or whatever it was. The mud reached above her ankles, and she was completely alone! What could she mean? It shows a horrible, proud independence and a country-town disregard for proper behaviour.”

“It shows a very pleasing love for her sister,” said Bingley.

“I fear, Mr. Darcy,” Miss Bingley said quietly, “that this adventure has weakened your admiration for her fine eyes.”

“Not at all,” he replied. “The exercise made them brighter.” After a short pause, Mrs. Hurst began again.

“I care deeply for Jane Bennet. She really is a sweet girl, and I sincerely hope she marries well. But with such parents and such low family connections, I fear she has no chance.”

“I think you said their uncle is a lawyer in Meryton?”

“Yes, and they have another uncle who lives somewhere near Cheapside.”

“Excellent,” her sister added, and they both laughed warmly.

“Even if enough uncles filled all of Cheapside,” cried Bingley, “the Bennet girls would not become any less pleasant.”

“But it must greatly reduce their chance of marrying men with any social importance,” Darcy replied.

Bingley did not answer. His sisters strongly agreed with Darcy and continued laughing at their dear friend's ordinary relations.

However, after leaving the dining room, they became tender again. They went to Jane's room and stayed until coffee. Jane remained very ill, and Elizabeth would not leave her until late evening. At last, Jane fell asleep. Elizabeth then felt it was right, though not pleasant, to go downstairs. In the drawing room, everyone was playing loo and immediately invited her to join. She suspected they were gambling for large amounts and refused. Using Jane as her reason, she said she would read during her short time below. Mr. Hurst stared at her in amazement.

“Do you prefer reading to cards?” he asked. “That is rather strange.”

“Miss Eliza Bennet,” said Miss Bingley, “looks down on cards. She reads constantly and enjoys nothing else.”

“I deserve neither that praise nor that criticism,” cried Elizabeth. “I am not a great reader, and I enjoy many things.”

“I am sure you enjoy caring for your sister,” said Bingley. “I hope you will soon have the greater pleasure of seeing her completely well.”

Elizabeth thanked him sincerely and walked toward a table holding a few books. He immediately offered to bring every other book in his library.

“I wish my collection were larger, for your benefit and my honour. But I am a lazy man. I do not own many books, though I own more than I have read.”

Elizabeth assured him that the books in the room would suit her perfectly.

“I am amazed,” said Miss Bingley, “that my father left so few books. What a wonderful library you have at Pemberley, Mr. Darcy!”

“It should be good,” he replied. “Many generations have built it.”

“And you have added so much yourself. You are always buying books.”

“I cannot understand anyone neglecting a family library today.”

“Neglect! I am sure you neglect nothing that can improve that noble place. Charles, when you build your house, I hope it is half as wonderful as Pemberley.”

“So do I.”

“I truly advise you to buy nearby and use Pemberley as your model. No county in England is finer than Derbyshire.”

“Gladly. I will buy Pemberley itself if Darcy sells it.”

“I am speaking about possible plans, Charles.”

“Honestly, Caroline, buying Pemberley seems more possible than copying it.”

The conversation interested Elizabeth too much for her to read. Soon she put the book aside and approached the card table. She stood between Mr. Bingley and Mrs. Hurst to watch the game.

“Has Miss Darcy grown much since spring?” asked Miss Bingley. “Will she be as tall as I am?”

“I think she will. She is now about Miss Elizabeth Bennet's height, or slightly taller.”

“I long to see her again! No one has ever delighted me so much. What a face and such manners! She is extremely accomplished for her age. Her piano playing is beautiful.”

“It amazes me,” said Bingley, “that young ladies have enough patience to become so accomplished.”

“All young ladies accomplished! My dear Charles, what do you mean?”

“I think they all are. They all paint tables, decorate screens, and make net purses. I hardly know anyone who cannot do those things. Whenever I first hear about a young lady, someone always says she is accomplished.”

“Your list of ordinary accomplishments contains too much truth,” said Darcy. “People call many women accomplished only because they make purses or decorate screens. But I do not agree with your opinion of ladies in general. Among all my acquaintances, I know no more than six truly accomplished women.”

“Nor do I, certainly,” said Miss Bingley.

“Then,” Elizabeth observed, “your idea of an accomplished woman must include a great deal.”

“Yes, it includes a great deal.”

“Oh, certainly,” cried Miss Bingley, his loyal helper. “A truly accomplished woman must be far above the ordinary. She must thoroughly know music, singing, drawing, dancing, and modern languages. Besides this, her appearance, walk, voice, manner, and expressions must have a special quality. Otherwise, she only half deserves the word.”

“She must have all that,” added Darcy. “She must also improve her mind through wide reading.”

“Now I am not surprised that you know only six accomplished women. I am surprised you know any.”

“Do you judge your own sex so harshly that you think this impossible?”

“I have never seen such a woman. I have never seen that much ability, taste, effort, and elegance united in one person.”

Mrs. Hurst and Miss Bingley protested loudly against Elizabeth's unfair doubt. They insisted they knew many such women. Then Mr. Hurst demanded their attention, bitterly complaining that they ignored the card game. The conversation ended, and Elizabeth soon left the room.

“Eliza Bennet,” said Miss Bingley after the door closed, “is one of those women who attract men by criticising other women. With many men, it probably works. In my opinion, it is a cheap trick and a very low art.”

“Certainly,” Darcy replied, because the remark was mainly for him. “All the tricks women sometimes use to attract men are low. Anything connected with cunning is shameful.”

Miss Bingley did not like this reply enough to continue the subject.

Elizabeth returned only to say that Jane was worse and she could not leave her. Bingley wanted Mr. Jones called immediately. His sisters believed local advice was useless and suggested sending to London for a famous doctor. Elizabeth refused that idea. However, she accepted Bingley's plan more readily. They agreed to call Mr. Jones early if Miss Bennet was not clearly better. Bingley was deeply worried, and his sisters said they were miserable. After supper, the sisters comforted their misery by singing duets. Bingley could only ease his feelings by ordering the housekeeper to give every possible care to Jane and Elizabeth.
