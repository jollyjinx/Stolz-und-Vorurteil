# Chapter XLII

If Elizabeth had judged marriage only from her own family, she would not expect much happiness or comfort. Her father had been attracted by youth, beauty, and the cheerful appearance they often create. He married a woman whose weak mind and narrow opinions soon ended his real love. Respect, admiration, and trust disappeared forever. All his hopes of happiness at home were destroyed. But Mr. Bennet did not seek comfort through the pleasures people often use after their own foolish mistakes. He loved the countryside and books, and these interests became his chief joys. His wife gave him little except amusement through her ignorance and foolishness. Most men would not want to receive this kind of happiness from a wife. But when better entertainment is missing, a true thinker uses what is available.

Elizabeth had never been blind to her father’s improper behaviour as a husband. It had always pained her. But she respected his intelligence and felt grateful for his loving treatment of her. So she tried to forget what she could not excuse. He regularly failed in his duties as a husband. By making his wife look foolish, he taught their children to feel scorn for her. This was deeply wrong. Elizabeth now understood the harm caused to children by such an unsuitable marriage. She had never seen so clearly the damage caused by using intelligence so badly. Properly used, her father’s abilities could at least have protected his daughters’ good name. Perhaps he could never have improved his wife’s mind.

Elizabeth had celebrated Wickham’s departure, but found little other pleasure in losing the regiment. Their social gatherings outside home became less varied. At home, her mother and Kitty constantly complained that everything was dull. Their unhappiness cast a real shadow over the household. In time, Kitty might recover her natural common sense because the officers no longer disturbed her mind. Lydia’s character created more serious danger. Life in both a seaside resort and a camp would probably strengthen her foolishness and boldness. Elizabeth therefore discovered something others had learned before. An event she had eagerly desired did not bring all the satisfaction she expected. She needed another date for the beginning of true happiness. Her wishes and hopes needed a new object. Looking forward to it would comfort her now and prepare her for another disappointment. Her tour to the Lakes became her happiest thought. It best comforted her during the unhappy hours caused by her mother and Kitty. If Jane could have joined the plan, every part would have been perfect.

“But it is fortunate,” she thought, “that I still have something to wish for. A complete plan would certainly disappoint me. But Jane’s absence will give me one constant regret. Therefore, I may reasonably expect every other hope for pleasure to become real. A plan promising pleasure in every part can never succeed. One special disappointment must protect us from disappointment in everything.”

When Lydia left, she promised very frequent, detailed letters to her mother and Kitty. Her letters were always expected for a long time and always very short. Those to her mother offered little information. They had just returned from the library with several named officers. Lydia had seen such beautiful decorations there that excitement almost drove her wild. She had a new dress or new sunshade. She would describe it fully, but Mrs. Forster was calling her. They were going to the camp, so she had to stop immediately. Her letters to Kitty offered even less public information. They were slightly longer but contained too many secretly underlined words to share with others.

After Lydia had been away for two or three weeks, Longbourn became healthy and cheerful again. Everything looked happier. Families who spent winter in London returned. Summer clothes and summer social plans appeared. Mrs. Bennet returned to her usual complaining calm. By mid-June, Kitty could enter Meryton without crying. This promising event made Elizabeth hopeful. Perhaps by Christmas Kitty would become sensible enough to mention an officer only once daily. Of course, the War Office might cruelly send another regiment to Meryton.

The date for their northern tour was quickly approaching. Only two weeks remained when a letter arrived from Mrs. Gardiner. It delayed the journey and made it shorter. Business would prevent Mr. Gardiner from leaving until two weeks later in July. He also had to return to London within one month. This gave them too little time to travel so far and see everything comfortably. They had to give up the Lakes and choose a shorter tour. Their new plan went no farther north than Derbyshire. That county offered enough to fill most of their three weeks. It also held a special attraction for Mrs. Gardiner. She had once lived for several years in one of its towns. They would spend a few days there, and some people she knew still remained. That town probably interested her as much as the famous beauty of Matlock, Chatsworth, Dovedale, or the Peak.

Elizabeth was extremely disappointed because she had strongly wished to see the Lakes. She still thought they might have had enough time. But she needed to accept the change. Happiness was natural to her, and she soon felt satisfied again.

The word Derbyshire brought many ideas with it. Elizabeth could not see it without thinking of Pemberley and its owner. “But surely,” she thought, “I can safely enter his county. I can steal a few pieces of ancient stone without him noticing me.”

She now had to wait twice as long. Four weeks would pass before her uncle and aunt arrived. But the weeks passed, and Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner finally came to Longbourn with their four children. The two girls were six and eight, and the two boys were younger. Their cousin Jane would take special care of them. Every child loved her. Her steady good sense and sweet nature perfectly suited every task. She could teach them, play with them, and love them.

The Gardiners stayed only one night at Longbourn. The next morning, they left with Elizabeth to seek new sights and pleasure. One enjoyment was certain: they suited each other as travelling companions. Their health and tempers could bear difficulties. Their cheerfulness could increase every pleasure. If the outside world disappointed them, their love and intelligence could entertain them.

This book does not aim to describe Derbyshire or the remarkable places along their route. Oxford, Blenheim, Warwick, Kenilworth, Birmingham, and others are well known. Only a small part of Derbyshire matters now. They saw all the area’s main wonders, then travelled towards the little town of Lambton. Mrs. Gardiner had once lived there and recently learned that some old acquaintances remained. When they were within five miles of Lambton, she told Elizabeth that Pemberley was nearby. It was not directly on their route, but only one or two miles away. The previous evening, while planning their journey, Mrs. Gardiner said she wished to see Pemberley again. Mr. Gardiner willingly agreed, and they asked for Elizabeth’s approval.

“My dear, would you not like to see a place you have heard so much about?” her aunt asked. “Many people you know are connected with it too. Wickham spent his entire youth there, you know.”

Elizabeth became uncomfortable. She felt she had no right to visit Pemberley. Therefore, she pretended she did not wish to see it. She said she was tired of large houses. After visiting so many, fine carpets and satin curtains gave her no pleasure.

Mrs. Gardiner criticised her foolishness. “If it were only a richly decorated house, I would not care either,” she said. “But the grounds are delightful. They have some of the finest woods in the country.”

Elizabeth said nothing more, but her mind could not accept the plan. She immediately imagined meeting Mr. Darcy while visiting. It would be terrible! The thought alone made her blush. She considered speaking honestly to her aunt instead of taking such a risk. But there were reasons against this. She finally decided honesty would be her last choice. First, she would privately ask whether the family was away.

That night, Elizabeth asked the maid whether Pemberley was very beautiful. She also asked its owner’s name. With considerable fear, she asked whether the family was there for summer. The maid’s answer to the last question was a very welcome no. Elizabeth’s fear disappeared, allowing her to feel great curiosity about the house. The next morning, they discussed the subject again and asked her opinion. She could easily answer with proper indifference that she did not truly dislike the plan.

Therefore, they would go to Pemberley.
