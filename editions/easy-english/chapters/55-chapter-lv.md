# Chapter LV

A few days after this visit, Mr. Bingley called again, this time alone. His friend had left for London that morning but would return in ten days. Bingley stayed for over an hour and was in excellent spirits. Mrs. Bennet invited him to dinner, but he regretfully admitted that he had another engagement.

“I hope we shall be luckier next time you call,” she said.

He said he would be especially happy to come at any time, and so on. If she allowed it, he would soon take the opportunity to visit them.

“Can you come tomorrow?”

Yes, he had no engagement at all tomorrow, and eagerly accepted her invitation.

He arrived so early that none of the ladies was dressed. Mrs. Bennet rushed into her daughters’ room in her dressing gown, her hair only half finished, and cried—

“My dear Jane, hurry and go downstairs. He has come—Mr. Bingley has come. He really has. Hurry, hurry. Sarah, come to Miss Bennet immediately and help her put on her dress. Never mind Miss Lizzy’s hair.”

“We will come down as soon as possible,” Jane said. “But Kitty is probably more ready than either of us. She went upstairs half an hour ago.”

“Oh, forget Kitty! What has she to do with this? Come, hurry, hurry! Where is your sash, my dear?”

After her mother left, however, Jane refused to go downstairs without one of her sisters.

That evening, the same determination to leave Jane and Bingley alone appeared again. After tea, Mr. Bennet went to the library as usual, while Mary went upstairs to her instrument. Two of the five obstacles had disappeared. Mrs. Bennet then spent a long time looking and winking at Elizabeth and Catherine, but neither reacted. Elizabeth pretended not to notice. When Kitty finally noticed, she innocently asked, “What is wrong, mamma? Why do you keep winking at me? What should I do?”

“Nothing, child, nothing. I did not wink at you.” She sat still for another five minutes. Then she could no longer waste such a valuable opportunity. She suddenly rose and said to Kitty—

“Come here, my love. I want to speak to you.” She took Kitty from the room. Jane immediately looked at Elizabeth with distress. Her expression begged Elizabeth not to support this obvious plan. A few minutes later, Mrs. Bennet half opened the door and called—

“Lizzy, my dear, I want to speak with you.”

Elizabeth had to leave the room.

“We may as well leave them alone, you know,” her mother said in the hall. “Kitty and I are going upstairs to sit in my dressing room.”

Elizabeth did not try to reason with her. She waited quietly until her mother and Kitty disappeared, then returned to the drawing room.

Mrs. Bennet’s plans achieved nothing that day. Bingley was charming in every way, except that he did not openly act as Jane’s lover. His relaxed cheerfulness made him a delightful companion that evening. He patiently accepted her foolish interference and every silly comment. He also controlled his expression, for which Jane was especially grateful.

He hardly needed an invitation to stay for supper. Before leaving, he arranged to return the next morning and shoot with her husband. This plan was mainly created by Bingley and Mrs. Bennet.

After this day, Jane said nothing more about her indifference. The sisters did not discuss Bingley at all. Still, Elizabeth went to bed happily believing everything would soon be settled—unless Mr. Darcy returned at the promised time. More seriously, she felt fairly sure that Darcy had agreed to all of this.

Bingley arrived on time, and he spent the morning with Mr. Bennet as planned. The latter was much more pleasant than Bingley expected. Bingley had no pride or foolishness that might invite his mockery or disgust him into silence. He therefore talked more and behaved less strangely than Bingley had ever known him to do. Naturally, Bingley returned with him for dinner. That evening, Mrs. Bennet again invented plans to remove everyone from Bingley and Jane. Elizabeth needed to write a letter, so she went to the breakfast room after tea. The others were playing cards, so nobody needed her to oppose her mother’s schemes.

When her letter was finished, Elizabeth returned to the drawing room. To her great surprise, she feared that her mother had been too clever for her. She opened the door and saw Jane and Bingley standing together by the fire, apparently in serious conversation. Even if this had caused no suspicion, their faces told the whole story. Both quickly turned and moved away from each other. Their situation was awkward, but Elizabeth thought hers was worse. Neither spoke a word. Elizabeth was about to leave again when Bingley suddenly rose from his chair. He whispered a few words to Jane and ran from the room.

Jane could hide nothing from Elizabeth when sharing it would give pleasure. She immediately embraced her sister and emotionally admitted that she was the happiest person in the world.

“It is too much!” she added. “Far too much. I do not deserve it. Oh, why is everyone not as happy?”

Words could hardly express the sincerity, warmth, and delight of Elizabeth’s congratulations. Every kind sentence brought Jane fresh happiness. But Jane would not remain with her sister or say even half of what still remained to say.

“I must go to my mother immediately,” she cried. “I would never treat her loving concern carelessly or let anyone else tell her. He has already gone to my father. Oh, Lizzy, everything I must tell them will bring my dear family such pleasure! How can I bear so much happiness?”

She hurried to her mother, who had deliberately ended the card game and was sitting upstairs with Kitty.

Left alone, Elizabeth smiled at how quickly and easily the matter had finally been settled. It had caused them many months of uncertainty and pain.

“And this,” she said, “is the result of all his friend’s anxious caution! Of all his sister’s lies and planning! It is the happiest, wisest, and most reasonable result!”

Within minutes, Bingley joined her. His conversation with her father had been short and direct.

“Where is your sister?” he asked quickly as he opened the door.

“Upstairs with my mother. I am sure she will return soon.”

He closed the door and approached Elizabeth, asking for a sister’s affection and good wishes. Elizabeth honestly and warmly expressed her delight that they would become family. They shook hands with great warmth. Until Jane returned, Elizabeth listened to him describe his happiness and Jane’s perfection. He was a man in love, but Elizabeth believed his hopes of happiness were reasonable. They were based on Jane’s excellent intelligence, exceptionally good nature, and the couple’s similar feelings and tastes.

It was an evening of unusual happiness for them all. Miss Bennet’s joy made her face shine, and she looked more beautiful than ever. Kitty smiled foolishly and hoped her own turn would come soon. Mrs. Bennet could not express her approval warmly enough to satisfy herself. Still, she spoke to Bingley of nothing else for half an hour. When Mr. Bennet joined them at supper, his voice and manner clearly showed his true happiness.

However, he said nothing about the engagement until their visitor left for the night. As soon as Bingley was gone, he turned to his daughter and said—

“Jane, I congratulate you. You will be a very happy woman.”

Jane immediately went to him, kissed him, and thanked him for his kindness.

“You are a good girl,” he replied, “and I am delighted that you will marry so happily. I am certain you will do very well together. Your personalities are quite similar. You are both so ready to agree that no decision will ever be made. You are so trusting that every servant will cheat you. And you are so generous that you will always spend more than your income.”

“I hope not. Carelessness or foolishness about money would be unforgivable in me.”

“Spend more than their income! My dear Mr. Bennet,” his wife cried, “what are you saying? He has four or five thousand pounds a year, and probably more.” Then she addressed her daughter. “Oh, my dear, dear Jane, I am so happy! I am sure I shall not sleep at all tonight. I knew this would happen. I always said it must happen at last. I knew you could not be so beautiful for no reason! When he first came to Hertfordshire last year, I immediately thought you would probably marry. Oh, he is the most handsome young man anyone has ever seen!”

Wickham and Lydia were completely forgotten. Jane was now easily her favourite child, and she cared about no other. Her younger sisters soon asked Jane for future pleasures that she might be able to provide.

Mary requested permission to use the library at Netherfield. Kitty urgently begged for several balls there every winter.

From then on, Bingley naturally visited Longbourn every day. He often arrived before breakfast and always remained until after supper. The only exception came when some terrible neighbour invited him to dinner and he felt required to accept. Such a neighbour could not be hated enough.

Elizabeth now had little time to speak with her sister. When Bingley was present, Jane could pay attention to nobody else. Yet Elizabeth was very useful to both during the times when they had to be apart. When Jane was absent, Bingley stayed beside Elizabeth for the pleasure of discussing her. When Bingley left, Jane always sought the same comfort.

“He has made me so happy,” Jane said one evening, “by telling me he never knew I was in London last spring! I had thought that impossible.”

“I suspected it,” Elizabeth replied. “But how did he explain it?”

“His sisters must have arranged it. They certainly did not want him to know me. I cannot be surprised, because in many ways he could have made a much more advantageous choice. But when they see that their brother is happy with me, as I trust they will, they will accept it. Then we shall be friendly again, though we can never be as close as before.”

“That is the least forgiving thing I have ever heard you say,” Elizabeth replied. “Good girl! I would truly hate to see Miss Bingley’s false affection deceive you again.”

“Would you believe, Lizzy, that he truly loved me when he went to London last November? Only the belief that I did not care for him prevented his return.”

“He certainly made a small mistake, but it shows his modesty.”

Naturally, Jane then praised his lack of confidence and his low opinion of his own good qualities.

Elizabeth was pleased that he had not revealed his friend’s interference. Jane had the world’s most generous and forgiving heart. Still, Elizabeth knew that this knowledge would surely make Jane think worse of Darcy.

“I am certainly the luckiest person who ever lived!” Jane cried. “Oh, Lizzy, why was I chosen from our family and blessed above everyone? If only I could see you equally happy! If only another man like him existed for you!”

“Even if you gave me forty such men, I could never be as happy as you. I cannot have your happiness until I have your nature and goodness. No, no, let me manage for myself. Perhaps, if I am extremely lucky, I may eventually meet another Mr. Collins.”

The news at Longbourn could not remain secret for long. Mrs. Bennet had permission to whisper it to Mrs. Philips. Without anyone’s permission, she dared to whisper it to every neighbour in Meryton.

People quickly called the Bennets the luckiest family in the world. Yet only weeks earlier, when Lydia first ran away, everyone had decided they were meant for misfortune.
