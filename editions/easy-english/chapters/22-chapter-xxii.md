# Chapter XXII

The Bennets were invited to dine with the Lucases. Once again, Miss Lucas kindly spent most of the day listening to Mr. Collins. Elizabeth found a chance to thank her. “You keep him cheerful,” she said, “and I am more grateful than I can express.”

Charlotte assured her friend that she was glad to help. It only cost her a little time and was worth the effort. Charlotte’s kindness was certainly generous, but it went much further than Elizabeth guessed. Charlotte wanted to save Elizabeth from any new proposal by Mr. Collins. She did this by drawing his attention towards herself. Such was Miss Lucas’s plan, and events supported it very well. When they said goodnight, she felt almost certain of success. His plan to leave Hertfordshire so soon was her only concern. However, she had underestimated the passion and independence of his character. The next morning, he escaped from Longbourn House with admirable secrecy. He hurried to Lucas Lodge to throw himself at Charlotte’s feet. He did not want his cousins to see him leave. If they did, he believed they would certainly guess his plan. He did not want anyone to know about his attempt before they also knew of its success. He felt almost certain, with good reason, because Charlotte had encouraged him. Still, the events of Wednesday had made him less confident. However, Miss Lucas received him in the most encouraging way. She saw him approaching from an upstairs window. She immediately went out to meet him by accident in the lane. She had hardly dared to hope that so much love and fine speech waited for her there.

Everything was settled as quickly as Mr. Collins’s long speeches allowed. Both of them were satisfied. As they entered the house, he begged her to name their wedding day. It would make him the happiest of men. She had to delay that decision for the present. However, she did not wish to play with his happiness. Nature had kindly given him enough stupidity to remove all charm from his courtship. No woman could wish to make it last. Miss Lucas accepted him only because she sincerely wanted a secure home. She therefore did not care how soon she gained it.

They quickly asked Sir William and Lady Lucas for permission. Both gave it with joyful eagerness. Mr. Collins’s present position made him a very suitable husband for their daughter. Her parents could give Charlotte little money, and his chances of future wealth were excellent. Lady Lucas immediately began a new calculation. She now wondered with great interest how many more years Mr. Bennet might live. Sir William also gave his firm opinion. When Mr. Collins inherited the Longbourn estate, he and his wife should certainly appear at St. James’s. In short, the whole family was properly delighted. The younger girls hoped to enter society one or two years earlier. The boys no longer feared that Charlotte would die an old maid. Charlotte herself remained fairly calm. She had achieved her purpose and could now consider it. Her thoughts were generally satisfying. Mr. Collins was neither sensible nor pleasant. His company was tiring, and his love for her had to be imaginary. Still, he would be her husband. Charlotte had never thought highly of men or marriage. However, marriage had always been her goal. For an educated young woman with little money, it was the only respectable security. It might not bring happiness, but it was the most pleasant protection from poverty. At 27, after never being beautiful, she knew how lucky she was. The least pleasant part was Elizabeth Bennet’s likely surprise. Charlotte valued Elizabeth’s friendship more than anyone else’s. Elizabeth would be astonished and would probably blame her. Charlotte’s decision would not change, but Elizabeth’s disapproval would hurt her. She decided to tell Elizabeth herself. She ordered Mr. Collins not to mention the engagement when he returned to Longbourn for dinner. He dutifully promised to keep it secret. However, this was difficult. His long absence caused direct questions when he returned, and he needed some cleverness to avoid them. He also had to control himself greatly, because he longed to announce his successful love.

He had to begin his journey too early the next morning to see the family. Therefore, they said goodbye when the ladies went upstairs for the night. Mrs. Bennet was very polite and warm. She said they would be happy to see him at Longbourn again whenever his duties allowed another visit.

“My dear madam,” he replied, “this invitation gives me particular pleasure, because I hoped to receive it. You may be certain that I will accept it as soon as possible.”

They were all astonished. Mr. Bennet did not want him to return so soon and immediately said,—

“But might Lady Catherine disapprove, my good sir? You should neglect your relations rather than risk offending your patroness.”

“My dear sir,” Mr. Collins replied, “I am especially grateful for this friendly warning. You may depend upon it. I will not take such an important step without her Ladyship’s agreement.”

“You cannot be too careful. Risk anything rather than her displeasure. A return visit to us might make her angry, and I think that very likely. In that case, stay quietly at home. Be sure that we will not be offended.”

“Believe me, my dear sir, your loving concern fills me with warm gratitude. You will soon receive a letter thanking you for this and every other kindness during my stay in Hertfordshire. As for my fair cousins, my absence may not be long enough to require it. Still, I will now wish them health and happiness, including my cousin Elizabeth.”

After the proper polite words, the ladies went upstairs. They were equally surprised that he planned a quick return. Mrs. Bennet hoped he meant to propose to one of her younger daughters. Mary might have been persuaded to accept him. She rated his abilities much higher than the others did. His thoughts had a seriousness that often impressed her. He was certainly not as clever as she was. However, her example might encourage him to read and improve himself. She thought he could then become a very pleasant companion. The next morning ended every such hope. Miss Lucas called soon after breakfast and privately told Elizabeth what had happened the day before.

During the last day or two, Elizabeth had considered one possibility. Mr. Collins might imagine himself in love with her friend. However, Charlotte encouraging him seemed almost as impossible as Elizabeth encouraging him herself. Her surprise was so great that she forgot good manners and cried out,—

“Engaged to Mr. Collins! My dear Charlotte, impossible!”

Miss Lucas had kept a calm face while telling her story. This direct criticism confused her for a moment. However, she had expected it and soon became calm again. She quietly replied,—

“Why are you surprised, my dear Eliza? Mr. Collins was not fortunate enough to succeed with you. Do you therefore think it impossible that any woman could think well of him?”

Elizabeth now controlled herself with great effort. She firmly assured Charlotte that she welcomed their future family connection. She also wished her every possible happiness.

“I understand your feelings,” Charlotte replied. “You must be surprised—very surprised—because Mr. Collins wanted to marry you so recently. But after you have considered everything, I hope you will approve of my choice. You know I am not romantic and never was. I only want a comfortable home. When I consider Mr. Collins’s character, connections, and position, I believe I have a fair chance of happiness. It is as good as most people can expect when they marry.”

Elizabeth quietly answered, “Certainly.” After an awkward pause, they returned to the rest of the family. Charlotte did not remain much longer. Elizabeth was left to consider what she had heard. For a long time, she could not accept such an unsuitable marriage. Mr. Collins making two proposals in three days was strange. His being accepted now was far stranger. Elizabeth had always known that Charlotte viewed marriage differently. Still, she had never imagined Charlotte would sacrifice every better feeling for worldly advantage. Charlotte, the wife of Mr. Collins, was a deeply humiliating picture! Elizabeth felt the pain of seeing her friend lower herself and lose her respect. She also had one more painful certainty. Charlotte could not possibly be even reasonably happy in the life she had chosen.
