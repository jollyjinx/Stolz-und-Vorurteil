# Chapter XLVII

“I have been thinking again, Elizabeth,” said her uncle as they drove away. “After serious thought, I now agree more with your eldest sister. It seems very unlikely that a young man would plan such harm against Lydia. She has friends and protection and was staying with his Colonel’s family. I strongly hope for the best. Could he expect her friends to do nothing? Could the regiment accept him after such an insult to Colonel Forster? The possible reward is not worth the risk.”

“Do you truly think so?” cried Elizabeth, becoming hopeful for a moment.

“Honestly,” said Mrs. Gardiner, “I am beginning to agree with your uncle. Such an act would be against decency, honour, and his own interests. I cannot think so badly of Wickham. Can you, Lizzy, completely give him up? Do you believe he could do this?”

“Perhaps he would not ignore his own interests. But I believe he could ignore every other duty. If only you were right! But I dare not hope. If they meant to marry, why did they not continue to Scotland?”

“First,” replied Mr. Gardiner, “we have no complete proof that they did not go to Scotland.”

“But changing from their carriage into a hired coach strongly suggests it! And no one found any sign of them on the Barnet road.”

“Then suppose they are in London. They may be hiding there for an innocent reason. Neither of them is likely to have much money. They may have realised that a London wedding would cost less than Scotland. It would take longer, but it would be cheaper.”

“But why keep everything secret? Why fear discovery? Why must the wedding be private? Oh no, this is not likely. Jane says his closest friend believed he never planned to marry Lydia. Wickham will never marry a woman without money. He cannot afford it. What does Lydia have besides youth, health, and good humour? Why would he give up every chance of improving his position through a rich marriage? I do not know how the regiment would respond to such a dishonourable elopement. Perhaps fear of disgrace there would control him. But I fear your other argument is weak. Lydia has no brothers to defend her. Wickham may have judged my father by his usual behaviour. My father is inactive and pays little attention to family events. Wickham may expect him to do and think as little as any father could.”

“But can you believe Lydia cares only about her love for him? Would she agree to live with him without marriage?”

“It is terrible that anyone can doubt my sister’s decency and morals,” replied Elizabeth, with tears in her eyes. “I do not know what to say. Perhaps I am unfair to her. But she is very young and has never learned to think seriously. For the last six months—indeed, for a whole year—she has cared only about amusement and vanity. She has spent her time in the most idle and foolish way. She has accepted any opinion that happened to reach her. Since the ——shire regiment came to Meryton, she has thought only of love, flirting, and officers. She has constantly thought and talked about these things. This has made her feelings even more—what shall I call it?—ready for love. They were already lively enough. And we all know Wickham’s appearance and manner can charm a woman.”

“But Jane does not think so badly of Wickham,” said her aunt. “She does not believe he could try such a thing.”

“When does Jane ever think badly of anyone? No matter what someone has done, Jane needs proof before believing such evil. But she knows Wickham’s true character as well as I do. We both know that he has behaved badly in every way. He has no honesty or honour. He is as false and dishonest as he is charming.”

“Do you truly know all this?” cried Mrs. Gardiner. She was now very curious about how Elizabeth had learned it.

“I do,” replied Elizabeth, blushing. “The other day I told you about his shameful behaviour to Mr. Darcy. When you were last at Longbourn, you heard how Wickham spoke about him. Yet Mr. Darcy had been patient and generous towards Wickham. There are other facts I am not free to tell—or which are not worth telling. But Wickham has told endless lies about the Pemberley family. His description of Miss Darcy prepared me to meet a proud, cold, unpleasant girl. Yet he knew that was false. He must know she is as kind and modest as we found her.”

“But does Lydia know none of this? Can she know nothing about what you and Jane understand so well?”

“No, and that is the worst part. I did not learn the truth until I went to Kent. There I saw much of Mr. Darcy and Colonel Fitzwilliam. When I returned, the ——shire regiment was leaving Meryton within one or two weeks. I told Jane everything, but neither of us thought we should make it public. What good could it do to destroy the whole neighbourhood’s good opinion of Wickham then? Even when Lydia’s visit with Mrs. Forster was arranged, I never thought of warning her. I never imagined Wickham’s lies could put her in danger. You can easily believe I never expected such a result as this.”

“So when they all moved to Brighton, you had no reason to think they loved each other?”

“None at all. I remember no sign of love from either of them. If there had been a sign, our family would certainly have noticed and discussed it. Lydia admired him when he first joined the regiment, but we all did. Every girl near Meryton was foolish about him for the first two months. But he gave Lydia no special attention. After a reasonable period of wild admiration, she stopped preferring him. Other officers noticed her more and became her favourites.”

Their repeated discussion could add little to their fears, hopes, and guesses. Yet no other subject held them for long during the whole journey. Elizabeth never stopped thinking about it. The sharpest pain was blaming herself. She found no moment of peace or forgetfulness.

They travelled as quickly as possible and slept one night on the road. They reached Longbourn by dinnertime the next day. Elizabeth was glad that Jane had not suffered a long wait for them.

The young Gardiner children saw the carriage and stood on the house steps. When it entered the field, they watched eagerly. As it reached the door, joyful surprise filled their faces and bodies. They jumped and danced about, giving the first happy sign of welcome.

Elizabeth jumped out and quickly kissed each child. Then she hurried into the entrance hall. Jane ran downstairs from their mother’s room and met her at once.

Elizabeth embraced Jane warmly while tears filled both their eyes. Without delay, she asked whether anyone had heard from Lydia and Wickham.

“Not yet,” replied Jane. “But now our dear uncle is here, I hope everything will be well.”

“Is my father in London?”

“Yes. He went on Tuesday, as I wrote to you.”

“Have you heard from him often?”

“Only once. He wrote a few lines on Wednesday to say he had arrived safely. He also sent his address because I had especially asked for it. He only added that he would not write again without important news.”

“And my mother? How is she? How are all of you?”

“I believe my mother is fairly well, though the shock has deeply upset her. She is upstairs and will be very glad to see you all. She still does not leave her dressing room. Thank Heaven, Mary and Kitty are completely well.”

“But how are you?” cried Elizabeth. “You look pale. You must have suffered so much!”

Jane assured her that she was perfectly well. They had talked while Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner greeted their children. Now the whole group approached, ending the private conversation. Jane ran to her aunt and uncle. With smiles and tears, she welcomed and thanked them both.

When everyone entered the drawing room, the others repeated Elizabeth’s questions. They soon learned that Jane had no news. Yet her kind heart still encouraged hopeful thoughts. She expected a happy ending. Each morning, she hoped for a letter from Lydia or her father. It might explain their actions and perhaps announce a marriage.

After a few minutes, they all went to Mrs. Bennet’s room. She received them exactly as expected. She cried, complained, and attacked Wickham’s wicked behaviour. She described her own suffering and bad treatment. She blamed everyone except herself, though her foolish indulgence had chiefly encouraged Lydia’s mistakes.

“If I had taken the whole family to Brighton, this would never have happened,” she said. “Poor dear Lydia had no one to care for her. Why did the Forsters ever let her leave their sight? They must have neglected her terribly. She would never do such a thing if someone watched her properly. I always thought they were unfit to care for her. But people always decide against me. Poor dear child! And now Mr. Bennet has gone away. I know he will fight Wickham when he finds him. Then Wickham will kill him, and what will happen to us? The Collinses will turn us out before he is cold in his grave. Brother, if you are not kind to us, I do not know what we shall do.”

They all protested against these terrible ideas. Mr. Gardiner assured her that he loved her and her whole family. He would be in London the next day and help Mr. Bennet in every attempt to recover Lydia.

“Do not surrender to useless fear,” he added. “We should prepare for the worst, but should not treat it as certain. They left Brighton less than a week ago. In a few more days, we may receive news. Until we know they are unmarried and do not plan to marry, we must not give up. As soon as I reach London, I will find my brother. I will bring him home with me to Gracechurch Street. Then we can decide together what to do.”

“Oh, my dear brother,” replied Mrs. Bennet, “that is exactly what I want. When you reach London, find them wherever they are. If they are not married, make them marry. Do not let them wait for wedding clothes. Tell Lydia she can buy as many as she likes after the wedding. Above all, stop Mr. Bennet from fighting. Tell him about my dreadful state. I am frightened out of my mind. I shake and tremble. I have pains in my side and head and terrible beating in my heart. I cannot rest by night or day. Tell my dear Lydia not to order clothes before seeing me. She does not know the best shops. Oh, brother, you are so kind! I know you will arrange everything.”

Mr. Gardiner again promised to make every effort. Still, he advised moderation in both her hopes and fears. He spoke with her until dinner was ready. Then they left her to express all her feelings to the housekeeper. The housekeeper was attending her while her daughters were away.

Her brother and sister saw no true reason for her separation from the family. Yet they did not oppose it. They knew she could not speak carefully in front of all the servants at dinner. It seemed better for only one household servant to know all her fears. The housekeeper was also the servant they trusted most.

Mary and Kitty soon joined them in the dining room. Both had been too busy in their own rooms to appear earlier. Mary came from her books and Kitty from dressing. Both looked fairly calm. Kitty alone showed a change. Losing her favourite sister, or receiving blame for the affair, had made her voice more cross than usual. Mary controlled herself well. Soon after they sat down, she whispered seriously to Elizabeth:

“This is a most unfortunate affair, and people will probably discuss it greatly. But we must resist their unkindness. We must comfort each other’s wounded hearts with sisterly love.”

Elizabeth showed no wish to answer. Mary therefore added, “This event must be unhappy for Lydia, but it teaches us something useful. When a woman loses her virtue, she can never recover it. One false step causes endless ruin. Her reputation is beautiful but easily broken. She must always guard herself from unworthy men.”

Elizabeth looked up in amazement but felt too troubled to answer. Mary continued comforting herself with moral lessons drawn from the disaster.

That afternoon, the two eldest Miss Bennets spent half an hour alone. Elizabeth immediately used the chance to ask questions, and Jane eagerly answered them. They first mourned the dreadful likely result. Elizabeth considered it almost certain, while Miss Bennet could not call it impossible. Then Elizabeth said, “Tell me everything I have not heard. Give me more details. What did Colonel Forster say? Did they suspect anything before Lydia ran away? They must have seen Lydia and Wickham together constantly.”

“Colonel Forster admitted he had often suspected some affection, especially from Lydia. But he saw nothing that alarmed him. I am so sorry for him. His behaviour was extremely thoughtful and kind. He was already coming to express his concern. At that time, he still believed they had gone to Scotland. When he first heard the other fear, he travelled here more quickly.”

“Was Denny certain that Wickham would not marry? Did he know about their plan to leave? Did Colonel Forster question Denny himself?”

“Yes. But when Colonel Forster questioned him, Denny denied knowing their plan. He would not give his true opinion. He did not repeat his belief that they would not marry. Therefore, I hope people misunderstood his earlier words.”

“Until Colonel Forster arrived, none of you doubted that they were truly married, I suppose?”

“How could such a doubt have entered our minds? I felt a little worried about Lydia’s happiness in marriage. I knew Wickham’s behaviour had not always been right. My father and mother knew nothing about that. They only saw that the match was very unwise. Then Kitty admitted that Lydia had prepared her for this in her last letter. Kitty was naturally proud to know more than the rest of us. Apparently, she had known of their love for several weeks.”

“But not before they went to Brighton?”

“No, I believe not.”

“Did Colonel Forster himself seem to think badly of Wickham? Does he know Wickham’s true character?”

“I must admit he did not speak as well of Wickham as before. He thought Wickham careless and wasteful. Since this sad affair, people say he left Meryton with large debts. But I hope that is false.”

“Oh, Jane, if we had kept fewer secrets and told what we knew, this could not have happened!”

“Perhaps telling would have been better,” replied her sister.

“But it seemed unfair to reveal someone’s old faults without knowing his present feelings.”

“We acted with the best intentions.”

“Could Colonel Forster remember the details of Lydia’s note to his wife?”

“He brought it for us to see.”

Jane took the note from her pocketbook and gave it to Elizabeth. It said:

“My dear Harriet,

“You will laugh when you learn where I have gone. I cannot stop laughing when I imagine your surprise tomorrow morning. You will miss me then. I am going to Gretna Green. If you cannot guess who is with me, I will think you very foolish. There is only one man in the world whom I love, and he is an angel. I could never be happy without him, so you must see no harm in my leaving. You need not tell them at Longbourn if you do not want to. The surprise will be greater when I write and sign my name Lydia Wickham. What a good joke it will be! I can hardly write because I am laughing. Please apologise to Pratt because I cannot keep my promise to dance tonight. Tell him I hope he will forgive me when he knows everything. I will gladly dance with him at the next ball we both attend. I will send for my clothes when I reach Longbourn. But please tell Sally to mend the large tear in my decorated muslin dress before packing it. Goodbye. Give my love to Colonel Forster. I hope you will drink to our good journey.

“Your affectionate friend, “Lydia Bennet.”

“Oh, careless, careless Lydia!” cried Elizabeth after reading it. “What a letter to write at such a time! But at least it proves she was serious about her journey’s purpose. Whatever Wickham later persuaded her to do, she did not plan anything shameful. My poor father! How he must have felt when he read it!”

“I have never seen anyone so shocked. He could not speak for ten full minutes. My mother became ill immediately, and the whole house was in confusion.”

“Oh, Jane,” cried Elizabeth, “did any servant in the house remain ignorant by the end of that day?”

“I do not know. I hope someone did. But careful silence is difficult at such a time. My mother became hysterical. I tried to give her every help I could. I fear I did less than I should have done. The horror of what might happen almost stopped me from thinking.”

“Caring for her has been too much for you. You do not look well. I wish I had been here! You have carried every care and worry alone.”

“Mary and Kitty have been very kind and would have shared every difficulty. But I did not think it right for either of them. Kitty is small and delicate. Mary studies so much that we should not interrupt her hours of rest. Aunt Philips came to Longbourn on Tuesday after my father left. She kindly stayed until Thursday and gave us great help and comfort. Lady Lucas has also been very kind. She walked here on Wednesday morning to express sympathy. She offered her own help or any of her daughters’ help.”

“She should have stayed at home,” cried Elizabeth. “Perhaps she meant well. But during such trouble, one should see as little of one’s neighbours as possible. They cannot help, and their sympathy is unbearable. Let them enjoy our trouble from a distance and be satisfied.”

Elizabeth then asked what steps her father planned to take in London to recover Lydia.

“I believe he planned to go to Epsom, where they last changed horses,” replied Jane. “He would question the carriage riders and try to learn something. His main aim was to discover the number of the hired coach from Clapham. The coach had brought a passenger from London. My father thought someone might remember a gentleman and lady changing carriages. He planned to ask questions at Clapham. If he could learn where the coachman left his earlier passenger, he would ask there. He hoped to discover the coach station and the coach’s number. I know of no other plans. He left in such a hurry and was so upset that I could hardly learn even this much.”
