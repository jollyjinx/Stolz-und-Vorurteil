# Chapter LIX

“My dear Lizzy, where can you have been walking?” Jane asked as soon as Elizabeth entered the room. Everyone else asked the same question at dinner. Elizabeth answered only that they had wandered until she no longer knew where they were. She became red while speaking. However, neither that nor anything else made anyone suspect the truth.

The evening passed quietly, without any extraordinary event. The openly acknowledged lovers talked and laughed, while the secret lovers remained silent. Darcy’s happiness did not naturally express itself through cheerful laughter. Elizabeth felt nervous and confused. She knew she was happy more than she actually felt it. Besides her present embarrassment, future troubles faced her. She imagined the family’s feelings when they learned her situation. Nobody except Jane liked him. She even feared the others disliked him so much that his wealth and importance could not change them.

That night, she opened her heart to Jane. Miss Bennet rarely suspected anyone of anything, but she completely refused to believe this.

“You are joking, Lizzy. This cannot be true! Engaged to Mr. Darcy! No, no, you will not deceive me. I know it is impossible.”

“This is truly a terrible beginning! I depended entirely on you. If you do not believe me, nobody else will. But I am completely serious. Every word is true. He still loves me, and we are engaged.”

Jane looked doubtful. “Oh, Lizzy! It cannot be. I know how strongly you dislike him.”

“You understand nothing about it. That must all be forgotten. Perhaps I did not always love him as much as I do now. But in such cases, a good memory is unforgivable. This is the last time I shall remember it myself.”

Miss Bennet still looked completely amazed. Elizabeth again assured her, more seriously, that it was true.

“Good heaven! Can it truly be so? But now I must believe you,” Jane cried. “My dear, dear Lizzy, I want to congratulate you, and I do. But are you certain—forgive my question—quite certain that you can be happy with him?”

“There can be no doubt. We have already agreed that we shall be the world’s happiest couple. But are you pleased, Jane? Will you like having such a brother?”

“Very, very much. Nothing could delight Bingley or me more. But we considered it and discussed it as impossible. Do you truly love him enough? Oh, Lizzy! Do anything except marry without love. Are you completely certain that your feelings are right?”

“Oh, yes! When I tell you everything, you will only think I feel more than I should.”

“What do you mean?”

“Well, I must admit that I love him more than I love Bingley. I am afraid you will be angry.”

“My dearest sister, now please be serious. I want a very serious conversation. Tell me immediately everything I should know. How long have you loved him?”

“It happened so slowly that I hardly know when it began. But I believe it started when I first saw his beautiful grounds at Pemberley.”

Another request for seriousness had the desired effect. Elizabeth soon satisfied Jane with sincere promises of her love. Once convinced about that, Miss Bennet wanted nothing more.

“Now I am completely happy,” she said, “because you will be as happy as I am. I always respected him. Even if he had nothing except his love for you, I would still value him. But now he is Bingley’s friend and your husband. Only Bingley and you can be dearer to me. But Lizzy, you have been very secretive and quiet with me. You told me so little about Pemberley and Lambton! Someone else, not you, gave me all my knowledge about it.”

Elizabeth explained why she had kept silent. She had not wanted to mention Bingley. Her own uncertain feelings had equally prevented her from mentioning his friend. But now, she no longer hid Darcy’s part in Lydia’s marriage. Elizabeth admitted everything, and they spent half the night talking.

“Good gracious!” Mrs. Bennet cried at the window the next morning. “That unpleasant Mr. Darcy is coming again with our dear Bingley! Why must he be so tiresome and constantly visit us? I thought he would go shooting or do something else, instead of troubling us with his company. What shall we do with him? Lizzy, you must walk with him again so he does not trouble Bingley.”

Elizabeth could hardly keep from laughing at this very convenient suggestion. Yet she was truly upset that her mother always described him so unpleasantly.

As soon as they entered, Bingley looked meaningfully at Elizabeth and warmly shook her hand. Clearly, Darcy had informed him. Soon afterwards, Bingley said aloud, “Mrs. Bennet, are there no more paths nearby where Lizzy may lose herself again today?”

“I advise Mr. Darcy, Lizzy, and Kitty to walk to Oakham Mount this morning,” Mrs. Bennet said. “It is a pleasantly long walk, and Mr. Darcy has never seen the view.”

“It may suit the others very well,” Mr. Bingley replied, “but I am sure it is too far for Kitty. Is it not, Kitty?”

Kitty admitted that she preferred to remain home. Darcy expressed great interest in the view from the Mount, and Elizabeth silently agreed. As she went upstairs to prepare, Mrs. Bennet followed her and said—

“I am quite sorry, Lizzy, that you must keep that unpleasant man entirely to yourself. But I hope you will not mind. It is all for Jane, you know. You need only speak to him occasionally, so do not trouble yourself.”

During their walk, they decided to ask Mr. Bennet’s consent that evening. Elizabeth kept the task of speaking to her mother. She could not guess her mother’s reaction. Sometimes she doubted that all his wealth and importance could overcome her dislike. She might strongly oppose the marriage or become wildly delighted. Either way, her behaviour would equally fail to show good sense. Elizabeth could not bear Mr. Darcy hearing either her first cries of joy or her first violent disapproval.

That evening, Mr. Bennet went to the library. Soon afterwards, Elizabeth saw Mr. Darcy rise and follow him, which made her extremely anxious. She did not fear her father’s opposition. But this news would make him unhappy, and she would cause his pain. She was his favourite child, yet her choice would fill him with fear and regret as he gave her away. This thought made her miserable. She remained so until Mr. Darcy returned, when his smile gave her some relief. He soon approached the table where she sat with Kitty. Pretending to admire her work, he whispered, “Go to your father. He wants you in the library.” She went immediately.

Her father walked around the room, looking serious and anxious. “Lizzy,” he said, “what are you doing? Have you lost your mind by accepting this man? Have you not always hated him?”

How strongly she now wished her old opinions had been more reasonable and her words less extreme! Then she might have avoided explanations and declarations that felt terribly awkward. But they were now necessary. With some embarrassment, she assured him that she loved Mr. Darcy.

“In other words, you are determined to marry him. He is certainly rich. You may have finer clothes and carriages than Jane. But will those things make you happy?”

“Do you have any objection,” Elizabeth asked, “except your belief that I do not care for him?”

“None at all. We all know he is a proud, unpleasant sort of man. But that would not matter if you truly liked him.”

“I do, I do like him,” she replied with tears in her eyes. “I love him. He does not have improper pride. He is perfectly kind and lovable. You do not know his true character. Please do not hurt me by speaking about him that way.”

“Lizzy,” her father said, “I have given him my consent. Indeed, he is the sort of man whose request I would never dare refuse. Now I give my consent to you, if you are determined to marry him. But I advise you to consider more carefully. I know your nature, Lizzy. You could never be happy or respected unless you truly valued your husband and looked up to him as a better person. Your lively intelligence would put you in great danger in an unsuitable marriage. You could hardly escape shame and misery. My child, do not make me watch you lose all respect for your life partner. You do not understand what you are doing.”

Elizabeth became even more emotional, and her answer was serious and sincere. Again and again, she promised that Mr. Darcy was truly her chosen partner. She explained how her opinion of him had gradually changed. His love was not a brief feeling but had survived many uncertain months. She strongly described all his good qualities. At last, she overcame her father’s disbelief and made him accept the marriage.

“Well, my dear,” he said when she finished, “I have nothing more to say. If that is true, he deserves you. I could not give my Lizzy to anyone less worthy.”

To improve his opinion further, she described what Mr. Darcy had freely done for Lydia. Her father listened with astonishment.

“This truly is an evening of wonders! So Darcy did everything. He arranged the marriage, provided money, paid that man’s debts, and obtained his military position! Even better. It will save me endless trouble and careful saving. If your uncle had done it, I would have been required and determined to repay him. But these passionate young lovers always achieve everything their own way. I shall offer to repay him tomorrow. He will loudly protest about his love for you, and that will end the matter.”

Then he remembered her embarrassment several days earlier while he read Mr. Collins’s letter. After laughing at her for some time, he finally let her leave. As she went, he said, “If any young men come for Mary or Kitty, send them inside. I am completely free.”

A great weight had now lifted from Elizabeth’s mind. After half an hour of quiet thought in her room, she joined the others fairly calmly. Everything was too recent for cheerful excitement, but the evening passed peacefully. She no longer had anything important to fear. Comfort and familiarity would come with time.

That night, Elizabeth followed her mother into the dressing room and shared the important news. Its effect was extraordinary. At first, Mrs. Bennet sat perfectly still, unable to say one word. Many, many minutes passed before she could understand what she had heard. Usually, she quickly believed anything that benefited her family or involved a daughter’s lover. At last, she recovered. She moved anxiously in her chair, stood, sat again, wondered, and cried out in amazement.

“Good gracious! Lord bless me! Just imagine! Dear me! Mr. Darcy! Who would have thought it? Is it really true? Oh, my sweetest Lizzy! How rich and important you will be! Think of your personal money, jewels, and carriages! Jane’s marriage is nothing compared with it—nothing at all. I am so pleased—so happy. Such a charming man! So handsome! So tall! Oh, my dear Lizzy! Please apologize because I disliked him so much before. I hope he will forgive it. Dear, dear Lizzy. A house in London! Every possible pleasure! Three daughters married! Ten thousand pounds a year! Oh, Lord! What will happen to me? I shall lose my mind.”

This speech proved that her approval was certain. Elizabeth was glad nobody else had heard it and soon left. But she had spent less than three minutes in her room when her mother followed.

“My dearest child,” she cried, “I can think of nothing else. Ten thousand pounds a year, and probably more! It is as good as being a lord! And a special licence—you must and shall marry by a special licence. But my dearest love, tell me Mr. Darcy’s favourite dish so I may serve it tomorrow.”

This was a worrying sign of her mother’s future behaviour towards Darcy. Elizabeth possessed his warmest love and had her family’s consent, yet something still remained imperfect. But the next day went much better than she expected. Luckily, Mrs. Bennet felt such respectful fear of her future son-in-law that she hardly dared speak. She only addressed him to offer some kindness or show her respect for his opinion.

Elizabeth happily watched her father make an effort to know Darcy. Mr. Bennet soon assured her that he respected him more every hour.

“I greatly admire all three of my sons-in-law,” he said. “Perhaps Wickham is my favourite. But I think I shall like your husband just as much as Jane’s.”
