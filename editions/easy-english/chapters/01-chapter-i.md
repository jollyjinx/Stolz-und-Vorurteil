# Chapter I

It is a truth accepted everywhere: a single man with a large fortune must need a wife.

No one may know what such a man feels or plans when he enters a neighbourhood. Still, every nearby family firmly believes this truth. They see him as the rightful future husband of one of their daughters.

“My dear Mr. Bennet,” his wife said one day, “have you heard that Netherfield Park is finally rented?”

Mr. Bennet said that he had not.

“But it is,” she replied. “Mrs. Long has just been here, and she told me everything.”

Mr. Bennet did not answer.

“Do you not want to know who has rented it?” his wife cried impatiently.

“You want to tell me, and I am willing to hear it.”

That was enough encouragement.

“Well, my dear, Mrs. Long says a rich young man from northern England has rented Netherfield. He came on Monday in a carriage pulled by four horses to see the place. He liked it so much that he immediately made an agreement with Mr. Morris. He will move in before Michaelmas. Some of his servants will be in the house by the end of next week.”

“What is his name?”

“Bingley.”

“Is he married or single?”

“Oh, single, of course, my dear! A single man with a large fortune—four or five thousand pounds a year. What wonderful news for our girls!”

“Why? How can it affect them?”

“My dear Mr. Bennet,” his wife replied, “how can you be so annoying? You must know that I am thinking he may marry one of them.”

“Is that why he is moving here?”

“Why? What nonsense! How can you talk like that? But he may easily fall in love with one of them. Therefore, you must make a formal visit as soon as he arrives.”

“I see no need. You and the girls may go. Or you may send them alone, which might be even better. You are as handsome as any of them, so Mr. Bingley might like you best.”

“My dear, you flatter me. I was once beautiful, but I do not claim to be remarkable now. When a woman has five grown daughters, she should stop thinking about her own beauty.”

“In that situation, a woman often has little beauty left to think about.”

“But, my dear, you really must visit Mr. Bingley when he comes here.”

“I cannot promise that, I assure you.”

“But think of your daughters. Imagine what a secure future one of them could have. Sir William and Lady Lucas plan to visit him for that reason alone. You know they do not usually visit new neighbours. You must go. We cannot visit him if you do not.”

“You worry too much about the rules. Mr. Bingley will surely be glad to see you. I will send him a note with you. I shall warmly agree to his marrying whichever girl he chooses—though I must say something good about my little Lizzy.”

“I demand that you do no such thing. Lizzy is no better than the others. She is not half as handsome as Jane or half as cheerful as Lydia. But you always prefer her.”

“None of them has much to recommend her,” he replied. “They are all silly and ignorant, like other girls. But Lizzy thinks more quickly than her sisters.”

“Mr. Bennet, how can you insult your own children like that? You enjoy upsetting me. You have no pity for my poor nerves.”

“You misunderstand me, my dear. I have great respect for your nerves. They are my old friends. I have heard you speak seriously about them for at least twenty years.”

“Ah, you do not know how much I suffer.”

“But I hope you recover and live to see many young men with four thousand pounds a year move here.”

“Even twenty such men would be useless to us if you refused to visit them.”

“You may depend on this, my dear: when there are twenty, I will visit them all.”

Mr. Bennet had a strange mixture of intelligence, sharp humour, silence, and sudden changes of mood. Even after twenty-three years, his wife did not understand him. Her own mind was easier to understand. She had little sense, little knowledge, and an uncertain temper. When unhappy, she imagined that her nerves were ill. Her life's work was to marry off her daughters. Visits and news were her comfort.
