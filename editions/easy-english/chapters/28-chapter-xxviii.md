# Chapter XXVIII

Everything on the next day’s journey was new and interesting to Elizabeth. She was in excellent spirits. Jane had looked so well that Elizabeth no longer feared for her health. The thought of her northern tour was also a constant delight.

They left the main road and entered the lane to Hunsford. Everyone searched for the Parsonage around each turn. The fence of Rosings Park ran along one side. Elizabeth smiled as she remembered everything she had heard about its residents.

At last, they saw the Parsonage. Its garden sloped towards the road, with the house standing above it. Green fences and a laurel hedge showed they had arrived. Mr. Collins and Charlotte appeared at the door. The carriage stopped at the small gate, and everyone smiled and nodded. A short gravel path led to the house. They quickly left the carriage, delighted to see one another. Mrs. Collins welcomed her friend with lively pleasure. Her warm welcome made Elizabeth increasingly glad she had come. She immediately saw that marriage had not changed her cousin’s manners. His formal politeness was exactly the same. He kept Elizabeth at the gate for several minutes. He asked about every member of her family and listened to the answers. Then he pointed out the neat entrance before taking them inside. In the sitting room, he formally welcomed them a second time to his humble home. He also carefully repeated all his wife’s offers of food and drink.

Elizabeth was prepared to see Mr. Collins in all his glory. He proudly described the room’s size, position, and furniture. She imagined that he spoke especially to her. He seemed to want her to understand what she had lost by refusing him. Everything was neat and comfortable, but Elizabeth gave him no sigh of regret. Instead, she looked at Charlotte with wonder. How could her friend look so cheerful with such a companion? Mr. Collins often said things that could reasonably shame his wife. Elizabeth then looked at Charlotte without meaning to. Once or twice, she noticed a faint blush. Usually, Charlotte wisely heard nothing. They sat long enough to admire every piece of furniture, from the sideboard to the fireplace guard. They also described their journey and everything that had happened in London. Mr. Collins then invited them into his large, well-planned garden, which he cared for himself. Gardening was one of his most respectable pleasures. Elizabeth admired Charlotte’s calm face as she praised the healthy exercise. Charlotte admitted that she encouraged it as much as possible. Mr. Collins led them along every path and side path. He barely gave them time to offer the praise he expected. He described every view in such detail that no beauty remained. He could count the fields in every direction and the trees in the most distant group. Yet no view in his garden, country, or kingdom could equal Rosings. An opening in the trees opposite his house showed it clearly. It was a handsome modern building on rising ground.

Mr. Collins wanted to lead them around his two fields. The ladies’ shoes were not suitable for the remains of the morning frost, so they returned. Sir William went on with Mr. Collins. Charlotte happily showed her sister and friend around the house without her husband’s help. It was rather small but well built and convenient. Everything was neat and arranged with good sense, and Elizabeth gave Charlotte all the credit. Whenever Mr. Collins could be forgotten, the house truly felt comfortable. Charlotte’s clear enjoyment suggested that she often forgot him.

Elizabeth had already learned that Lady Catherine was still at Rosings. They discussed it again at dinner, and Mr. Collins observed,—

“Yes, Miss Elizabeth, you will have the honour of seeing Lady Catherine de Bourgh at church next Sunday. I need not say that she will delight you. She is all friendliness and kindness to those below her. I am sure she will honour you with some attention after the service. I can almost promise that she will include you and my sister Maria in every invitation during your stay. Her behaviour towards my dear Charlotte is charming. We dine at Rosings twice every week and are never allowed to walk home. Her Ladyship always orders a carriage for us. I should say one of her carriages, because she owns several.”

“Lady Catherine is certainly a very respectable, sensible woman,” Charlotte added, “and a most attentive neighbour.”

“Very true, my dear. That is exactly what I say. One cannot treat such a woman with too much respect.”

They spent most of the evening discussing Hertfordshire news and repeating what letters had already said. Afterwards, Elizabeth considered Charlotte’s happiness alone in her room. She saw how cleverly Charlotte guided her husband and how calmly she endured him. Elizabeth had to admit that Charlotte managed everything very well. She also imagined the rest of her visit. There would be quiet daily activities, annoying interruptions from Mr. Collins, and entertainment at Rosings. Her lively imagination soon arranged the whole picture.

Around the middle of the next day, Elizabeth was in her room preparing for a walk. A sudden noise downstairs suggested that the whole house was in confusion. After listening, she heard someone running upstairs and loudly calling her name. Elizabeth opened the door and met Maria on the landing. Maria was breathless with excitement and cried,—

“Oh, my dear Eliza! Hurry into the dining room. There is such a sight to see! I will not tell you what it is. Hurry and come downstairs this moment.”

Elizabeth asked questions, but Maria would say nothing more. They ran into the dining room, which faced the lane. The great wonder was two ladies in a low open carriage at the garden gate.

“Is that all?” cried Elizabeth. “I expected at least pigs in the garden. It is only Lady Catherine and her daughter!”

“Goodness, my dear,” said Maria, shocked by the mistake, “that is not Lady Catherine. The older lady is Mrs. Jenkinson, who lives with them. The other is Miss de Bourgh. Only look at her! She is such a little creature. Who could imagine anyone so thin and small?”

“It is terribly rude to keep Charlotte outside in all this wind. Why does she not come in?”

“Oh, Charlotte says she almost never does. It is a very great favour when Miss de Bourgh enters the house.”

“I like her appearance,” said Elizabeth, thinking of something else. “She looks sickly and bad-tempered. Yes, she will suit him very well. She will make him a proper wife.”

Mr. Collins and Charlotte stood at the gate talking with the ladies. Sir William greatly amused Elizabeth. He stood in the doorway, seriously studying the greatness before him. Whenever Miss de Bourgh looked towards him, he bowed.

At last, there was nothing more to say. The ladies drove away, and the others returned inside. As soon as Mr. Collins saw the two girls, he congratulated them on their good fortune. Charlotte explained his meaning. The whole party had been invited to dine at Rosings the next day.
