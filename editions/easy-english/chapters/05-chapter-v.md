# Chapter V

A family who were close friends of the Bennets lived a short walk from Longbourn. Sir William Lucas had once worked in trade in Meryton and made a fair fortune. While serving as mayor, he gave a formal address to the king and received a knighthood. Perhaps he felt this honour too strongly. It made him dislike his business and his home in a small market town. He left both and moved his family to a house about a mile from Meryton. From then on, it was called Lucas Lodge. There he could enjoy thinking about his own importance. Free from business, he spent his time being polite to everyone. His rank made him proud but not rude or superior. Instead, he was attentive to everybody. He was naturally harmless, friendly, and helpful. Being presented at St. James's had made him extremely courteous.

Lady Lucas was a very good woman and not too clever to be useful to Mrs. Bennet. They had several children. The eldest was Charlotte, an intelligent and sensible woman of about twenty-seven. She was Elizabeth's close friend.

Of course, the Miss Lucases and the Miss Bennets had to discuss the ball. The morning after it, the Miss Lucases came to Longbourn to hear news and share it.

“You began the evening well, Charlotte,” Mrs. Bennet said to Miss Lucas with controlled politeness. “You were Mr. Bingley's first choice.”

“Yes, but he seemed to prefer his second.”

“Oh, you mean Jane, because he danced with her twice. That certainly looked like admiration. In fact, I think he did admire her. I heard something about it, though I hardly remember what. It was something about Mr. Robinson.”

“Perhaps you mean the conversation I overheard between him and Mr. Robinson. Did I not tell you? Mr. Robinson asked how he liked our Meryton public balls. He asked whether there were many pretty women and which was the prettiest. Mr. Bingley answered the last question immediately: ‘Oh, the eldest Miss Bennet, without question. No one could disagree about that.’”

“Really! That was very definite. It does seem as if—but it may still lead to nothing.”

“What I overheard was more useful than what you heard, Eliza,” said Charlotte. “Mr. Darcy is not as worth hearing as his friend, is he? Poor Eliza, to be only just tolerable.”

“Please do not make Lizzy angry about his bad treatment. He is so unpleasant that his liking her would be a misfortune. Mrs. Long told me he sat beside her for half an hour without speaking once.”

“Are you certain, ma’am? Could there be a small mistake?” said Jane. “I definitely saw Mr. Darcy speaking to her.”

“Yes, because she finally asked how he liked Netherfield. He had to answer. But she said he looked very angry that she spoke to him.”

“Miss Bingley told me,” said Jane, “that he says little except among close friends. With them, he is remarkably pleasant.”

“I do not believe a word of it, my dear. If he were so pleasant, he would have spoken to Mrs. Long. But I can guess the reason. Everyone says pride has completely filled him. He probably heard that Mrs. Long has no carriage and came to the ball in a hired one.”

“I do not care that he ignored Mrs. Long,” said Miss Lucas, “but I wish he had danced with Eliza.”

“Next time, Lizzy,” said her mother, “I would refuse to dance with him.”

“I believe, ma’am, I can safely promise never to dance with him.”

“His pride does not offend me as much as pride usually does,” said Miss Lucas. “He has an excuse. He is a handsome young man with family, fortune, and every advantage. Naturally, he thinks highly of himself. In a way, he has a right to be proud.”

“That is true,” Elizabeth replied. “I could easily forgive his pride if he had not wounded mine.”

Mary was proud of the seriousness of her thoughts. “Pride is a very common fault, I believe. Everything I have read convinces me of it. Human nature is especially likely to be proud. Almost everyone is pleased with some real or imagined quality. Vanity and pride are different, though people often use the words in the same way. A person may be proud without being vain. Pride concerns our opinion of ourselves. Vanity concerns what we want others to think of us.”

“If I were as rich as Mr. Darcy,” cried a young Lucas boy, “I would not care how proud I was. I would keep hunting dogs and drink a bottle of wine every day.”

“Then you would drink far too much,” said Mrs. Bennet. “If I saw you, I would take your bottle away immediately.”

The boy insisted that she would not. She continued to say that she would. Their argument ended only when the visit did.
