# Chapter XXIII

Elizabeth sat with her mother and sisters, thinking about Charlotte’s news. She did not know whether she had permission to mention it. Then Sir William Lucas appeared. His daughter had sent him to announce her engagement to the family. He gave them many compliments and congratulated himself on the future connection between the families. His listeners were not only surprised; they did not believe him. Mrs. Bennet firmly and impolitely insisted that he must be mistaken. Lydia, always careless and often rude, loudly cried out,—

“Good Lord! Sir William, how can you tell such a story? Do you not know that Mr. Collins wants to marry Lizzy?”

Only the patience of an experienced courtier could endure such treatment without anger. Sir William’s good manners carried him through it. He asked to insist that his information was true. Then he listened to all their rude remarks with the greatest patience.

Elizabeth felt she must rescue him from this unpleasant situation. She stepped forward and confirmed his story. She said that Charlotte herself had already told her. Elizabeth also tried to stop her mother’s and sisters’ cries. She warmly congratulated Sir William, and Jane quickly joined her. Elizabeth spoke about the happiness expected from the marriage and Mr. Collins’s excellent character. She also mentioned the convenient distance between Hunsford and London.

Mrs. Bennet was too overwhelmed to say much while Sir William remained. As soon as he left, however, her feelings poured out. First, she continued to disbelieve the whole story. Second, she was certain that someone had deceived Mr. Collins. Third, she hoped they would never be happy together. Fourth, she hoped the engagement might end. She drew two clear conclusions from everything. Elizabeth had caused the whole disaster, and everyone had treated Mrs. Bennet cruelly. She repeated these two points for the rest of the day. Nothing could comfort or calm her. Her anger did not end that day. A week passed before she could look at Elizabeth without scolding her. A month passed before she could speak politely to Sir William or Lady Lucas. Many months passed before she could forgive their daughter at all.

Mr. Bennet’s feelings were much calmer and, he said, very pleasant. He had always thought Charlotte Lucas fairly sensible. Now he was delighted to discover she was as foolish as his wife and more foolish than his daughter!

Jane admitted that the engagement surprised her a little. However, she spoke more about her sincere wish for their happiness. Elizabeth could not persuade her that such happiness was unlikely. Kitty and Lydia did not envy Miss Lucas at all, because Mr. Collins was only a clergyman. For them, the engagement was simply news to spread in Meryton.

Lady Lucas naturally enjoyed her victory over Mrs. Bennet. Now she could speak about the comfort of having a well-married daughter. She visited Longbourn more often than usual to say how happy she was. Mrs. Bennet’s bitter looks and unkind remarks might have driven happiness away.

Elizabeth and Charlotte now felt uncomfortable with each other and avoided the subject. Elizabeth believed they could never again share true trust. Her disappointment in Charlotte made her value Jane’s friendship even more. She could never doubt Jane’s good judgment or delicate feelings. She also became more worried each day about Jane’s happiness. Bingley had now been away for a week, and nobody had heard of his return.

Jane had answered Caroline’s letter quickly. She was counting the days until she could reasonably expect another. Mr. Collins’s promised letter of thanks arrived on Tuesday and was addressed to Mr. Bennet. Its gratitude was as solemn as if he had lived with them for a year. Once he had satisfied his conscience on that subject, he announced his happiness. With many joyful expressions, he said he had won the love of their pleasant neighbour, Miss Lucas. He explained why he had so readily accepted their invitation to visit Longbourn again. He only wanted to enjoy her company. He hoped to return on Monday in two weeks. Lady Catherine warmly approved of his marriage and wanted it to happen soon. He hoped this argument would persuade his dear Charlotte to choose an early wedding day. She would then make him the happiest of men.

Mrs. Bennet no longer welcomed Mr. Collins’s return to Hertfordshire. Instead, she complained about it as much as her husband did. It was strange that he planned to stay at Longbourn instead of Lucas Lodge. It was also very inconvenient and troublesome. She hated visitors while her health was poor, and people in love were the worst visitors. These were Mrs. Bennet’s gentle complaints. Only the greater pain of Mr. Bingley’s continued absence could replace them.

Neither Jane nor Elizabeth felt comfortable about Bingley. Day after day passed without any news of him. Soon a report spread through Meryton that he would not return to Netherfield all winter. It made Mrs. Bennet extremely angry. She always called it a terrible lie.

Even Elizabeth began to fear that Bingley’s sisters would succeed in keeping him away. She did not fear that his feelings had disappeared. She hated an idea so harmful to Jane’s happiness and Bingley’s loyalty. Still, it returned often. His two uncaring sisters and his powerful friend might work together. Miss Darcy’s attractions and London’s pleasures would help them. Elizabeth feared that all these things might overcome the strength of his love.

Jane’s anxiety was naturally more painful than Elizabeth’s. However, she wanted to hide whatever she felt. Therefore, she and Elizabeth never mentioned the subject. Their mother showed no such care. Hardly an hour passed without her speaking of Bingley or complaining about his delay. She even demanded that Jane admit how badly he had treated her by not returning. Jane needed all her steady gentleness to bear these attacks calmly.

Mr. Collins returned exactly on the Monday two weeks later. Longbourn did not welcome him as warmly as it had before. However, he was too happy to need much attention. Fortunately, his courtship kept him away from the others. He spent most of each day at Lucas Lodge. Sometimes he returned to Longbourn only just before bedtime. He then had enough time to apologise for his absence.

Mrs. Bennet was truly in a miserable state. Any mention of the engagement caused an agony of bad temper. Wherever she went, she heard people discussing it. She hated the sight of Miss Lucas. Charlotte would one day replace her as the lady of Longbourn. Mrs. Bennet therefore watched her with jealous hatred. Whenever Charlotte visited, Mrs. Bennet believed she was already waiting to possess the house. Whenever Charlotte spoke quietly to Mr. Collins, Mrs. Bennet knew what they discussed. They were certainly talking about the Longbourn estate. They must be planning to throw Mrs. Bennet and her daughters out after Mr. Bennet’s death. She complained bitterly to her husband.

“Indeed, Mr. Bennet,” she said, “it is very hard to think Charlotte Lucas will rule this house. I will have to make way for her and live to see her take my place!”

“My dear, do not surrender to such gloomy thoughts. Let us hope for something better. Let us imagine that I may live longer than you.”

This did not comfort Mrs. Bennet. Instead of answering, she continued as before.

“I cannot bear the thought of their having this whole estate. Without the entail, I would not mind it.”

“What would you not mind?”

“I would not mind anything at all.”

“Let us be thankful that you are protected from such a state of complete indifference.”

“I can never be thankful for anything about the entail, Mr. Bennet. I do not understand how anyone could make an entail that prevents daughters from inheriting their own estate. And all for Mr. Collins! Why should he have it instead of anyone else?”

“I will leave you to decide that,” said Mr. Bennet.
