# Chapter LII

Elizabeth was pleased to receive an answer as soon as possible. She immediately hurried into the little wood, where no one was likely to interrupt her. She sat on a bench and prepared to be happy. The letter’s length proved that her aunt had not refused her request.

“Gracechurch Street, September 6.

“My dear Niece,

“I have just received your letter and will spend the whole morning answering it. A few lines cannot contain everything I must tell you. I admit that your request surprised me. I did not expect it from you. Do not think I am angry. I only mean that I thought such questions were unnecessary for you. If you choose not to understand me, please forgive my boldness. Your uncle is equally surprised. He let Mr. Darcy act only because he believed you had a special interest in the affair. But if you are truly innocent and know nothing, I must speak more clearly. On the day I returned from Longbourn, your uncle received a most unexpected visitor. Mr. Darcy came and spoke privately with him for several hours. Their meeting ended before I arrived, so my curiosity suffered less than yours. Mr. Darcy told Mr. Gardiner that he had found your sister and Mr. Wickham. He had spoken with Wickham several times and Lydia once. As far as I understand, he left Derbyshire only one day after us. He came to London determined to find them. He said he acted because he blamed himself. Wickham’s worthlessness had not been made known, so a respectable young woman could love and trust him. Mr. Darcy generously blamed his own mistaken pride. Earlier, he had thought it beneath him to make Wickham’s private actions public. His own character should speak for itself. He now considered it his duty to correct the harm he had helped cause. If he had another reason, I am sure it did him honour. He spent several days in London before finding them. But he had a clue to guide him, unlike us. Knowing this was another reason for following us. A woman named Mrs. Younge was once Miss Darcy’s governess. She was dismissed for improper behaviour, though Mr. Darcy did not explain it. She then took a large house in Edward Street and began renting rooms. Mr. Darcy knew Mrs. Younge was closely acquainted with Wickham. He asked her for information as soon as he reached London. It took two or three days to get what he wanted. I suppose she would not betray her friend without a bribe. Yet she truly knew where Wickham was. He had visited her when he and Lydia first reached London. If she had room, they would have stayed in her house. At last, our kind friend obtained their address in —— Street. He saw Wickham and then insisted on seeing Lydia. He first tried to persuade Lydia to leave her shameful position. He wanted her to return to her friends as soon as they agreed to receive her. He offered every help he could give. But Lydia was completely determined to stay with Wickham. She cared nothing about her friends and wanted no help from Mr. Darcy. She refused to consider leaving Wickham. She was sure they would marry one day and did not care when. Given her feelings, Mr. Darcy could only secure and hurry the marriage. In his first conversation with Wickham, he easily learned that Wickham had never planned to marry. Wickham admitted that urgent gambling debts forced him to leave the regiment. He freely blamed every bad result of the elopement on Lydia’s foolishness. He planned to resign his officer’s position at once. He knew almost nothing about his future. He had to go somewhere but did not know where. He had nothing to live on. Mr. Darcy asked why he did not marry your sister immediately. Mr. Bennet was not considered rich, but he could give Wickham something. Marriage would improve Wickham’s position. Wickham’s answer showed that he still hoped for a richer marriage elsewhere. Yet in his present trouble, he could hardly resist an offer of immediate help. They met several times because there was much to discuss. Wickham naturally wanted more than he could receive. At last, he had to become reasonable. After they agreed, Mr. Darcy’s next step was to inform your uncle. He first called at Gracechurch Street the evening before I returned. Mr. Gardiner was unavailable. Mr. Darcy learned that your father was still staying there but would leave the next morning. He thought your uncle a more suitable adviser than your father. Therefore, he willingly waited until Mr. Bennet left. He did not leave his name, so we knew only that a gentleman had called on business. On Saturday, he returned. Your father was gone, and your uncle was home. As I said, they had a great deal to discuss. They met again on Sunday, when I also saw Mr. Darcy. Everything was not settled until Monday. Then the express messenger immediately left for Longbourn. But our visitor was very stubborn. Lizzy, I believe stubbornness is his true fault after all. People have accused him of many faults, but this is the real one. He had to do everything himself. Your uncle would gladly have settled the whole affair. I do not say this to receive thanks, so mention nothing about it. They argued for a long time. Neither the young gentleman nor the young lady deserved so much effort. At last, your uncle had to give way. He could not help his niece but would probably receive the credit. This greatly annoyed him. I believe your letter pleased him because it demanded an explanation. The truth will remove his borrowed honour and give the praise to the right person. But, Lizzy, you may tell only Jane at most. You probably know what has been done for the young couple. Wickham’s debts, considerably more than a thousand pounds, will be paid. Another thousand pounds will be settled on Lydia in addition to her own money. His new officer’s position will also be purchased. Mr. Darcy’s stated reason for doing everything himself was the one I described. His silence and lack of proper thought had allowed people to misunderstand Wickham’s character. Therefore, society had welcomed and noticed Wickham. There may be some truth in this. But I doubt that anyone’s silence can be responsible for the event. Despite all this fine talk, dear Lizzy, your uncle would never have yielded without another belief. We believed Mr. Darcy had another interest in the affair. When everything was decided, he returned to his friends at Pemberley. He agreed to return to London for the wedding and finish the financial matters. I believe I have now told you everything. You said the story would greatly surprise you. I hope it causes no displeasure. Lydia came to us, and Wickham could visit the house freely. He behaved exactly as he had when I knew him in Hertfordshire. I would not describe Lydia’s poor behaviour with us without Jane’s letter last Wednesday. That letter showed she behaved exactly the same at home. Therefore, my account will cause no new pain. I spoke to Lydia seriously several times. I explained the wickedness of her action and her family’s suffering. If she heard me, it was by good luck, because she certainly did not listen. Sometimes she made me very angry. Then I remembered my dear Elizabeth and Jane and remained patient for your sake. Mr. Darcy returned exactly when promised and attended the wedding, as Lydia told you. He dined with us the next day and planned to leave London on Wednesday or Thursday. My dear Lizzy, will you be angry if I use this chance to say something? I was never brave enough before: I like him very much. His behaviour towards us was as pleasant as in Derbyshire. I like his intelligence and opinions. He needs only a little more liveliness. If he marries wisely, his wife may teach him that. I thought him very clever and secretive because he hardly mentioned your name. But secrecy seems fashionable. Please forgive me if I assume too much. At least do not punish me by keeping me away from P. I cannot be completely happy until I travel around the whole park. A low carriage with two pretty ponies would be perfect. I must stop writing. The children have wanted me for half an hour.

“Yours very sincerely, “M. Gardiner.”

The letter threw Elizabeth into great excitement. She could hardly decide whether pleasure or pain was stronger. Uncertainty had caused vague suspicions about Mr. Darcy’s help with Lydia’s marriage. She had resisted them because such goodness seemed too great to believe. She had also feared they were true because she would owe him so much. Now the truth went beyond her strongest suspicions. He had followed them to London on purpose. He had accepted all the trouble and shame of the search. He had begged a woman he must hate and despise. He had repeatedly met the man he most wished to avoid. Even speaking Wickham’s name punished him. Yet Darcy had reasoned with him, persuaded him, and finally bribed him. He had done all this for a girl he could neither respect nor like. Elizabeth’s heart whispered that he had done it for her. But other thoughts soon stopped that hope. Even her vanity could not trust that his love remained strong enough. She had already refused him. Could love overcome his natural horror at becoming related to Wickham? Wickham’s brother-in-law! Every kind of pride must oppose that connection. Mr. Darcy had certainly done much. Elizabeth felt ashamed when she considered how much. But he had given a believable reason for acting. He reasonably felt that he had done wrong. He was generous and had the money to help. Elizabeth would not imagine herself as his main reason. Still, some remaining affection for her might have strengthened his efforts. Her peace of mind depended greatly on the result. It was extremely painful to owe so much to someone she could never repay. Lydia’s return and saved reputation—everything came from him. Elizabeth deeply regretted every unkind feeling and bold speech against him. She felt humbled for herself but proud of him. Compassion and honour had helped him overcome his own feelings. She read her aunt’s praise again and again. It was hardly enough, but it pleased her. She even felt some joy mixed with regret. Her aunt and uncle firmly believed that love and trust existed between Darcy and Elizabeth.

Someone approached and interrupted Elizabeth’s thoughts. Before she could turn onto another path, Wickham caught up with her.

“I fear I am interrupting your lonely walk, my dear sister?” he said as he joined her.

“You certainly are,” she replied with a smile. “But that does not mean the interruption is unwelcome.”

“I would be very sorry if it were. We were always good friends, and now we are better.”

“True. Are the others coming outside?”

“I do not know. Mrs. Bennet and Lydia are taking the carriage to Meryton. My dear sister, our aunt and uncle tell me you actually saw Pemberley.”

She said that she had.

“I almost envy your pleasure, though I think it would have been too much for me. Otherwise, I could visit on my way to Newcastle. I suppose you saw the old housekeeper. Poor Reynolds was always very fond of me. But of course she did not mention my name.”

“Yes, she did.”

“And what did she say?”

“She said you had joined the army. She feared you had—not turned out well. At such a distance, stories become strangely changed.”

“Certainly,” he replied, biting his lips. Elizabeth hoped she had silenced him. But soon he said:

“I was surprised to see Darcy in London last month. We passed each other several times. I wonder what he was doing there.”

“Perhaps he was preparing to marry Miss de Bourgh,” said Elizabeth. “Something special must bring him there at this time of year.”

“Certainly. Did you see him while staying at Lambton? I thought the Gardiners said you did.”

“Yes. He introduced us to his sister.”

“And do you like her?”

“Very much.”

“I have heard she has greatly improved during the last year or two. When I last saw her, she did not promise much. I am very glad you liked her. I hope she becomes a good woman.”

“I am sure she will. She has passed the most difficult age.”

“Did you travel through the village of Kympton?”

“I do not remember doing so.”

“I mention it because I should have received the parish position there. It is a delightful place with an excellent parsonage. It would have suited me perfectly.”

“How would you have liked writing sermons?”

“Very much. I would have considered it part of my duty, and the work would soon become easy. One should not complain. Still, what a life it would have been for me! Its peace and privacy would have matched all my ideas of happiness. But it was not meant to happen. Did Darcy ever mention this while you were in Kent?”

“I heard from an authority just as good. The position was left to you only under certain conditions. The present patron had the right to decide.”

“You heard that! Yes, there was something of that kind. I told you so from the beginning, if you remember.”

“I also heard that writing sermons once pleased you much less than it does now. You had firmly decided never to enter the church. The matter was settled with you in another way.”

“You heard that! It has some truth in it. You may remember what I said when we first discussed the subject.”

They were almost at the house because Elizabeth had walked quickly to escape him. For her sister’s sake, she did not want to anger him. She only answered with a good-humoured smile:

“Come, Mr. Wickham, we are brother and sister now. Let us not argue about the past. In future, I hope we will always agree.”

She held out her hand. He kissed it with warm politeness, though he hardly knew where to look. Then they entered the house.
