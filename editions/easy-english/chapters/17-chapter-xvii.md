# Chapter XVII

The next day, Elizabeth told Jane about her conversation with Mr. Wickham. Jane listened with shock and concern. She could not believe Mr. Darcy was unworthy of Mr. Bingley's friendship. Yet she also could not doubt a young man with Wickham's pleasant appearance. The possibility that he had truly suffered such cruelty touched all her kind feelings. She could only think well of both men and defend each one's conduct. Anything she could not explain must come from accident or mistake.

“I am sure they have both been deceived in some way,” she said. “We cannot imagine how. Selfish people may have given each man a false picture of the other. Without truly blaming either one, we cannot guess what circumstances separated them.”

“Very true. Now, dear Jane, how will you defend the selfish people who probably caused all this? Please prove them innocent too. Otherwise, we shall have to think badly of someone.”

“Laugh as much as you like, but you will not change my opinion. Dearest Lizzy, consider how shameful this story makes Mr. Darcy appear. He supposedly treated his father's favourite this way, after his father promised to provide for Wickham. It is impossible. No man with ordinary human feeling, or any care for his reputation, could do it. Could all his closest friends be completely mistaken about him? Oh, no.”

“I can much more easily believe that Mr. Bingley is deceived. Mr. Wickham could hardly invent the whole story he told me last night. He gave names and facts without any hesitation. If it is false, Mr. Darcy can deny it. Besides, Wickham's face showed the truth.”

“It is very difficult and upsetting. One does not know what to think.”

“Forgive me—one knows exactly what to think.”

Jane could be certain about only one point. If someone had deceived Mr. Bingley, he would suffer greatly when the matter became public.

This conversation took place among the bushes in the garden. Then the arrival of some people they had discussed called them inside. Mr. Bingley and his sisters came to invite them personally to the long-awaited Netherfield ball. It would take place the following Tuesday. His sisters were delighted to see their dear friend again. They said it felt like years since they had met. They repeatedly asked what Jane had done since they separated. They paid little attention to the rest of the family. They avoided Mrs. Bennet as much as possible, said little to Elizabeth, and nothing to the others. Soon they left again. They rose so quickly that their brother was surprised. They hurried away as if escaping Mrs. Bennet's polite attention.

Every woman in the family was delighted by the thought of the Netherfield ball. Mrs. Bennet believed it was being held in honour of her eldest daughter. She felt especially flattered that Mr. Bingley invited them himself instead of sending a formal card. Jane imagined a happy evening with her two friends and their brother's attention. Elizabeth happily imagined dancing often with Mr. Wickham. She expected Mr. Darcy's face and behaviour to confirm Wickham's entire story. Catherine and Lydia did not depend on one event or person for happiness. Like Elizabeth, each planned to dance half the evening with Mr. Wickham. But many other partners could satisfy them, and a ball was always a ball. Even Mary assured her family that she did not object to going.

“As long as my mornings remain my own, that is enough,” she said. “Occasionally attending evening events is no sacrifice. Society has a claim on all of us. I believe everyone should sometimes rest and enjoy amusement.”

Elizabeth was so cheerful that she even spoke to Mr. Collins without needing to. She asked whether he planned to accept Mr. Bingley's invitation. If so, would he think it proper to join the evening's amusement? She was surprised that he had no doubts at all. He did not fear criticism from either the Archbishop or Lady Catherine de Bourgh for dancing.

“I certainly do not believe such a ball can cause any harm,” he said. “A respectable young man is giving it for respectable people. I have no objection to dancing myself. In fact, I hope every one of my beautiful cousins will honour me with her hand. Miss Elizabeth, I especially ask you for the first two dances. I trust that my cousin Jane will understand the proper reason for this preference. She must not think I mean any disrespect towards her.”

Elizabeth felt completely trapped. She had fully expected Wickham to ask her for those very dances. Now she would have Mr. Collins instead! Her cheerful question could not have come at a worse time. Yet she could do nothing. Her happiness and Mr. Wickham's had to wait a little longer. She accepted Mr. Collins as politely as possible. His attention pleased her even less because it suggested a more serious intention. She now realized he had chosen her from among her sisters. He considered her worthy to become mistress of Hunsford Parsonage. She could also help complete a quadrille card table at Rosings when better guests were absent. The idea soon became a certainty. His politeness towards her steadily increased. He often tried to praise her intelligence and lively manner. She was more surprised than pleased by the effect of her charms. Before long, her mother made it clear that she greatly wished them to marry. Elizabeth refused to respond to the hint. She knew any answer would cause a serious argument. Mr. Collins might never propose. Until he did, there was no reason to quarrel about him.

Without preparations and talk about the Netherfield ball, the younger Miss Bennets would have suffered badly. From the invitation until the ball, constant rain stopped every walk to Meryton. They could not visit their aunt, see the officers, or search for news. Someone else even had to collect their decorative shoe ribbons for Netherfield. The weather also tested Elizabeth's patience because she could not know Mr. Wickham better. Only Tuesday's dance made such a Friday, Saturday, Sunday, and Monday bearable to Kitty and Lydia.
