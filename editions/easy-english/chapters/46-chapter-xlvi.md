# Chapter XLVI

Elizabeth was very disappointed when no letter from Jane waited at Lambton. She felt the same disappointment each morning. On the third morning, however, two letters arrived together. One had been sent to the wrong place. This did not surprise Elizabeth because Jane had written the address very badly.

The letters arrived just as they were preparing for a walk. Her aunt and uncle left without her, so she could read in peace. She opened the misdirected letter first. Jane had written it five days earlier. The beginning described small parties, visits, and local news. The second half was dated a day later and showed great anxiety. It contained much more serious news:

“Since writing the words above, dearest Lizzy, something very serious and unexpected has happened. I am afraid of alarming you, but please remember that we are all well. My news concerns poor Lydia. An express messenger arrived at midnight, just after we went to bed. Colonel Forster wrote that Lydia had gone to Scotland with one of his officers. To tell the truth, she has gone with Wickham! Imagine our surprise. Kitty, however, does not seem completely surprised. I am very, very sorry. It is such an unwise match for both of them! Still, I hope for the best. Perhaps people have misunderstood his character. I can easily believe that he is careless and foolish. Yet this action—and we should be glad of it—does not show a bad heart. At least he cannot be marrying her for money. He must know that my father can give her nothing. Our poor mother is deeply upset. My father is bearing it better. I am thankful that we never told them what people had said against Wickham. We must forget it ourselves. They probably left at about midnight on Saturday. No one missed them until eight yesterday morning. The express messenger left at once. My dear Lizzy, they must have passed within ten miles of us. Colonel Forster expects to come here soon. Lydia left a short note for his wife and explained their plan. I must finish because I cannot leave my poor mother for long. I fear you may not understand this letter. I hardly know what I have written.”

Elizabeth gave herself no time to think and hardly understood her own feelings. She seized the other letter and opened it with great impatience. Jane had written it one day after finishing the first letter. It said:

“By now, my dearest sister, you have received my hurried letter. I hope this one is clearer. I have enough time, but my thoughts are so confused that I cannot promise to write clearly. Dearest Lizzy, I hardly know what I want to say. But I have bad news, and I must tell you at once. A marriage between Mr. Wickham and our poor Lydia would be very unwise. Yet now we are desperate to know that they have married. We have strong reasons to fear they did not go to Scotland. Colonel Forster arrived yesterday. He had left Brighton the day before, only a few hours after the express. Lydia’s short letter to Mrs. Forster suggested that they were going to Gretna Green. But Denny had said something which showed that he believed otherwise. He thought Wickham never meant to go there or marry Lydia. Colonel Forster heard this and became alarmed. He left B. at once and tried to follow their route. He easily followed them to Clapham, but no farther. When they reached that place, they changed into a hired coach. They dismissed the carriage that had brought them from Epsom. We only know that they continued towards London. I do not know what to think. Colonel Forster made every possible inquiry on that side of London. Then he came into Hertfordshire and asked again at every toll gate. He also asked at the inns in Barnet and Hatfield, but learned nothing. No such people had passed through. He came to Longbourn with the kindest concern. He told us his fears in a way that showed his good heart. I am truly sorry for him and Mrs. Forster. No one can blame either of them. Our distress is very great, my dear Lizzy. My father and mother believe the worst, but I cannot think so badly of Wickham. Many things might make a private wedding in London better than their first plan. And even if he could plan such harm against a young woman with Lydia’s family connections, could she have lost all sense? It is impossible! However, Colonel Forster does not trust that they will marry. When I explained my hopes, he shook his head. He fears Wickham cannot be trusted. My poor mother is truly ill and stays in her room. It would be better if she could make an effort, but we cannot expect that. I have never seen my father so deeply affected. Poor Kitty is being blamed for hiding their attachment. But Lydia told her in confidence, so we cannot be surprised. Dearest Lizzy, I am truly glad you avoided some of these painful scenes. Yet now the first shock has passed, may I admit that I long for your return? I will not selfishly urge you to come if it is inconvenient. Farewell! I have taken up my pen again to do exactly what I said I would not do. The situation leaves me no choice. I earnestly beg all of you to come here as soon as possible. I know my dear aunt and uncle so well that I am not afraid to ask. I have one more request for my uncle. My father is leaving immediately for London with Colonel Forster. He will try to find Lydia. I do not know what he plans to do. His extreme distress will prevent him from acting in the safest and wisest way. Colonel Forster must return to Brighton tomorrow evening. In this crisis, my uncle’s advice and help would mean everything. He will understand my feelings at once, and I trust his goodness.”

“Oh! Where is my uncle?” cried Elizabeth when she finished the letter. She jumped up, eager to follow him without wasting a moment. But when she reached the door, a servant opened it. Mr. Darcy appeared. Her pale face and sudden movement alarmed him. Lydia's situation had pushed every other thought from Elizabeth's mind. Before he could speak, Elizabeth cried, “Please excuse me, but I must leave you. I must find Mr. Gardiner at once about urgent business. I cannot lose a moment.”

“Good God! What is the matter?” he cried, showing more feeling than politeness. Then he controlled himself. “I will not delay you for a minute. But let me or the servant follow Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner. You are not well enough to go yourself.”

Elizabeth hesitated, but her knees were shaking. She knew that she could not follow them successfully. She called the servant back and told him to bring his master and mistress home immediately. She spoke so breathlessly that he could hardly understand her.

After the servant left, Elizabeth sat down because she could not stand. She looked so ill that Darcy could not leave her. In a gentle, pitying voice, he said, “Let me call your maid. Is there anything you could take for immediate relief? Shall I bring you a glass of wine? You are very ill.”

“No, thank you,” she replied, trying to control herself. “Nothing is wrong with me. I am quite well. I am only distressed by dreadful news from Longbourn.”

She began to cry and could not speak for several minutes. Darcy waited in painful uncertainty. He could only express his concern in broken words and watch her with compassion. At last she continued. “I have just received a letter from Jane with dreadful news. It cannot remain secret. My youngest sister has left all her friends. She has run away with—with Mr. Wickham. They left Brighton together. You know him too well to doubt what this means. She has no money or connections that could attract him. She is lost for ever.”

Darcy stood still in astonishment.

“And I might have prevented it!” Elizabeth added in an even more troubled voice. “I knew what he was. If only I had told my family part of what I learned! If they had known his character, this could not have happened. But now it is all too late.”

“I am truly sorry,” cried Darcy. “I am sorry and shocked. But are you absolutely certain?”

“Oh, yes! They left Brighton together on Sunday night. They were followed almost to London, but no farther. They certainly did not go to Scotland.”

“And what has been done to find her and bring her back?”

“My father has gone to London. Jane has written to beg for my uncle’s immediate help. I hope we will leave within half an hour. But nothing can be done. I know nothing can be done. How can anyone influence such a man? How can they even find them? I have no hope at all. Everything about it is horrible!”

Darcy shook his head in silent agreement.

“When I learned his real character, I should have known what to do!” she continued. “But I did not know how much I dared to say. I feared doing too much. What a terrible, terrible mistake!”

Darcy did not answer. He seemed hardly to hear her and walked around the room, thinking deeply. His face was tense and gloomy. Elizabeth soon noticed and believed she understood. His feelings for her were fading. Everything must fail after such proof of her family’s weakness and disgrace. She could not blame him or feel surprised. Yet believing that he had overcome his love did not comfort her. Instead, it made her understand her own wishes. She had never known so clearly that she could love him. She knew it now, when such love must be useless.

Thoughts about herself entered her mind, but they could not hold it. Lydia’s disgrace and the misery she brought them soon replaced every private concern. Elizabeth covered her face with her handkerchief and forgot everything else. After several minutes, Darcy’s voice brought her back. His manner showed both compassion and restraint as he said:

“I fear you have wanted me to leave for some time. I have no excuse for staying except real but useless concern. I wish I could say or do something to comfort you. But I will not trouble you with useless wishes that may seem to ask for thanks. I fear this unhappy affair will prevent my sister from seeing you at Pemberley today.”

“Oh, yes! Please apologise to Miss Darcy for us. Tell her that urgent business calls us home immediately. Hide the unhappy truth as long as possible. I know it cannot stay hidden for long.”

He readily promised to keep the secret and again expressed his sorrow. He hoped the affair would end more happily than they could now expect. He left his compliments for her relations. Then, after one serious final look, he went away.

As he left, Elizabeth felt they would probably never meet again with such warmth. Their meetings in Derbyshire had all been friendly. She looked back over their whole strange and changing acquaintance. She sighed at the unfairness of her feelings. Now she wanted the acquaintance to continue. Earlier, she would have been glad to see it end.

If gratitude and respect are good foundations for love, Elizabeth’s changed feelings are natural and blameless. Perhaps some people believe such love is unreasonable. They prefer love that begins at first sight, before two words are exchanged. Elizabeth had already tried that more exciting method with Wickham. Its failure might allow her to try this quieter kind of love. Whatever the truth, she watched Darcy leave with regret. This first result of Lydia’s disgrace added to her pain. Since Jane’s second letter, Elizabeth had never believed that Wickham meant to marry Lydia. She thought only Jane could keep such a hope. This new information caused little surprise. The first letter had astonished her. Why would Wickham marry a girl who could bring him no money? How could Lydia have attracted him enough? But now everything seemed too natural. Lydia had enough charm for a brief affair. Elizabeth did not think Lydia had knowingly run away without expecting marriage. Yet neither Lydia’s sense nor her morals could protect her from becoming an easy victim.

While the regiment was in Hertfordshire, Elizabeth had never noticed Lydia preferring Wickham. But Lydia needed very little encouragement to attach herself to anyone. One officer and then another became her favourite whenever he noticed her. Her feelings changed constantly, but always had an object. Elizabeth now understood the harm caused by neglect and foolish indulgence. She felt it painfully.

Elizabeth desperately wanted to be at home. She wanted to hear everything, see everyone, and help Jane with the family’s troubles. Their father was away, and their mother could do nothing and needed constant care. Elizabeth almost believed nothing could help Lydia. Still, her uncle’s involvement seemed extremely important. Waiting for him was painful. Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner hurried back in alarm. The servant’s message had made them fear Elizabeth was suddenly ill. She quickly assured them that she was well. Then she explained why she had called them. She read both letters aloud and stressed the final request with a shaking voice. Lydia had never been their favourite, but Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner were deeply affected. The danger concerned the whole family, not Lydia alone. After their first cries of shock and horror, Mr. Gardiner promised every help he could give. Elizabeth had expected no less, but thanked him with grateful tears. All three wanted the same thing, so they quickly arranged the journey. They would leave as soon as possible. “But what shall we do about Pemberley?” cried Mrs. Gardiner. “John said Mr. Darcy was here when you sent for us. Was he?”

“Yes. I told him that we could not keep our engagement. It is all settled.”

“What is all settled?” Mrs. Gardiner repeated as she ran to prepare. “Are they close enough for Elizabeth to tell him the truth? Oh, I wish I understood them!”

Wishes were useless, though they distracted her during the next hurried hour. Elizabeth believed she was too miserable to do anything. If she had been free to sit still, she would have remained certain of it. But she had work to do like her aunt. They had to write notes to all their Lambton friends. These notes gave false reasons for their sudden departure. Everything was finished within an hour. Meanwhile, Mr. Gardiner paid their bill at the inn. Nothing remained except to leave. After all the morning’s misery, Elizabeth soon found herself in the carriage on the road to Longbourn.
