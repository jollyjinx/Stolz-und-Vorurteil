# Chapter XLIII

As they drove, Elizabeth anxiously watched for her first sight of Pemberley Woods. When they finally turned through the entrance, her heart beat with nervous excitement.

The park was very large and its land had many different forms. They entered at one of its lowest points. For some time, they drove through a wide, beautiful wood.

Elizabeth’s mind was too busy for conversation, but she admired every remarkable place and view. They slowly climbed for half a mile and reached the top of a high hill. The wood ended, and Pemberley House immediately caught their eyes across the valley. The road curved sharply down towards it. The large, handsome stone house stood well on rising ground. High wooded hills rose behind it. In front, a naturally important stream had been made larger without looking artificial. Its banks were neither formal nor covered in false decoration. Elizabeth was delighted. She had never seen nature do more for a place. She had also never seen natural beauty less damaged by poor human taste. They all warmly admired it. At that moment, she thought being mistress of Pemberley might mean something!

They descended the hill, crossed the bridge, and drove to the door. As Elizabeth examined the house more closely, her fear of meeting its owner returned. Perhaps the maid had been mistaken. They asked to see the house and were allowed into the hall. While waiting for the housekeeper, Elizabeth wondered at finding herself there.

The housekeeper arrived. She was a respectable older woman, less grand and more polite than Elizabeth expected. They followed her into the dining room. It was large, well-shaped, and handsomely decorated. After a quick look, Elizabeth went to a window for the view. Across the valley, distance made the wooded hill they had descended look steeper and more beautiful. Every part of the land was well arranged. Elizabeth looked with delight at the river and trees along its banks. She followed the valley’s curves as far as she could see. These objects changed position as they entered other rooms, but every window offered beauty. The rooms were high and handsome. Their furniture suited their owner’s wealth. With admiration for his taste, Elizabeth saw that nothing was too bright or unnecessarily grand. Pemberley had less splendour than Rosings, but far more true elegance.

“And I might have been mistress of this place!” she thought. “I might know these rooms as my home! Instead of seeing them as a stranger, I might enjoy them as my own. I could welcome my uncle and aunt here as visitors. But no,” she remembered, “that could never happen. I would have lost my uncle and aunt. I would not have been allowed to invite them.”

This fortunate thought saved her from something close to regret.

She wanted to ask the housekeeper whether her master was truly absent, but lacked courage. At last, her uncle asked. Elizabeth turned away in alarm as Mrs. Reynolds said that he was away. She added, “But we expect him tomorrow with a large group of friends.” Elizabeth felt delighted that nothing had delayed their own journey by one day!

Her aunt called her to examine a picture. Elizabeth saw Mr. Wickham’s face among several small portraits above the fireplace. Smiling, her aunt asked what she thought of it. The housekeeper explained that he was her late master’s steward’s son. Her late master had paid to raise and educate him. “He has now joined the army,” she added, “but I fear he has become very wild.”

Mrs. Gardiner smiled at her niece, but Elizabeth could not smile back.

“And that,” said Mrs. Reynolds, pointing to another small portrait, “is my master. It looks very like him. Both pictures were made at the same time, about eight years ago.”

“I have heard much about your master’s handsome appearance,” said Mrs. Gardiner, studying the picture. “It is a handsome face. But Lizzy can tell us whether it looks like him.”

When Mrs. Reynolds learned Elizabeth knew her master, her respect for Elizabeth seemed to increase.

“Does that young lady know Mr. Darcy?”

Elizabeth blushed and said, “A little.”

“And do you not think him a very handsome gentleman, ma’am?”

“Yes, very handsome.”

“I know nobody as handsome. But upstairs, you will see a larger and better picture of him. This was my late master’s favourite room. These small portraits remain exactly where they were then. He loved them very much.”

This explained to Elizabeth why Mr. Wickham’s picture was still among them.

Mrs. Reynolds then showed them a picture of Miss Darcy at only eight years old.

“And is Miss Darcy as handsome as her brother?” Mr. Gardiner asked.

“Oh yes—the handsomest young lady anyone ever saw, and so talented! She plays music and sings all day. A new instrument has just arrived for her in the next room. It is a present from my master. She comes here with him tomorrow.”

Mr. Gardiner’s easy and pleasant manners encouraged the housekeeper through questions and remarks. Whether from pride or love, Mrs. Reynolds clearly enjoyed discussing her master and his sister.

“Does your master spend much of the year at Pemberley?”

“Not as much as I would like, sir. But I think he spends half his time here. Miss Darcy always comes for the summer months.”

“Except,” thought Elizabeth, “when she goes to Ramsgate.”

“If your master married, you might see him more.”

“Yes, sir, but I do not know when that will happen. I do not know who is good enough for him.”

Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner smiled. Elizabeth had to say, “It speaks very well of him that you think so.”

“I say only the truth and what everyone who knows him will say,” Mrs. Reynolds replied. Elizabeth thought this praise went rather far. She listened with growing surprise as the housekeeper added, “He has never spoken one angry word to me. I have known him since he was four years old.”

This praise was the most surprising of all and completely opposed Elizabeth’s beliefs. She had firmly believed he was not good-tempered. Now she listened with intense attention and wanted to hear more. She felt grateful when her uncle said—

“Very few people deserve such words. You are fortunate to have such a master.”

“Yes, sir, I know I am. I could travel the whole world without finding a better one. I have always noticed that kind children grow into kind adults. He was the sweetest-tempered, most generous boy in the world.”

Elizabeth almost stared at her. “Can this be Mr. Darcy?” she thought.

“His father was an excellent man,” said Mrs. Gardiner.

“Yes, ma’am, he truly was. His son will be just like him—just as friendly to poor people.”

Elizabeth listened, wondered, doubted, and impatiently wanted more. Mrs. Reynolds could interest her in no other subject. She uselessly explained the pictures, the room sizes, and the furniture’s prices. Mr. Gardiner found her family loyalty amusing. He believed it explained her extreme praise of her master. As they climbed the great stairs, he soon guided her back to the subject. She spoke warmly about Mr. Darcy’s many good qualities.

“He is the best landowner and the best master who ever lived,” she said. “He is unlike today’s wild young men, who think only of themselves. Every one of his tenants and servants speaks well of him. Some people call him proud, but I have never seen any pride. I think they only say that because he does not talk endlessly like other young men.”

“How kind and good this makes him appear!” Elizabeth thought.

“This wonderful description,” her aunt whispered as they walked, “does not match his treatment of our poor friend.”

“Perhaps we were deceived.”

“That is not very likely. Our source was too reliable.”

Upstairs, they entered a very pretty sitting room. It had recently been made brighter and more elegant than the rooms below. Mrs. Reynolds explained that Miss Darcy had liked it during her last stay at Pemberley. Her brother had changed it especially to please her.

“He is certainly a good brother,” Elizabeth said as she walked towards a window.

Mrs. Reynolds imagined Miss Darcy’s joy when she saw the room. “This is always his way,” she added. “Anything that might please his sister is done immediately. There is nothing he would not do for her.”

Only the picture gallery and two or three main bedrooms remained. The gallery held many good paintings, but Elizabeth understood little about art. Downstairs, she had preferred some of Miss Darcy’s coloured drawings. Their subjects were usually more interesting and easier to understand.

Many family portraits hung in the gallery, but most offered little interest to a stranger. Elizabeth searched for the only face she would know. At last, it caught her attention. Mr. Darcy’s portrait looked remarkably like him. He wore the smile she sometimes remembered seeing when he looked at her. She studied the picture seriously for several minutes. Before leaving the gallery, she returned to it again. Mrs. Reynolds said it had been painted while his father was alive.

Elizabeth now felt more gently towards the real man than ever before. Mrs. Reynolds’s praise of him was important. What praise is more valuable than an intelligent servant’s? Elizabeth considered him as a brother, landowner, and master. The happiness of many people was under his care! He could give them so much pleasure or pain, and do so much good or harm. Everything the housekeeper said supported his character. As Elizabeth stood before his painted eyes, she thought of his love with deeper gratitude. She remembered its strength and became more forgiving of how badly he had expressed it.

After seeing every part of the house open to visitors, they returned downstairs. They said goodbye to the housekeeper. At the hall door, the gardener took charge of them.

They crossed the lawn towards the river, and Elizabeth turned for another look. Her uncle and aunt stopped too. Mr. Gardiner was guessing the building’s age. Then its owner suddenly appeared from the road behind the house leading to the stables.

They were only twenty yards apart, and his sudden appearance made escape impossible. Their eyes immediately met, and both blushed deeply. He actually jumped and stood still with surprise for a moment. Then he recovered, approached the group, and spoke to Elizabeth. He was not perfectly calm, but he was perfectly polite.

Elizabeth naturally turned away, but stopped as he approached. She received his greeting with uncontrollable embarrassment. The Gardiners might have recognised Mr. Darcy from his face or the portrait. If not, the gardener’s surprise at seeing his master made his identity clear. They stood a short distance away while he spoke with their niece. Elizabeth was surprised and confused. She hardly dared look at his face and did not know how she answered his polite family questions. His manner had completely changed since their last meeting. Every sentence increased her embarrassment. She constantly remembered how improper it might seem to find her there. Those few minutes were among the most uncomfortable of her life. He did not seem much more comfortable. His voice lacked its usual calm seriousness. He repeatedly and quickly asked when she left Longbourn and how long she would stay in Derbyshire. His troubled thoughts were obvious.

At last, he seemed unable to think of anything more. After standing silently for several moments, he suddenly remembered himself and said goodbye.

The others joined Elizabeth and praised his appearance, but she heard nothing. Lost in her own feelings, she silently followed them. Shame and annoyance overwhelmed her. Coming here was the world’s most unfortunate and badly judged decision! How strange must it appear to him! Such a proud man might view it as shameful. Perhaps he thought she had deliberately placed herself in his way again! Why had she come? Why had he arrived one day early? If they had been ten minutes earlier, they would have escaped his notice. He had clearly only just arrived and stepped from his horse or carriage. She blushed repeatedly at the meeting’s bad luck. And why was his behaviour so completely changed? It was amazing that he spoke to her at all! But he spoke so politely and asked about her family. She had never seen him less formal or heard such gentleness from him. It strongly contrasted with their last meeting at Rosings Park, when he gave her his letter. She did not know what to think or how to explain it.

They now followed a beautiful walk beside the water. Every step revealed finer land or a better view of the approaching woods. But Elizabeth noticed nothing for some time. She automatically answered her uncle and aunt when they repeatedly addressed her. She appeared to look at the objects they showed her, but saw no part of the scene. Her thoughts remained fixed on one place in Pemberley House, wherever Mr. Darcy now was. She wanted to know his thoughts and how he viewed her. Despite everything, was she still dear to him? Perhaps he was polite only because he felt comfortable. Yet something in his voice did not sound comfortable. She could not tell whether seeing her caused him more pain or pleasure. But he certainly had not seen her calmly.

At last, her companions noticed her absent mind and brought her back. She knew she must behave more like herself.

They entered the woods and left the river for a while. From higher ground, openings between the trees offered lovely views. They saw the valley, opposite hills, long woods, and sometimes part of the stream. Mr. Gardiner wanted to walk around the whole park but feared it was too far. With a proud smile, the gardener said it was ten miles around. That settled the question, so they followed the usual path. Later, it descended through hanging woods to a narrow part of the river. They crossed a simple bridge that suited the natural scene. This place had less decoration than anywhere else they had visited. The valley narrowed into a deep hollow with only the stream and a narrow path. Rough young woods bordered it. Elizabeth wanted to explore every curve. But after crossing the bridge, they saw how far they were from the house. Mrs. Gardiner was not a strong walker and could go no farther. She only wanted to return quickly to the carriage. Elizabeth had to agree. They took the shortest route towards the house on the river’s opposite side. Still, they moved slowly. Mr. Gardiner loved fishing, though he rarely enjoyed it. He watched for trout in the water and discussed them with the gardener. While they wandered slowly, Mr. Darcy surprised them by approaching again. Elizabeth felt as amazed as before. This path was more open, so they saw him before meeting. Despite her surprise, she was better prepared. If he truly intended to meet them, she would speak and behave calmly. For a few moments, she thought he might choose another path. A turn hid him, but after it he stood immediately before them. One look showed that he remained equally polite. Elizabeth tried to match him by praising the place’s beauty. But she only managed “delightful” and “charming.” Then she remembered her situation. He might misunderstand her praise of Pemberley. She blushed and said nothing more.

Mrs. Gardiner stood a little behind Elizabeth. When Elizabeth paused, Darcy asked for the honour of an introduction to her friends. Such politeness completely surprised her. She almost smiled because he now wished to meet the same people his pride had opposed during his proposal. “How surprised he will be,” she thought, “when he learns who they are! He now believes they are fashionable people.”

Elizabeth immediately introduced them. As she explained their relationship, she secretly watched Darcy’s reaction. She half expected him to escape such embarrassing companions as quickly as possible. Their connection clearly surprised him, but he faced it bravely. Instead of leaving, he turned back with them and began talking to Mr. Gardiner. Elizabeth felt pleased and even proud. He would know she had some relatives who caused no shame. She listened carefully to every word between the men. Every sentence showing her uncle’s intelligence, taste, or good manners filled her with pride.

They soon discussed fishing. Mr. Darcy very politely invited Mr. Gardiner to fish there whenever he wished. He offered fishing equipment and pointed out the stream’s best places. Mrs. Gardiner walked arm in arm with Elizabeth and gave her a surprised look. Elizabeth said nothing, but felt extremely pleased. The invitation must really be a kindness to her. Still, his behaviour amazed her. She constantly wondered, “Why has he changed so much? What caused it? It cannot be for me. He cannot have softened his manners for my sake. My criticism at Hunsford could not create such a change. He cannot possibly still love me.”

For some time, the women walked in front and the men behind. They went down to examine an unusual water plant, then changed places afterwards. The morning’s exercise had tired Mrs. Gardiner. Elizabeth’s arm did not provide enough support, so she preferred her husband’s. Mr. Darcy took her place beside Elizabeth, and they walked together. After a short silence, Elizabeth spoke first. She wanted him to know that she believed he was away before visiting. She said his arrival had been completely unexpected. “Your housekeeper told us you would certainly not return until tomorrow,” she added. “Before leaving Bakewell, we also understood you were not expected here immediately.” He agreed this had been true. Business with his estate manager made him arrive several hours before the rest of his group. “They will join me early tomorrow,” he continued. “Some of them already know you—Mr. Bingley and his sisters.”

Elizabeth answered only with a small bow. Her thoughts returned to their last conversation about Mr. Bingley. If Darcy’s blush meant anything, he remembered it too.

“There is one other person in the group,” he continued after a pause. “She especially wishes to know you. During your stay at Lambton, may I introduce my sister to you? Or am I asking too much?”

This request greatly surprised her. She was too surprised to know how she agreed. She immediately understood that Miss Darcy’s wish to know her came from her brother. Without considering further, this pleased her. His anger had not made him think truly badly of her.

They continued in silence, both deep in thought. Elizabeth could not feel comfortable, but she felt pleased and honoured. His wish to introduce his sister was the highest compliment. They soon walked far ahead. When they reached the carriage, Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner were one eighth of a mile behind.

Darcy invited her into the house, but she insisted she was not tired. They stood together on the lawn. They could have said much, yet the silence was very awkward. Elizabeth wanted to talk, but every subject seemed forbidden. At last, she remembered her travels. They worked hard to discuss Matlock and Dovedale. But time and her aunt both moved slowly. Elizabeth had nearly used all her patience and ideas before their private conversation ended.

When Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner arrived, Darcy strongly invited everyone inside for refreshments. They refused, and both sides parted with perfect politeness. Mr. Darcy helped the ladies into their carriage. As it drove away, Elizabeth saw him walking slowly towards the house.

Her uncle and aunt now began their comments. They both declared Darcy far better than anything they had expected.

“He behaves perfectly. He is polite and not proud,” said her uncle.

“He does have a slightly grand manner,” her aunt replied. “But it appears only in his bearing and suits him well. Like the housekeeper, I can say some people may call him proud. Yet I have seen none of it.”

“His behaviour towards us surprised me greatly. He was more than polite. He truly paid attention to us without any need. He hardly knows Elizabeth.”

“Certainly, Lizzy,” her aunt said, “he is not as handsome as Wickham. Or rather, he lacks Wickham’s expression, because his features are excellent. But why did you tell us he was so unpleasant?”

Elizabeth defended herself as well as possible. She said she liked him better in Kent than before. She had also never seen him as pleasant as he was that morning.

“But perhaps his politeness changes with his mood,” her uncle replied. “Great men often behave that way. I will not trust his fishing invitation. Tomorrow he might change his mind and order me away from his land.”

Elizabeth knew they had completely misunderstood his character, but she said nothing.

“From what we saw,” Mrs. Gardiner continued, “I would never imagine him treating anyone as cruelly as poor Wickham. He does not look unkind. In fact, his mouth looks pleasant when he speaks. His face has a dignity that suggests a good heart. But the good lady showing us the house praised him wonderfully! Sometimes I could hardly stop myself laughing aloud. I suppose he is a generous master. To a servant, that quality probably includes every virtue.”

Elizabeth now felt she must defend Darcy’s behaviour towards Wickham. She spoke as carefully as possible. Darcy’s relatives in Kent had given her a very different explanation of his actions. His character was not as bad as Hertfordshire believed. Wickham’s was also not so pleasant. To support this, she described all the financial dealings between the men. She did not name her source, but said it was completely reliable.

Mrs. Gardiner was surprised and concerned. But they were approaching places she once loved, and her memories replaced every other thought. She eagerly pointed out every interesting place to her husband. Although tired by the morning walk, she went out again immediately after dinner. She searched for former acquaintances. That evening, she enjoyed renewing friendships after many years apart.

The day’s events were too interesting for Elizabeth to pay much attention to these new friends. She could only think with wonder about Mr. Darcy’s politeness. Above all, she thought about his wish to introduce his sister.
