# Chapter LIV

As soon as they had gone, Elizabeth walked outside to recover her spirits. In other words, she wanted to think without interruption about subjects that could only lower them further. Mr. Darcy’s behaviour surprised and upset her.

“If he came only to be silent, serious, and indifferent,” she said, “why did he come at all?”

She could find no answer that pleased her.

“In London, he could still be pleasant and charming to my uncle and aunt. Why can he not be so to me? If he is afraid of me, why come here? If he no longer cares for me, why remain silent? Annoying, annoying man! I will think no more about him.”

For a short time, she kept this decision without any effort. Her sister approached and joined her with a cheerful expression. She was clearly more pleased with their visitors than Elizabeth was.

“Now that this first meeting is over,” Jane said, “I feel completely comfortable. I know my own strength, and his visits will never embarrass me again. I am glad he is dining here on Tuesday. Everyone will then see that we meet only as ordinary, indifferent acquaintances.”

“Yes, very indifferent indeed,” Elizabeth said, laughing. “Oh, Jane! Be careful.”

“My dear Lizzy, you cannot think I am weak enough to be in danger now.”

“I think you are in great danger of making him love you as much as ever.”

They did not see the gentlemen again until Tuesday. Meanwhile, Mrs. Bennet enjoyed every happy plan that Bingley’s good humour and ordinary politeness had revived during a half-hour visit.

A large party gathered at Longbourn on Tuesday. The two most eagerly expected guests arrived very early, which gave credit to their punctual habits as sportsmen. When everyone went into the dining room, Elizabeth watched Bingley closely. Would he take his usual place beside her sister? Her careful mother had the same thought, so she did not invite him to sit beside herself. He seemed uncertain when he entered. Then Jane happened to turn and smile, and the matter was settled. He sat beside her.

Elizabeth looked triumphantly towards his friend. He accepted the situation with noble calm. She might have thought Bingley had received his permission to be happy. But she saw Bingley also glance at Mr. Darcy with an expression of amused anxiety.

During dinner, Bingley’s behaviour showed his admiration for Jane. He was more careful than before, but Elizabeth felt certain of one thing. If he could act freely, both Jane and he would soon be happy. She dared not depend on this result, but watching him gave her pleasure. It gave her all the energy her spirits could manage, because she was not cheerful. Mr. Darcy sat almost as far from her as the table allowed, beside her mother. Elizabeth knew that neither of them would enjoy this position or appear at their best. She was too far away to hear them, but she saw how rarely they spoke. Whenever they did, both behaved formally and coldly. Her mother’s unfriendly manner made Elizabeth feel their debt to him even more painfully. At times, she would have given anything to tell him that some family members knew and valued his kindness.

She hoped the evening would give them some chance to be together. Surely the entire visit would not pass with only the formal greeting he gave when entering. Before the gentlemen joined them in the drawing room, Elizabeth felt anxious and restless. The time seemed so dull and tiring that she almost became rude. All her hope of enjoying the evening depended on his entrance.

“If he does not come to me then,” she thought, “I shall give him up forever.”

The gentlemen entered, and she thought he looked ready to fulfil her hopes. But sadly, the ladies had crowded closely around the table. Miss Bennet was making tea, and Elizabeth was pouring coffee. Their circle left no empty space for a chair near her. When the gentlemen approached, one girl moved even closer to Elizabeth and whispered—

“I am determined that the men shall not come and separate us. We do not want them, do we?”

Darcy walked to another part of the room. Elizabeth followed him with her eyes and envied everyone he spoke to. She could hardly remain patient while serving coffee. Then she became angry with herself for being so foolish!

“He has already been refused once! How could I foolishly expect him to love me again? Is there one man who would not object to the weakness of proposing twice to the same woman? Nothing could offend their pride more.”

However, she felt slightly encouraged when he brought back his own coffee cup. She used the opportunity to ask—

“Is your sister still at Pemberley?”

“Yes. She will remain there until Christmas.”

“And is she quite alone? Have all her friends left?”

“Mrs. Annesley is with her. The others left for Scarborough three weeks ago.”

Elizabeth could think of nothing else to say. If he wished to talk to her, perhaps he might do better. Still, he stood beside her silently for several minutes. At last, when the young lady whispered to Elizabeth again, he walked away.

After the tea things were removed, card tables were prepared, and all the ladies rose. Elizabeth hoped Darcy would soon join her. But her plans collapsed when her mother eagerly claimed him as a whist player. Within moments, he was seated with the other players. Elizabeth now expected no pleasure. They had to spend the evening at separate tables. She could only hope that his frequent looks towards her made him play as badly as she did.

Mrs. Bennet had planned to keep the two Netherfield gentlemen for supper. Unfortunately, their carriage had been ordered before everyone else’s, so she could not delay them.

“Well, girls,” she said when the family was alone, “what do you think of today? Everything went extremely well, I assure you. The dinner was as well prepared as any I have seen. The venison was perfectly roasted—and everybody said they had never seen such a fine, fat piece. The soup was fifty times better than the Lucases’ soup last week. Even Mr. Darcy admitted that the partridges were prepared extremely well. I suppose he has at least two or three French cooks. And my dear Jane, you have never looked more beautiful. Mrs. Long said so too, because I asked her. And what else do you think she said? ‘Ah! Mrs. Bennet, we shall have her at Netherfield at last!’ She really did. I think Mrs. Long is the kindest person alive. Her nieces are also very well-behaved girls, and not at all pretty. I like them enormously.”

In short, Mrs. Bennet was in excellent spirits. She had seen enough of Bingley’s behaviour to feel sure Jane would win him at last. When she was happy, her hopes for family advantages became completely unreasonable. She was quite disappointed when Bingley did not return the next day to propose.

“It has been a very pleasant day,” Miss Bennet told Elizabeth. “The guests seemed perfectly chosen and well suited to one another. I hope we often meet again.”

Elizabeth smiled.

“Lizzy, you must not smile like that. You must not suspect me. It embarrasses me. I assure you, I have learned to enjoy his conversation without wanting anything more. He is a pleasant, sensible young man. His present manners prove that he never meant to gain my affection. He simply speaks more sweetly and wishes to please everyone more strongly than any other man.”

“You are very cruel,” her sister said. “You will not let me smile, yet you constantly make me want to.”

“How difficult it is to be believed in some situations! And how impossible in others! But why do you want to convince me that I feel more than I admit?”

“I hardly know how to answer that question. We all love giving advice, though we can only teach what is not worth knowing. Forgive me. If you insist that you feel nothing, please do not make me the person who hears all your secrets.”
