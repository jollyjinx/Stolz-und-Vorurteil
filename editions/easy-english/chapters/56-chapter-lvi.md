# Chapter LVI

One morning, about a week after Bingley’s engagement to Jane, he sat with the family’s women in the dining room. Suddenly, a carriage drew their attention to the window. A four-horse carriage was crossing the lawn. It was too early for visitors, and no neighbour owned such a carriage. The horses came from a posting station. Neither the carriage nor the servant’s uniform looked familiar. Still, someone was certainly arriving. Bingley immediately convinced Miss Bennet to escape the unwelcome visitor and walk with him among the bushes. They left together. The other three continued guessing without success until the door opened and their visitor entered. It was Lady Catherine de Bourgh.

Naturally, they had all expected to be surprised. But their astonishment was greater than they had imagined. Mrs. Bennet and Kitty did not know her, yet even their surprise was less than Elizabeth’s.

She entered with an even more unfriendly manner than usual. She answered Elizabeth’s greeting only by slightly bending her head, then sat without speaking. When she entered, Elizabeth had told her mother the visitor’s name. However, the visitor had not requested an introduction.

Mrs. Bennet was amazed but proud to receive such an important guest. She welcomed her with extreme politeness. After a silent moment, the guest spoke very stiffly to Elizabeth—

“I hope you are well, Miss Bennet. I suppose that lady is your mother?”

Elizabeth briefly answered that she was.

“And I suppose that is one of your sisters?”

“Yes, madam,” Mrs. Bennet said, delighted to speak to Lady Catherine. “She is my second-youngest daughter. My youngest recently married. My eldest is walking somewhere outside with a young man who, I believe, will soon join the family.”

“Your park here is very small,” Lady Catherine said after a short silence.

“It is probably nothing compared with Rosings, my Lady. But I assure you, it is much larger than Sir William Lucas’s.”

“This sitting room must be extremely uncomfortable on summer evenings. The windows face directly west.”

Mrs. Bennet assured her that they never sat there after dinner. Then she added—

“May I ask your Ladyship whether Mr. and Mrs. Collins were well when you left?”

“Yes, very well. I saw them the night before last.”

Elizabeth now expected her to give her a letter from Charlotte. That seemed the only likely reason for the visit. But no letter appeared, and Elizabeth was completely confused.

With great politeness, Mrs. Bennet invited Lady Catherine to eat or drink something. She firmly and rather rudely refused everything. Then she rose and said to Elizabeth—

“Miss Bennet, I noticed a fairly pretty little wild garden beside your lawn. I would like to walk through it, if you will accompany me.”

“Go, my dear,” her mother cried, “and show her Ladyship the different paths. I think she will like the small garden building.”

Elizabeth obeyed. She ran to her room for her parasol, then joined her noble guest downstairs. As they crossed the hall, Lady Catherine opened the dining-parlour and drawing-room doors. After briefly examining them, she declared that the rooms looked acceptable and continued walking.

Her carriage remained at the door, and Elizabeth saw her female servant inside it. They silently followed the gravel path towards the small woods. Elizabeth would not try to talk with a woman who was behaving more rudely and unpleasantly than usual.

“How could I ever think she resembled her nephew?” Elizabeth wondered while looking at her face.

As soon as they entered the woods, Lady Catherine began—

“You cannot possibly misunderstand why I have travelled here, Miss Bennet. Your own heart and conscience must tell you why I came.”

Elizabeth looked truly astonished.

“You are mistaken, madam. I cannot explain at all why you have honoured us with this visit.”

“Miss Bennet,” she replied angrily, “you should know that nobody plays games with me. You may choose to be dishonest, but I shall not. People have always praised my honesty and directness. I will certainly speak openly about a matter this important. Two days ago, I heard a very alarming report. I was told your sister would soon make a very advantageous marriage. But I also heard that you—Miss Elizabeth Bennet—would probably soon marry my nephew, my own nephew, Mr. Darcy. I know this must be a shameful lie. I would not insult him by believing it possible. Still, I immediately decided to come here and tell you my opinion.”

“If you believed it could not possibly be true,” Elizabeth said, colouring with surprise and scorn, “why travel so far? What did your Ladyship hope to achieve?”

“I came to insist that everyone must be told the report is false.”

“Your visit to me and my family at Longbourn,” Elizabeth calmly replied, “will more likely confirm it—if such a report really exists.”

“If! Do you pretend you know nothing about it? Have you and your family not carefully spread it? Do you not know this report is everywhere?”

“I have never heard it.”

“Can you also declare that it has no truth behind it?”

“I do not claim to possess your Ladyship’s level of openness. You may ask questions that I choose not to answer.”

“This is unbearable. Miss Bennet, I demand an answer. Has he—has my nephew—asked you to marry him?”

“Your Ladyship has declared that impossible.”

“It should be impossible. It must be, while he can still think clearly. But your tricks and attractions may have made him forget his duty to himself and his family. During one foolish moment, you may have trapped him.”

“If I have, I shall be the last person to admit it.”

“Miss Bennet, do you know who I am? Nobody has ever spoken to me this way. I am nearly his closest relation in the world. I have the right to know all his most private concerns.”

“But you have no right to know mine. Such behaviour will never persuade me to explain them.”

“Understand me clearly. This marriage, which you proudly hope to make, can never happen. No, never. Mr. Darcy is engaged to my daughter. What do you say to that?”

“Only this: if he is promised to her, you have no reason to think he will propose to me.”

Lady Catherine hesitated for a moment, then replied—

“Their engagement is of a special kind. They have been intended for each other since infancy. His mother wished it, and so did I. We planned their marriage while they were babies. Now both sisters’ wishes should finally be fulfilled. Will their marriage be prevented by a young woman of lower birth? Someone unimportant and completely unconnected to the family? Do you care nothing for his friends’ wishes or his unspoken engagement to Miss de Bourgh? Have you lost every idea of proper and respectful behaviour? Did you not hear me say that he was intended for his cousin from childhood?”

“Yes, and I had heard it before. But what does it matter to me? If nothing else prevents me from marrying your nephew, his mother’s and aunt’s wishes that he marry Miss de Bourgh certainly will not. You both planned the marriage as far as you could. Its success depended on other people. If neither honour nor desire ties Mr. Darcy to his cousin, why can he not choose someone else? And if he chooses me, why may I not accept him?”

“Because honour, proper behaviour, good judgment—indeed, your own advantage—make it impossible. Yes, Miss Bennet, your advantage. If you deliberately oppose everyone’s wishes, his family and friends will ignore you. Everyone connected with him will criticize, avoid, and despise you. Your marriage will bring shame. None of us will even mention your name.”

“Those are serious troubles,” Elizabeth replied. “But Mr. Darcy’s wife must receive such extraordinary happiness from her position. Overall, she could have no reason to complain.”

“Stubborn, wilful girl! I am ashamed of you! Is this your gratitude for my kindness last spring? Do you owe me nothing for that? Let us sit down. Understand this, Miss Bennet: I came fully determined to achieve my purpose. Nobody will change my mind. I am not used to accepting another person’s foolish wishes. I am not accustomed to disappointment.”

“That makes your present situation more unfortunate, but it will not affect me.”

“Do not interrupt me! Listen silently. My daughter and nephew are perfectly suited. Through their mothers, they come from the same noble family. Through their fathers, they come from respectable, honourable, ancient families without titles. Both possess great fortunes. Every member of both families believes they should marry. And what will separate them?—the proud ambitions of a young woman without family, connections, or fortune! Can this be accepted? It must not and shall not happen! If you understood your own good, you would not leave the social position in which you were raised.”

“By marrying your nephew, I would not consider myself to be leaving that position. He is a gentleman, and I am a gentleman’s daughter. In that respect, we are equal.”

“True. You are a gentleman’s daughter. But what was your mother? Who are your uncles and aunts? Do not imagine that I know nothing about their social position.”

“Whatever my family connections may be,” Elizabeth said, “if your nephew does not object, they cannot matter to you.”

“Tell me clearly, once and for all: are you engaged to him?”

Elizabeth would not have answered merely to please Lady Catherine. But after considering for a moment, she had to say—

“I am not.”

Lady Catherine seemed pleased.

“And will you promise never to enter such an engagement?”

“I will make no such promise.”

“Miss Bennet, I am shocked and astonished. I expected a more reasonable young woman. But do not imagine I will change my position. I shall not leave until you give me the promise I require.”

“And I shall certainly never give it. You cannot frighten me into something so completely unreasonable. Your Ladyship wants Mr. Darcy to marry your daughter. But would my promise make that marriage more likely? Suppose he loves me. Would my refusal make him want to marry his cousin? Lady Catherine, your arguments for this extraordinary request are as weak as the request is unwise. You completely misunderstand me if you think such arguments can influence me. I do not know whether your nephew would approve of your interference in his life. But you certainly have no right to interfere in mine. Therefore, I must ask you to stop troubling me about this subject.”

“Do not be so quick, please. I have not finished. Besides every objection already mentioned, I have another. I know all the details of your youngest sister’s shameful escape. Her marriage was an arrangement bought with your father’s and uncle’s money. Will such a girl become my nephew’s sister? Will her husband become his brother—the son of his dead father’s estate manager? Heaven and earth!—what are you thinking? Will the spirits of Pemberley’s ancestors be polluted in this way?”

“You can have nothing more to say,” Elizabeth answered angrily. “You have insulted me in every possible way. I must return to the house.”

She rose as she spoke. Lady Catherine also stood, and they turned back. Her Ladyship was extremely angry.

“Then you care nothing for my nephew’s honour and reputation! Cold, selfish girl! Do you not understand that a connection with you must shame him before everyone?”

“Lady Catherine, I have nothing more to say. You know my opinion.”

“Then you are determined to marry him?”

“I said no such thing. I am only determined to choose whatever I believe will make me happy. Your wishes will not influence me, nor will those of anyone so completely unconnected with me.”

“Very well. You refuse to please me. You refuse to obey the demands of duty, honour, and gratitude. You are determined to ruin his reputation among his friends and make the world despise him.”

“Duty, honour, and gratitude have no claim on me in this situation,” Elizabeth replied. “My marriage to Mr. Darcy would break none of those principles. If his family resented him for marrying me, I would not worry for one moment. And the wider world would have too much good sense to join their scorn.”

“And that is your true opinion! Your final decision! Very well. Now I know how to act. Do not imagine, Miss Bennet, that you will achieve your ambition. I came to test you and hoped you would be reasonable. But believe me, I will achieve my purpose.”

Lady Catherine continued speaking this way until they reached her carriage. Then she quickly turned and added—

“I shall not say goodbye, Miss Bennet. I send no greetings to your mother. You deserve no such attention. I am deeply displeased.”

Elizabeth did not answer or invite her Ladyship back inside. She quietly entered the house alone. As she went upstairs, she heard the carriage leave. Her mother impatiently met her at the dressing-room door. She wanted to know why Lady Catherine had not returned inside to rest.

“She did not wish to,” her daughter said. “She wanted to leave.”

“She is a very impressive-looking woman! And it was wonderfully polite of her to visit us! I suppose she only came to say that the Collinses were well. She is probably travelling somewhere and passed through Meryton. So she thought she might visit you. I suppose she had nothing special to say to you, Lizzy?”

Elizabeth had to tell a small lie. She could not possibly admit what their conversation had contained.
