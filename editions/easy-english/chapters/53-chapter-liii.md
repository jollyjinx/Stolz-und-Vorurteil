# Chapter LIII

Mr. Wickham was completely satisfied with this conversation. He never troubled himself or his dear sister Elizabeth by mentioning the subject again. She was pleased that she had said enough to keep him quiet.

The day of his and Lydia’s departure soon arrived. Mrs. Bennet had to accept the separation. Her husband refused her plan for the whole family to visit Newcastle. Therefore, she would probably not see Lydia for at least a year.

“Oh, my dear Lydia,” she cried, “when will we meet again?”

“Oh, Lord! I do not know. Perhaps not for two or three years.”

“Write to me very often, my dear.”

“As often as I can. But married women never have much time for letters. My sisters can write to me. They will have nothing else to do.”

Mr. Wickham said goodbye with much more affection than his wife. He smiled, looked handsome, and said many charming things.

“He is as fine a man as I have ever seen,” said Mr. Bennet after they left. “He gives us sweet smiles and tries to charm us all. I am extremely proud of him. Even Sir William Lucas could not produce a more valuable son-in-law.”

Losing her daughter made Mrs. Bennet very low for several days.

“I often think nothing is worse than parting from one’s friends,” she said. “Without them, one feels so lonely.”

“You see, madam, this is the result of marrying a daughter,” said Elizabeth. “It should make you more satisfied that your other four daughters are single.”

“It is nothing of the kind. Lydia did not leave because she married. She left because her husband’s regiment is so far away. If it were nearer, she would not have gone so soon.”

News soon lifted the sadness caused by Lydia’s departure. Mrs. Bennet’s mind filled again with excited hope. The Netherfield housekeeper had received orders to prepare for her master. He would arrive in one or two days and shoot there for several weeks. Mrs. Bennet became extremely restless. She looked at Jane, smiled, and shook her head again and again.

“Well, well, Mr. Bingley is coming back, sister,” she said. Mrs. Philips had brought the news. “That is for the best, though I do not care about it. He means nothing to us, and I never want to see him again. Still, he may come to Netherfield if he wishes. Who knows what might happen? But it has nothing to do with us. Sister, we agreed long ago never to mention it. And are you certain he is coming?”

“You can depend on it,” replied Mrs. Philips. “Mrs. Nichols was in Meryton last night. I saw her pass and went outside to learn the truth. She said it was certainly true. He will arrive by Thursday at the latest, and probably Wednesday. She was going to the butcher to order meat for Wednesday. She also has three pairs of ducks ready to be killed.”

Miss Bennet could not hear of his arrival without changing colour. She had not mentioned his name to Elizabeth for many months. As soon as they were alone, she said:

“I saw you look at me today, Lizzy, when our aunt told us the news. I know I seemed distressed, but do not imagine a foolish reason. I was confused only because I knew everyone would look at me. I promise the news gives me neither pleasure nor pain. I am glad he comes alone because we will see less of him. I do not fear my own feelings, but I fear other people’s remarks.”

Elizabeth did not know how to understand this. If she had not seen Bingley in Derbyshire, she might believe he came only to shoot. But she still thought he loved Jane. She could not decide between two ideas. Perhaps his friend had allowed him to return. Perhaps Bingley had become brave enough to come without permission.

“Still, it is unfair,” she sometimes thought. “This poor man cannot visit a house he legally rents without causing all this guessing! I will leave him alone.”

Jane truly believed her own description of her feelings. Yet while they waited for Bingley, Elizabeth easily saw that the news affected her. Jane’s spirits were more troubled and changeable than Elizabeth had often seen them.

Their parents now returned to a subject they had warmly argued about nearly a year earlier.

“As soon as Mr. Bingley comes, my dear,” said Mrs. Bennet, “you will formally visit him, of course.”

“No, no. Last year you forced me to visit him. You promised that if I went, he would marry one of my daughters. Nothing happened, and I will not go on another foolish mission.”

His wife explained that every gentleman in the neighbourhood must pay this attention when Bingley returned to Netherfield.

“I despise that rule,” he said. “If he wants our company, let him seek it. He knows where we live. I will not spend my time chasing neighbours whenever they leave and return.”

“I only know it will be terribly rude if you do not visit. Still, that will not stop me inviting him to dinner. I am determined. We must soon invite Mrs. Long and the Gouldings too. That will make thirteen of us, leaving exactly one place for him.”

This decision comforted her and helped her bear her husband’s rudeness. Still, she hated knowing that all her neighbours might see Mr. Bingley before she did. As his arrival approached:

“I am beginning to wish he was not coming,” Jane told her sister. “Meeting him would mean nothing. I could see him without feeling anything. But I can hardly bear hearing everyone discuss it constantly. My mother means well. Yet she does not know—no one can know—how much her words hurt me. I will be happy when his stay at Netherfield ends!”

“I wish I could say something to comfort you,” replied Elizabeth, “but I cannot. You must feel this pain. Usually, one can enjoy preaching patience to someone who suffers. You already have so much patience that I cannot even do that.”

Mr. Bingley arrived. With help from the servants, Mrs. Bennet received the news as early as possible. This made her period of anxiety and bad temper as long as possible. She counted the days before she could send her invitation. She had no hope of seeing him earlier. But on his third morning in Hertfordshire, she saw him from her dressing-room window. He entered the field and rode towards the house.

She eagerly called her daughters to share her joy. Jane firmly remained at the table. To satisfy her mother, Elizabeth went to the window. She looked, saw Mr. Darcy beside him, and sat down again near Jane.

“A gentleman is with him, Mama,” said Kitty. “Who can it be?”

“Some friend or other, my dear, I suppose. I certainly do not know.”

“Oh!” replied Kitty. “He looks exactly like the man who used to come with him. Mr. What-is-his-name—the tall, proud man.”

“Good gracious! Mr. Darcy! I declare it is. Any friend of Mr. Bingley will always be welcome here. Otherwise, I must say I hate the sight of him.”

Jane looked at Elizabeth with surprise and concern. She knew little about the meetings in Derbyshire. She therefore imagined Elizabeth’s discomfort at meeting Darcy after receiving his explanatory letter. Both sisters felt uncomfortable for each other and for themselves. Their mother continued talking about disliking Mr. Darcy. She would be polite only because he was Mr. Bingley’s friend. Neither sister heard her. But Elizabeth had further worries that Jane could not guess. She had never shown Jane Mrs. Gardiner’s letter. She also had not described her changed feelings for Darcy. To Jane, he was only a man whose proposal Elizabeth had refused and whose good qualities she had undervalued. Elizabeth knew much more. Her whole family owed Darcy the greatest possible service. She cared for him with feelings as reasonable and fair as Jane’s love for Bingley, though perhaps less tender. His decision to come to Netherfield, visit Longbourn, and seek her again amazed her. She felt almost as surprised as when she first saw his changed behaviour in Derbyshire.

Colour returned to Elizabeth’s pale face and became a bright blush. For half a minute, a joyful smile shone in her eyes. She thought his love and wishes must remain unchanged. But she would not feel certain.

“First I will see how he behaves,” she told herself. “Then I may begin to hope.”

She focused closely on her sewing and tried to remain calm. She did not dare look up. As the servant approached the door, anxious curiosity made her study Jane’s face. Jane looked slightly paler than usual but calmer than Elizabeth expected. When the gentlemen entered, Jane’s colour deepened. Still, she welcomed them with reasonable ease. Her manner showed neither anger nor unnecessary eagerness to please.

Elizabeth said as little as politeness allowed and returned eagerly to her sewing. It rarely received so much attention. She dared to look at Darcy only once. He looked serious as usual. She thought he looked more like his old Hertfordshire self than the man at Pemberley. But perhaps he could not behave before her mother as he did before her aunt and uncle. The thought was painful but believable.

Elizabeth also saw Bingley for a moment. He looked both pleased and embarrassed. Mrs. Bennet received him with such eager politeness that her daughters felt ashamed. Her cold and formal politeness towards his friend made the difference worse.

Elizabeth suffered most from this unfair difference. She knew her mother owed Darcy for saving her favourite daughter from permanent disgrace.

Darcy asked how Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner were. Elizabeth could not answer without confusion. After that, he said almost nothing. He was not sitting near her, which might explain his silence. But distance had not stopped him in Derbyshire. There, he spoke to her friends when he could not speak to her. Now several minutes passed without his voice. Curiosity sometimes made her look at his face. She found him looking at Jane as often as at herself, and often at the floor. He seemed more thoughtful and less eager to please than at their last meeting. Elizabeth felt disappointed and then angry with herself for it.

“How could I expect anything else?” she thought. “But then why did he come?”

She wanted to speak to no one except him. Yet she hardly had courage to address him.

She asked about his sister but could say no more.

“It has been a long time since you went away, Mr. Bingley,” said Mrs. Bennet.

He readily agreed.

“I began to fear you would never return. People said you planned to leave this place completely at Michaelmas. I hope that is untrue. Many changes have happened since you left. Miss Lucas is married and settled, and one of my own daughters is married too. I suppose you heard about it. You must have seen it in the newspapers. It was in the ‘Times’ and the ‘Courier,’ though the notice was badly written. It only said, ‘Recently, George Wickham, Esq., to Miss Lydia Bennet.’ It said nothing about her father, her home, or anything else. My brother Gardiner wrote it. I cannot imagine why he did the job so badly. Did you see it?”

Bingley said that he had and offered his congratulations. Elizabeth did not dare look up, so she could not see Mr. Darcy’s expression.

“Having a daughter well married is certainly delightful,” continued her mother. “But, Mr. Bingley, it is hard to have her taken away. They have gone to Newcastle, far in the north. I do not know how long they will stay. His regiment is there. I suppose you heard that he left the ——shire and joined the regular army. Thank Heaven he has some friends, though perhaps fewer than he deserves.”

Elizabeth knew this remark was aimed at Mr. Darcy. She felt so deeply ashamed that she could hardly remain seated. Yet it finally forced her to speak. Nothing else had succeeded before. She asked Bingley whether he planned to stay in the country. He believed he would remain for a few weeks.

“After you shoot all your own birds, Mr. Bingley,” said her mother, “please come here. You may shoot as many as you wish on Mr. Bennet’s estate. He will be delighted to help you. He will save all the best groups of birds for you.”

Elizabeth’s misery increased at such unnecessary and interfering attention. A year earlier, they had enjoyed the same hopeful situation. If that hope returned now, she believed it would end in the same painful disappointment. At that moment, years of happiness could not repay Jane or Elizabeth for such shameful minutes.

“My greatest wish,” she thought, “is never to share company with either man again. Their society cannot give enough pleasure to repay this misery. Let me never see either of them again!”

Yet soon she felt important relief from the misery that years could not repay. Her sister’s beauty clearly renewed Bingley’s admiration. At first, he had spoken little to Jane. Every five minutes, however, he paid her more attention. He found her as beautiful, kind, and natural as last year, though less talkative. Jane wanted no one to notice any change in her. She truly believed she spoke as much as before. But her mind was so busy that she did not always notice her own silence.

When the gentlemen rose to leave, Mrs. Bennet remembered her intended politeness. She invited them to dine at Longbourn within a few days, and they accepted.

“You owe me a visit, Mr. Bingley,” she added. “When you went to London last winter, you promised to dine with us after returning. You see that I have not forgotten. I was very disappointed when you did not return and keep your promise.”

Bingley looked rather foolish after this criticism. He said that business had prevented him and that he was sorry. Then they left.

Mrs. Bennet had strongly wanted to invite them to dinner that same day. She always served good food. Still, nothing less than two courses seemed enough for a man she hoped to attract. It also seemed necessary for the appetite and pride of a man with ten thousand pounds a year.
