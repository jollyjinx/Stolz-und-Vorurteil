# Chapter LX

Elizabeth’s spirits soon became playful again, and she wanted Mr. Darcy to explain why he had ever loved her. “How did you begin?” she asked. “I understand how you continued beautifully after starting. But what caused you to start at all?”

“I cannot name the hour, place, look, or words that created the beginning. It happened too long ago. I was already in the middle before I realized I had begun.”

“You resisted my beauty early. And as for my manners—my behaviour towards you was always almost rude. I hardly ever spoke without wanting to cause you some pain. Now answer honestly: did you admire my bold rudeness?”

“I admired your lively mind.”

“You may as well call it bold rudeness immediately. It was almost the same thing. The truth is that you were tired of politeness, respect, and eager attention. You disliked women who spoke, looked, and thought only to win your approval. I awakened and interested you because I was completely unlike them. If you had not truly been good, you would have hated me. But although you worked hard to hide yourself, your feelings were always noble and fair. Deep inside, you completely despised the people who constantly tried to please you. There—I have saved you the trouble of explaining it. Considering everything, I am beginning to find it perfectly reasonable. Certainly, you knew nothing truly good about me—but nobody considers that when falling in love.”

“Was there nothing good in your loving care for Jane while she was ill at Netherfield?”

“Dearest Jane! Who could have done less for her? But certainly, call it a virtue. My good qualities are now under your protection, and you must exaggerate them as much as possible. In return, I must find opportunities to tease and argue with you whenever possible. I shall begin immediately by asking why you took so long to speak clearly. Why were you so shy when you first called and later dined here? Especially during your call, why did you look as though you did not care for me?”

“Because you were serious and silent, and you gave me no encouragement.”

“But I was embarrassed.”

“So was I.”

“You could have spoken more to me when you came to dinner.”

“A man who felt less could have done so.”

“How unfortunate that you have a sensible answer, and I am reasonable enough to accept it! But how long would you have continued if nobody helped you? When would you have spoken if I had not questioned you? My decision to thank you for helping Lydia certainly had a great effect. Perhaps too great. What happens to the lesson if our happiness comes from breaking a promise? I should not have mentioned the subject. That will never do.”

“Do not trouble yourself. The lesson remains perfectly proper. Lady Catherine’s unacceptable attempt to separate us removed all my doubts. My present happiness does not come from your eager wish to express gratitude. I did not plan to wait until you gave me an opportunity. My aunt’s information gave me hope, and I was determined to learn everything immediately.”

“Lady Catherine has been endlessly useful, which should make her happy because she loves being useful. But tell me, why did you come to Netherfield? Was it only to ride to Longbourn and feel embarrassed? Or did you hope for more serious results?”

“My real purpose was to see you and decide whether I could ever hope for your love. My stated purpose—or what I admitted to myself—was different. I wanted to discover whether your sister still loved Bingley. If she did, I would make the confession to him that I later made.”

“Will you ever have enough courage to tell Lady Catherine what is going to happen to her?”

“I am more likely to need time than courage, Elizabeth. But she should be told. If you give me some paper, I shall do it immediately.”

“If I did not have my own letter to write, I could sit beside you. Then I might admire how evenly you write, as another young lady once did. But I also have an aunt who must not be ignored any longer.”

Elizabeth had never answered Mrs. Gardiner’s long letter. She had not wanted to admit how greatly her aunt had misunderstood her closeness with Mr. Darcy. Now Elizabeth had news that she knew would be very welcome. She felt almost ashamed that her uncle and aunt had already lost three days of happiness. She immediately wrote—

“I should have thanked you earlier, my dear aunt, for your long, kind, and satisfying account of every detail. But honestly, I was too annoyed to write. You imagined more than truly existed. Now, however, imagine as much as you please. Free your imagination and let it travel wherever this subject allows. Unless you believe I am already married, you cannot be greatly mistaken. You must write again very soon and praise him far more than you did before. I thank you again and again for not going to the Lakes. How could I foolishly have wanted it? Your idea about the small horses is delightful. We shall ride around the park every day. I am the happiest person in the world. Perhaps others have said that before, but nobody had such a good reason. I am even happier than Jane. She only smiles, while I laugh. Mr. Darcy sends all the love he can spare from me. You must all come to Pemberley at Christmas. Yours,” etc.

Mr. Darcy’s letter to Lady Catherine had a different style. Mr. Bennet’s answer to Mr. Collins’s last letter differed from both.

“Dear Sir,

“I must trouble you to congratulate me once again. Elizabeth will soon become Mr. Darcy’s wife. Comfort Lady Catherine as well as you can. But in your position, I would support the nephew. He has more to offer.

“Yours sincerely,” etc.

Miss Bingley’s congratulations to her brother about his coming marriage contained every possible expression of insincere affection. She even wrote to Jane, expressed delight, and repeated all her former claims of friendship. Jane was not deceived, but she was touched. Though she did not trust her, she wrote a much kinder reply than was deserved.

Miss Darcy’s joy at receiving similar news was as sincere as her brother’s joy in sending it. Four pages were not enough for all her delight and her eager wish for her sister’s love.

Before Mr. Collins could answer or his wife could congratulate Elizabeth, the Longbourn family heard that the Collinses had come to Lucas Lodge. The reason for this sudden move soon became clear. Her nephew’s letter had made Lady Catherine extremely angry. Charlotte truly celebrated the marriage, so she wanted to escape until the storm ended. At such a time, Elizabeth sincerely enjoyed her friend’s arrival. But during their meetings, this pleasure sometimes seemed expensive. Mr. Darcy had to suffer her husband’s proud display and humble politeness. He accepted it with excellent calm. He could even listen calmly while Sir William Lucas praised him for taking the county’s brightest jewel. Sir William also hoped they would all often meet at St. James’s. If Darcy lifted his shoulders in annoyance, he waited until Sir William had disappeared.

Mrs. Philips’s vulgar manners were another and perhaps greater test of his patience. She and her sister respected Darcy too fearfully to speak with the relaxed familiarity that Bingley’s good humour encouraged. But whenever Mrs. Philips spoke, her words had to be vulgar. Her respect made her quieter but could not make her more elegant. Elizabeth did everything possible to protect Darcy from both women’s frequent attention. She always tried to keep him with herself and relatives whose conversation caused no embarrassment. Such uncomfortable feelings removed much pleasure from their engagement. Yet they also increased her hope for the future. Elizabeth joyfully expected the time when they would escape such unpleasant company. At Pemberley, they would enjoy the comfort and elegance of their own family circle.
