# Chapter XIV

During dinner, Mr. Bennet hardly spoke. After the servants left, he decided to converse with his guest. He chose a subject on which Mr. Collins would shine: his good fortune in his patroness. Lady Catherine de Bourgh showed remarkable care for his wishes and comfort. Mr. Bennet could not have chosen better. Mr. Collins praised her with great skill. The subject made him even more serious and important than usual. He had never seen such behaviour from a person of rank. Lady Catherine had shown him extraordinary friendliness and kindness toward an inferior. She had generously approved both sermons he had preached before her. She had twice invited him to dine at Rosings. Only last Saturday, she had sent for him to complete her table of four at quadrille. Many people called Lady Catherine proud, but he saw only kindness. She spoke to him as she would to any other gentleman. She allowed him to meet his neighbours and sometimes leave his parish for a week or two. She had even advised him to marry quickly if he chose wisely. Once she visited his humble parsonage and approved his changes. She kindly suggested some herself, including shelves in the upstairs cupboards.

“That is all very proper and polite,” said Mrs. Bennet. “I am sure she is a very pleasant woman. It is a pity other great ladies are not more like her. Does she live near you, sir?”

“Only a lane separates the garden of my humble home from Rosings Park, her Ladyship's residence.”

“I think you said she is a widow, sir. Does she have children?”

“She has one daughter, who will inherit Rosings and a very large property.”

“Ah,” cried Mrs. Bennet, shaking her head, “then she has more than many girls. What kind of young lady is she? Is she handsome?”

“She is a most charming young lady. Lady Catherine says Miss de Bourgh has greater true beauty than any other woman. Her face clearly shows her high birth. Unfortunately, her health is weak. It has prevented her progress in many accomplishments which she would otherwise possess. The lady who managed her education told me this and still lives with them. But Miss de Bourgh is perfectly pleasant. She often kindly drives past my humble home in her small carriage and ponies.”

“Has she been presented at court? I do not remember her name among the ladies there.”

“Sadly, her weak health keeps her away from London. As I once told Lady Catherine, this has robbed the British court of its brightest decoration. Her Ladyship seemed pleased. You may imagine how happy I am to offer small, careful compliments that ladies always enjoy. I have often told Lady Catherine that her charming daughter was born to be a duchess. The highest rank would not make her important; instead, she would make the rank more impressive. Such little comments please her Ladyship. I believe I have a special duty to offer them.”

“You judge correctly,” said Mr. Bennet. “Fortunately, you can flatter people with great care. May I ask whether these pleasing remarks come naturally at the moment or from earlier study?”

“They mainly arise from what is happening. Sometimes I entertain myself by inventing elegant compliments for ordinary occasions. However, I always try to make them sound natural.”

Mr. Bennet's hopes were completely fulfilled. His cousin was as ridiculous as expected. He listened with the greatest enjoyment while keeping a perfectly serious face. Only an occasional look at Elizabeth allowed him to share his pleasure.

By tea-time, however, Mr. Bennet had heard enough. He was glad to take his guest into the drawing room. After tea, he was equally glad to ask Mr. Collins to read aloud to the ladies. Mr. Collins immediately agreed, and someone brought a book. He saw at once that it came from a lending library and stepped back in alarm. After apologising, he declared that he never read novels. Kitty stared, and Lydia cried out. They brought other books. After some thought, he chose “Fordyce’s Sermons.” Lydia yawned when he opened it. He had read only three pages in a dull, serious voice when she interrupted him.

“Mama, do you know that my uncle Philips may dismiss Richard? If he does, Colonel Forster will employ him. My aunt told me herself on Saturday. I shall walk to Meryton tomorrow to learn more. I also want to ask when Mr. Denny returns from London.”

Her two eldest sisters ordered Lydia to be quiet. Deeply offended, Mr. Collins put down his book and said:

“I have often noticed how little young ladies care for serious books written entirely for their benefit. I admit this amazes me. Nothing can help them more than instruction. But I shall no longer trouble my young cousin.”

He turned to Mr. Bennet and offered to play backgammon against him. Mr. Bennet accepted and observed that Mr. Collins acted wisely. The girls could continue their foolish entertainment. Mrs. Bennet and her daughters politely apologised for Lydia. They promised it would not happen again if he continued reading. Mr. Collins assured them he felt no anger toward his young cousin. He would never treat her behaviour as an insult. Then he sat at another table with Mr. Bennet and prepared for backgammon.
