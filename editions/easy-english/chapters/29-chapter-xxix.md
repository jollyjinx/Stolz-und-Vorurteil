# Chapter XXIX

This invitation completed Mr. Collins’s triumph. He could show his amazed visitors the greatness of his patroness. They would also see her kindness towards him and his wife. This was exactly what he wanted. Lady Catherine had given him the opportunity so soon. He could not admire her kindness enough.

“I confess,” he said, “I would not have been surprised by an invitation on Sunday. Her Ladyship might have asked us for tea and the evening at Rosings. Knowing her friendliness, I almost expected it. But who could have expected such attention as this? Who could imagine an invitation to dinner, including the whole party, so soon after your arrival?”

“I am less surprised,” Sir William replied. “My position has taught me how truly great people behave. At court, such examples of elegant manners are not unusual.”

They spoke of almost nothing except their Rosings visit for the rest of that day and the next morning. Mr. Collins carefully told them what to expect. He feared the rooms, many servants, and splendid dinner might completely overwhelm them.

When the ladies separated to dress, he said to Elizabeth,—

“Do not worry about your clothes, my dear cousin. Lady Catherine does not expect us to dress with her and her daughter’s elegance. I advise you to wear whichever clothes are better than the rest. Nothing more is needed. Lady Catherine will not think less of you for dressing simply. She likes the differences in social rank to remain clear.”

While they dressed, he came to their doors two or three times and urged them to hurry. Lady Catherine greatly disliked waiting for dinner. His frightening descriptions of her Ladyship and her way of life terrified Maria Lucas. Maria had little experience of grand company. She feared her introduction at Rosings as much as her father had feared appearing at St. James’s.

The weather was fine, and they enjoyed the half-mile walk across the park. Every park has beautiful views, and Elizabeth found much to enjoy. However, she could not feel the excitement Mr. Collins expected. She was also not impressed when he counted the house’s front windows. Nor did she care what Sir Lewis de Bourgh had paid for all the glass.

As they climbed the steps to the entrance, Maria’s fear increased every moment. Even Sir William did not look completely calm. Elizabeth remained brave. Nothing she had heard suggested that Lady Catherine had great talent or wonderful goodness. Elizabeth believed she could calmly face the simple greatness of money and rank.

Mr. Collins joyfully pointed out the entrance hall’s fine size and perfect decoration. Servants led them through a smaller room to Lady Catherine, her daughter, and Mrs. Jenkinson. Her Ladyship kindly stood to receive them. Mrs. Collins had agreed with her husband that she would make the introductions. She did so properly, without all the apologies and thanks he would have considered necessary.

Even after appearing at St. James’s, Sir William was overwhelmed by the grandeur around him. He only had courage to bow very low and sit without speaking. His terrified daughter sat on the edge of her chair and did not know where to look. Elizabeth felt completely able to face the scene. She calmly studied the three ladies. Lady Catherine was a tall, large woman with strong features that may once have been handsome. Her expression was not friendly. Her greeting reminded the visitors of their lower rank. Silence did not make her frightening. Instead, every word had a commanding tone that showed her importance to herself. This immediately reminded Elizabeth of Mr. Wickham. By the end of the day, she believed his description of Lady Catherine had been exact.

Elizabeth studied the mother and noticed some likeness to Mr. Darcy in her face and manner. Then she looked at the daughter. She could almost share Maria’s surprise at how thin and small Miss de Bourgh was. The two ladies did not resemble each other in face or body. Miss de Bourgh was pale and sickly. Her features were not ugly but had little character. She rarely spoke except in a low voice to Mrs. Jenkinson. Nothing about Mrs. Jenkinson was remarkable. She listened closely and carefully placed a screen to protect Miss de Bourgh’s eyes.

After a few minutes, they were sent to a window to admire the view. Mr. Collins pointed out its beauties. Lady Catherine kindly told them it was much more worth seeing in summer.

The dinner was extremely fine. All the servants and silver objects Mr. Collins had promised appeared. As he had predicted, Lady Catherine asked him to sit at the far end. He looked as if life could offer nothing greater. He carved, ate, and praised everything with joyful energy. He praised each dish first, and Sir William followed. Sir William had recovered enough to repeat whatever his son-in-law said. Elizabeth wondered how Lady Catherine could bear it. However, their great admiration pleased her, and she smiled kindly. She was especially pleased when a dish was new to them. The party offered little conversation. Elizabeth was ready to speak when possible. However, she sat between Charlotte and Miss de Bourgh. Charlotte listened to Lady Catherine, and Miss de Bourgh never spoke to Elizabeth. Mrs. Jenkinson watched how little Miss de Bourgh ate. She urged her to try other dishes and feared she was unwell. Maria could not imagine speaking, and the gentlemen only ate and admired.

When the ladies returned to the drawing room, they could only listen to Lady Catherine. She spoke without stopping until coffee arrived. She gave firm opinions on every subject. Clearly, nobody usually disagreed with her judgment. She asked Charlotte detailed questions about her home and gave much advice. She explained how such a small household should manage everything. She even instructed Charlotte about her cows and chickens. Elizabeth saw that nothing was too small for Lady Catherine’s attention when she could command others. Between speeches to Mrs. Collins, she asked Maria and Elizabeth many questions, especially Elizabeth. Lady Catherine knew least about Elizabeth’s family. She told Mrs. Collins that Elizabeth was a graceful, pretty sort of girl. At different times, she asked how many sisters Elizabeth had. She asked whether they were older or younger, beautiful, educated, or likely to marry. She asked what carriage Mr. Bennet owned and what Mrs. Bennet’s family name had been. Elizabeth recognised the rudeness of these questions but answered calmly. Lady Catherine then observed,—

“Your father’s estate must pass to Mr. Collins, I believe? For your sake,” she said to Charlotte, “I am glad. Otherwise, I see no reason to prevent daughters from inheriting estates. Sir Lewis de Bourgh’s family did not think it necessary. Do you play and sing, Miss Bennet?”

“A little.”

“Oh, then we will be happy to hear you one day. Our instrument is excellent, probably better than— You shall try it sometime. Do your sisters play and sing?”

“One of them does.”

“Why did you not all learn? You should all have learned. All the Miss Webbs play, and their father has less income than yours. Do you draw?”

“No, not at all.”

“What, none of you?”

“Not one.”

“That is very strange. But I suppose you had no opportunity. Your mother should have taken you to London every spring for teachers.”

“My mother would not object, but my father hates London.”

“Has your governess left you?”

“We never had a governess.”

“No governess! How was that possible? Five daughters raised at home without one! I never heard of such a thing. Your mother must have worked like a servant over your education.”

Elizabeth could hardly prevent a smile as she assured Lady Catherine that this had not happened.

“Then who taught you? Who looked after your studies? Without a governess, you must have been neglected.”

“Compared with some families, I believe we were. But those who wanted to learn always had the means. We were encouraged to read and had all the teachers we needed. Those who chose to be lazy certainly could be.”

“Yes, no doubt. But a governess prevents that. If I had known your mother, I would have strongly advised her to employ one. Education requires steady, regular teaching, and only a governess can provide it. It is wonderful how many families I have helped in that way. Through me, four of Mrs. Jenkinson’s nieces have excellent positions. Only recently, I recommended another young woman after someone mentioned her by chance. The family is delighted with her. Mrs. Collins, did I tell you Lady Metcalfe visited yesterday to thank me? She finds Miss Pope a treasure. ‘Lady Catherine,’ she said, ‘you have given me a treasure.’ Have any of your younger sisters entered society, Miss Bennet?”

“Yes, ma’am, all of them.”

“All! What, all five at once? Very strange! And you are only the second daughter. The younger ones entered society before the older ones married! Your younger sisters must be very young?”

“Yes, my youngest is not 16. Perhaps she is rather young for much society. But truly, ma’am, it would be hard on younger sisters to keep them from society and pleasure. Their older sisters may lack the chance or wish to marry early. The youngest has as much right to youth’s pleasures as the oldest. Keeping her back for such a reason would not encourage sisterly love or a generous mind.”

“Upon my word,” said her Ladyship, “you give very firm opinions for someone so young. What is your age?”

“With three younger sisters grown up,” Elizabeth replied with a smile, “your Ladyship can hardly expect me to admit it.”

Lady Catherine seemed astonished at not receiving a direct answer. Elizabeth suspected she was the first person who had ever joked with such grand rudeness.

“You cannot be more than 20, I am sure. Therefore, you need not hide your age.”

“I am not 21.”

After the gentlemen joined them and tea ended, servants placed the card tables. Lady Catherine, Sir William, and Mr. and Mrs. Collins played quadrille. Miss de Bourgh chose cassino, so the two girls helped Mrs. Jenkinson complete her group. Their table was extremely dull. Almost every word concerned the game. Mrs. Jenkinson sometimes feared Miss de Bourgh was too hot or cold. She also worried that she had too much or too little light. Much more happened at the other table. Lady Catherine usually spoke, pointing out the others’ mistakes or telling stories about herself. Mr. Collins agreed with everything she said. He thanked her for every counter he won and apologised if he won too many. Sir William said little. He was filling his memory with grand names and stories.

When Lady Catherine and her daughter had played long enough, the games ended. Lady Catherine offered Mrs. Collins a carriage. Mrs. Collins gratefully accepted, and it was ordered at once. Everyone gathered around the fire to hear Lady Catherine decide the next day’s weather. The carriage arrived and interrupted her instructions. Mr. Collins gave many speeches of thanks, and Sir William made just as many bows. Then they left. As soon as they drove away, Mr. Collins asked Elizabeth for her opinion of Rosings. For Charlotte’s sake, Elizabeth made it more favourable than she truly felt. Her praise cost her some effort but could not satisfy Mr. Collins. He soon had to praise her Ladyship himself.
