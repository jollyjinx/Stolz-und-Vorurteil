# Chapter XXXII

The next morning, Elizabeth sat alone and wrote to Jane. Mrs. Collins and Maria had gone into the village on business. A sudden ring at the door startled her, because it clearly announced a visitor. She had heard no carriage and thought it might be Lady Catherine. Elizabeth began hiding her half-finished letter to avoid rude questions. Then the door opened. To her great surprise, Mr. Darcy—and Mr. Darcy alone—entered.

He also seemed surprised to find her alone. He apologised for entering and explained his mistake. He had understood that all the ladies were at home.

They sat down. After Elizabeth asked about Rosings, the conversation nearly fell into complete silence. She had to think of something. She remembered their last meeting in Hertfordshire and felt curious about their sudden departure. She therefore observed,—

“You all left Netherfield very suddenly last November, Mr. Darcy! Mr. Bingley must have enjoyed the surprise of seeing you follow him so soon. If I remember correctly, he left only the day before. I hope he and his sisters were well when you left London?”

“Perfectly well, thank you.”

She saw that he would give no further answer. After a short pause, she added,—

“I believe I have heard that Mr. Bingley has little intention of ever returning to Netherfield?”

“I have never heard him say so. However, he may spend very little time there in future. He has many friends and is at an age when friends and engagements continually increase.”

“If he plans to spend little time at Netherfield, he should give it up completely. That would be better for the neighbourhood, because a settled family might come. But perhaps Mr. Bingley rented the house for himself, not for the neighbourhood. We must expect him to keep or leave it for the same reason.”

“I would not be surprised,” said Darcy, “if he gave it up when a suitable property became available to buy.”

Elizabeth did not answer. She feared continuing to discuss his friend. Since she had nothing else to say, he could now have the trouble of finding a subject.

He understood and soon said, “This seems a very comfortable house. I believe Lady Catherine improved it greatly when Mr. Collins first came to Hunsford.”

“I believe she did. I am sure she could not give her kindness to a more grateful person.”

“Mr. Collins seems very fortunate in his choice of a wife.”

“Yes, indeed. His friends may celebrate his meeting one of the few sensible women who would accept him. She is also one of the few who could make him happy. My friend has excellent understanding. Still, I am not sure that marrying Mr. Collins was her wisest action. However, she seems completely happy. As a practical choice, it is certainly a very good marriage for her.”

“It must please her to live so near her own family and friends.”

“Do you call this near? It is almost 50 miles.”

“What are 50 miles of good road? It is little more than half a day’s journey. Yes, I call it very near.”

“I would never call the distance an advantage of the marriage,” cried Elizabeth. “I would never say Mrs. Collins lives near her family.”

“That proves your own love of Hertfordshire. I suppose anything beyond Longbourn’s immediate neighbourhood would seem far.”

He smiled as he spoke, and Elizabeth thought she understood. He must believe she was thinking about Jane and Netherfield. She blushed and answered,—

“I do not mean that a woman cannot live too near her family. Near and far depend on many different circumstances. If people are rich enough to ignore travel costs, distance causes no problem. But that is not true here. Mr. and Mrs. Collins have a comfortable income, but cannot travel frequently. My friend would only call herself near her family if she lived less than half this distance away.”

Mr. Darcy moved his chair a little closer and said, “You have no right to love one place so strongly. You cannot have spent your whole life at Longbourn.”

Elizabeth looked surprised. His feelings seemed to change. He moved his chair back and took a newspaper from the table. Looking over it, he asked in a colder voice,—

“Do you like Kent?”

They had a short, calm conversation about the countryside. Charlotte and her sister soon ended it by returning from their walk. Finding them alone together surprised both. Mr. Darcy explained the mistake that had brought him in while Miss Bennet was alone. He remained a few minutes longer, speaking little to anyone, and then left.

“What can this mean?” Charlotte asked as soon as he was gone. “My dear Eliza, he must love you. Otherwise, he would never visit us in this informal way.”

However, Elizabeth described his silence, which made Charlotte’s idea seem unlikely even to her. They considered various explanations and found only one. He probably visited because he could find nothing else to do at this time of year. All outdoor sports had ended. Indoors, there were only Lady Catherine, books, and a billiard table. Gentlemen could not always remain inside. From then on, the two cousins walked to the Parsonage almost every day. Perhaps the house was close, the walk pleasant, or its residents agreeable. They came at different morning hours, alone, together, or sometimes with their aunt. Everyone understood that Colonel Fitzwilliam enjoyed their company. Naturally, this made them like him even more. Elizabeth enjoyed his company and noticed his clear admiration. Both reminded her of her former favourite, George Wickham. Colonel Fitzwilliam’s manners had less charming softness. However, Elizabeth believed his mind might be better informed.

Mr. Darcy’s frequent visits were harder to understand. He could not come for company. He often sat silently for ten minutes. When he spoke, he seemed forced by good manners rather than moved by pleasure. He rarely looked lively. Mrs. Collins did not understand him. Colonel Fitzwilliam sometimes laughed at his cousin’s dullness there. This proved Darcy usually behaved differently, though Charlotte did not know that herself. She wished to believe love caused the change and that Eliza was its object. Charlotte seriously tried to discover the truth. She watched him at Rosings and during every Hunsford visit, but had little success. He certainly looked at Elizabeth a great deal. However, the meaning of his look was uncertain. His gaze was serious and steady. Charlotte often doubted that it showed much admiration. Sometimes, it seemed to show only wandering thoughts.

Once or twice, Charlotte suggested that he might admire Elizabeth. Elizabeth always laughed at the idea. Mrs. Collins did not press the subject, because false hope might end in disappointment. Charlotte had no doubt about one thing. If Elizabeth believed she could control Mr. Darcy’s heart, all her dislike of him would disappear.

In her kind plans for Elizabeth, Charlotte sometimes imagined her marrying Colonel Fitzwilliam. He was easily the most pleasant man. He clearly admired her, and his social position was excellent. However, these advantages had one weakness. Mr. Darcy controlled valuable church positions, while his cousin controlled none at all.
