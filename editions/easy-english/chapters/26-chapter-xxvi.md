# Chapter XXVI

Mrs. Gardiner found the first good chance to speak privately to Elizabeth. She gave her warning kindly and directly. After honestly explaining her opinion, she continued:—

“You are too sensible, Lizzy, to fall in love merely because someone warns you not to. Therefore, I am not afraid to speak openly. Seriously, you should be careful. Do not involve yourself or encourage his love. Without money, such a relationship would be very unwise. I have nothing against him, because he is a most interesting young man. If he had the fortune he deserved, you could hardly choose better. But as matters stand, you must control your imagination. You have good sense, and we all expect you to use it. Your father trusts your judgment and good behaviour. You must not disappoint him.”

“My dear aunt, you are truly serious.”

“Yes, and I hope to make you serious too.”

“Then you need not worry. I will take care of myself and Mr. Wickham too. If I can prevent it, he will not fall in love with me.”

“Elizabeth, you are not serious now.”

“I beg your pardon. I will try again. At present, I am not in love with Mr. Wickham. No, I certainly am not. But he is easily the most pleasant man I have ever seen. If he becomes truly attached to me—I believe it would be better if he did not. I understand the danger. Oh, that terrible Mr. Darcy! My father’s good opinion is my greatest honour. I would be miserable to lose it. However, my father likes Mr. Wickham. In short, my dear aunt, I would hate to make any of you unhappy. But every day, young people become engaged without enough money because they love each other. If I face the same temptation, how can I promise to be wiser than everyone else? How can I even know that resisting would be wiser? I can only promise not to hurry. I will not quickly believe I am his first choice. When I am with him, I will not make wishes. In short, I will do my best.”

“Perhaps you should discourage him from coming here so often. At least, do not remind your mother to invite him.”

“As I did the other day,” said Elizabeth with a knowing smile. “Very true; avoiding that would be wise. But do not imagine he is usually here this often. He received so many invitations this week because of you. You know my mother believes her visitors need constant company. But truly, I give you my word. I will try to do what seems wisest. I hope you are now satisfied.”

Her aunt assured her that she was. Elizabeth thanked her for the kindness of her advice. Then they parted—a wonderful example of such advice being given without causing anger.

Mr. Collins returned to Hertfordshire soon after the Gardiners and Jane left it. He stayed with the Lucases, so his arrival caused Mrs. Bennet little trouble. His wedding was now close. At last, she had accepted it as unavoidable. She even repeatedly said in an unfriendly voice that she “hoped they might be happy.” The wedding was planned for Thursday. On Wednesday, Miss Lucas made her farewell visit. When Charlotte rose to leave, Mrs. Bennet offered cold and unwilling good wishes. Elizabeth felt ashamed of her mother and was truly moved herself. She followed Charlotte from the room. As they went downstairs together, Charlotte said,—

“I shall depend on hearing from you very often, Eliza.”

“You certainly shall.”

“I have another favour to ask. Will you come and see me?”

“I hope we will often meet in Hertfordshire.”

“I am not likely to leave Kent for some time. You must promise to visit me at Hunsford.”

Elizabeth could not refuse, although she expected little pleasure from the visit.

“My father and Maria will come to me in March,” Charlotte added. “I hope you will join them. Truly, Eliza, you will be as welcome as either of them.”

The wedding took place. The bride and bridegroom left for Kent directly from the church. Everyone had the usual amount to say and hear about it. Elizabeth soon received a letter from her friend. Their letters remained as regular and frequent as before, but could no longer be as open. Elizabeth could not write without feeling that the comfort of their close friendship was over. She would not become a careless correspondent. However, she wrote for the sake of their past friendship, not their present one. Charlotte’s first letters caused much interest. Elizabeth wanted to know how Charlotte described her new home. She wondered what Charlotte thought of Lady Catherine and how happy she dared to call herself. The letters were exactly what Elizabeth expected. Charlotte wrote cheerfully and seemed to have every comfort. She mentioned nothing she could not praise. She liked the house, furniture, neighbourhood, and roads. Lady Catherine behaved with great friendliness and kindness. It was Mr. Collins’s picture of Hunsford and Rosings, made calmer and more sensible. Elizabeth realised she must wait for her own visit to learn the rest.

Jane had already written a few lines to announce their safe arrival in London. Elizabeth hoped her next letter would contain some news about the Bingleys.

Her impatient wait for this second letter received the usual reward of impatience. Jane spent a week in London without seeing or hearing from Caroline. However, she supposed her last letter from Longbourn had somehow been lost.

“My aunt,” she continued, “is going to that part of London tomorrow. I will use the chance to call in Grosvenor Street.”

She wrote again after seeing Miss Bingley. “Caroline did not seem cheerful,” she wrote, “but she was very glad to see me. She blamed me for not telling her I was coming to London. Therefore, I was right: my last letter never reached her. Of course, I asked about their brother. He was well but so busy with Mr. Darcy that they hardly saw him. Miss Darcy was expected for dinner, and I wish I could meet her. My visit was short because Caroline and Mrs. Hurst were going out. I expect I will soon see them here.”

Elizabeth shook her head over this letter. It convinced her that only chance would tell Mr. Bingley that Jane was in London.

Four weeks passed, and Jane saw nothing of him. She tried to persuade herself that she did not regret it. However, she could no longer ignore Miss Bingley’s neglect. For two weeks, she waited at home every morning. Every evening, she invented a new excuse for Caroline. At last, Caroline came. The visit was short, and her manner had greatly changed. Jane could no longer deceive herself. Her letter to Elizabeth showed what she felt:—

“My dearest Lizzy will not celebrate her better judgment at my expense. I now admit that I was completely deceived about Miss Bingley’s affection for me. My dear sister, events have proved you right. Still, please do not call me stubborn when I say my trust was natural. Her former behaviour justified my confidence as much as it caused your suspicion. I cannot understand why she wanted my close friendship. If the same events happened again, I am sure I would be deceived again. Caroline did not return my visit until yesterday. Meanwhile, I received no note or line from her. When she came, she clearly felt no pleasure in it. She gave a brief, formal apology for not visiting sooner. She did not say she wished to see me again. Her whole manner was so changed that I decided to end the friendship after she left. I pity her, although I must also blame her. She was wrong to choose me for friendship as she did. I can safely say that every step towards closeness came from her. But I pity her because she must know she has acted wrongly. I am sure anxiety about her brother caused it. I need not explain further. We know such anxiety is unnecessary. Still, if she feels it, that easily explains her behaviour towards me. Her brother deserves her love, so concern for him is natural and kind. However, I wonder why she fears anything now. If he had cared for me, we would have met long ago. I am certain he knows I am in London because of something she said. Still, she speaks as though she wants to persuade herself that he truly prefers Miss Darcy. I cannot understand it. If I were not afraid of judging too harshly, I might almost call all this dishonest. I will try to send away every painful thought. I will think only of your love and our dear aunt and uncle’s constant kindness. Write to me very soon. Miss Bingley spoke about her brother never returning to Netherfield and giving up the house. She was not certain, so we should not mention it. I am extremely glad to receive pleasant news from our Hunsford friends. Please visit them with Sir William and Maria. I am sure you will be very comfortable there.

“Yours, etc.”

This letter caused Elizabeth some pain. Then her spirits improved when she considered one comfort. At least Miss Bingley would never deceive Jane again. Every hope concerning her brother was now completely over. Elizabeth did not even want him to renew his attention. Each review made her think less of his character. She seriously hoped he would soon marry Mr. Darcy’s sister. This would punish him and might help Jane. According to Wickham, Miss Darcy would make him deeply regret what he had lost.

At about this time, Mrs. Gardiner reminded Elizabeth of her promise about Mr. Wickham. She asked for news. Elizabeth had information that pleased her aunt more than herself. His apparent preference had faded, and his attentions had ended. He now admired someone else. Elizabeth noticed it all but felt little pain when writing about it. Her heart had only been slightly touched. Her pride was satisfied by one belief. Without their lack of money, she would have been his only choice. The young lady’s sudden ten thousand pounds was her most remarkable attraction. Elizabeth judged Wickham less sharply than she had judged Charlotte. She did not blame his wish for financial security. Nothing could be more natural. She imagined it had cost him some effort to give her up. Still, she accepted it as a wise and desirable choice for both. She could sincerely wish him happiness.

Elizabeth admitted all this to Mrs. Gardiner. After describing the circumstances, she continued:—“I am now certain, my dear aunt, that I was never deeply in love. If I had felt that pure and improving passion, I would now hate his name. I would wish every kind of evil upon him. Instead, I still feel friendly towards him and fair towards Miss King. I cannot discover any hatred for her. I am even willing to believe she is a very good sort of girl. There can be no love in all this. My careful behaviour has worked. I would certainly seem more interesting if I were madly in love. Still, I do not regret being less important. Importance can cost too much. Kitty and Lydia feel his change much more deeply. They are young and do not yet understand a painful truth. Handsome young men need money to live on just as much as plain ones do.”
