# Chapter XXXIII

More than once, Elizabeth unexpectedly met Mr. Darcy while walking inside the park. She found it extremely annoying that chance brought him where nobody else came. To prevent another meeting, she told him at once that this was her favourite walk. It was therefore very strange when it happened a second time—and even a third! It seemed like deliberate unkindness or a punishment he chose for himself. These meetings did not end after a few formal questions and an awkward pause. He actually turned back and walked with her. He never said much, and she made little effort to speak or listen. During their third meeting, however, she noticed several strange, unconnected questions. He asked whether she enjoyed Hunsford and liked walking alone. He also asked whether Mr. and Mrs. Collins were happy. When speaking of Rosings, he said she did not yet understand the household fully. His words seemed to suggest she would stay at the Parsonage whenever she returned to Kent. Could he be thinking about Colonel Fitzwilliam? If Darcy meant anything, Elizabeth supposed he must be suggesting some future event involving the Colonel. The thought worried her slightly. She was glad when they reached the gate opposite the Parsonage.

One day, Elizabeth walked while reading Jane’s latest letter again. She studied passages showing that Jane had not written cheerfully. When she looked up, it was not Mr. Darcy who surprised her. Colonel Fitzwilliam was approaching. She immediately put away the letter, forced a smile, and said,—

“I did not know you ever walked this way.”

“I have been walking around the park,” he replied, “as I usually do every year. I planned to finish with a formal call at the Parsonage. Are you going much farther?”

“No, I was about to turn back.”

She turned, and they walked towards the Parsonage together.

“Are you certainly leaving Kent on Saturday?” she asked.

“Yes—unless Darcy delays it again. But I must follow his wishes. He arranges everything as he pleases.”

“If he cannot please himself with the arrangement, he at least enjoys having the choice. Nobody seems to enjoy doing exactly as he likes more than Mr. Darcy.”

“He certainly likes to have his own way,” Colonel Fitzwilliam replied. “But we all do. He only has better means than most people, because he is rich and they are poor. I understand this well. A younger son must learn self-denial and dependence.”

“In my opinion, an earl’s younger son can know very little of either. Seriously, when have you ever had to deny yourself or depend on others? When has lack of money prevented you from travelling or buying something you wanted?”

“Those questions strike close—and perhaps I have suffered few such difficulties. But in more important matters, I may suffer from lacking money. Younger sons cannot marry wherever they wish.”

“Unless they happen to like rich women, which I believe they often do.”

“Our expensive habits make us too dependent. Few men in my social position can marry without considering money.”

“Does he mean me?” Elizabeth thought, and the idea made her blush. Recovering herself, she said brightly, “What is the usual price of an earl’s younger son? Unless his older brother is very sickly, I suppose he would not ask more than fifty thousand pounds.”

He answered in the same joking style, and they dropped the subject. Elizabeth wanted to prevent a silence. It might make him think his words had affected her. Soon she said,—

“I imagine your cousin brought you mainly so he could command somebody. I wonder why he does not marry and gain that advantage forever. Perhaps his sister serves well enough for now. Since she is entirely in his care, he may do as he likes with her.”

“No,” said Colonel Fitzwilliam, “he must share that advantage with me. I am also Miss Darcy’s guardian.”

“Are you really? And what kind of guardian are you? Does the young lady cause much trouble? Girls of her age can sometimes be difficult. If she has the true Darcy character, she may enjoy having her own way.”

As Elizabeth spoke, she noticed him watching her carefully. He immediately asked why she expected Miss Darcy to cause them trouble. His manner convinced Elizabeth that she had somehow come close to the truth. She quickly replied,—

“You need not be frightened. I have never heard anything bad about her. I expect she is one of the easiest people in the world to guide. Some ladies I know greatly admire her—Mrs. Hurst and Miss Bingley. I believe you said you know them.”

“I know them a little. Their brother is a pleasant, gentlemanlike man and a close friend of Darcy’s.”

“Oh yes,” Elizabeth said dryly. “Mr. Darcy is unusually kind to Mr. Bingley and takes great care of him.”

“Takes care of him! Yes, I truly believe Darcy helps where Bingley most needs help. From something Darcy told me during our journey, I think Bingley owes him a great deal. But I should apologise. I have no right to suppose he meant Bingley. It is only my guess.”

“What do you mean?”

“It concerns something Darcy would not want generally known. If the lady’s family heard it, the situation would be unpleasant.”

“You may depend upon me not to mention it.”

“Remember, I have little reason to think he meant Bingley. Darcy only told me this: he had recently saved a friend from a very unwise marriage. He congratulated himself on doing so but gave no names or details. I suspected Bingley because he might easily enter such trouble. I also knew they had spent the whole summer together.”

“Did Mr. Darcy explain his reasons for interfering?”

“I understood there were some very serious objections to the lady.”

“What tricks did he use to separate them?”

“He did not tell me about his tricks,” Fitzwilliam said with a smile. “He only told me what I have now told you.”

Elizabeth did not answer and walked on, her heart swelling with anger. Fitzwilliam watched her briefly and asked why she looked so thoughtful.

“I am thinking about what you told me,” she said. “I dislike your cousin’s behaviour. Why should he be the judge?”

“You would call his interference unwanted?”

“I see no right for Mr. Darcy to judge whether his friend’s love was proper. Why should his opinion alone decide how his friend should be happy? But,” she continued, controlling herself, “we know none of the details. It is unfair to condemn him. There was probably little true affection involved.”

“That is a natural guess,” said Fitzwilliam, “but it badly reduces the honour of my cousin’s victory.”

He spoke jokingly, but his words seemed to describe Mr. Darcy exactly. Elizabeth did not trust herself to answer. She suddenly changed the subject and discussed ordinary things until they reached the Parsonage. After their visitor left, she shut herself in her room. There she could consider everything without interruption. Colonel Fitzwilliam must have meant people connected with her. There could not be two other men over whom Mr. Darcy had such complete influence. Elizabeth had never doubted Darcy’s part in separating Mr. Bingley and Jane. However, she had always considered Miss Bingley the chief planner. If Darcy’s pride had not deceived him, he was the cause. His pride and changing wishes had caused all Jane’s past and present suffering. For a time, he had destroyed every hope of happiness for the world’s most loving and generous heart. Nobody could know how long the harm might last.

“There were some very serious objections to the lady,” Colonel Fitzwilliam had said. These objections were probably Jane’s two uncles. One was a country lawyer, and the other worked in trade in London.

“There can be no objection to Jane herself!” Elizabeth cried. “She is completely lovely and good! Her understanding is excellent, her mind is educated, and her manners charm everyone. Nothing can be said against my father either. He has some unusual habits, but he has abilities Mr. Darcy must respect. My father also deserves a respect Darcy may never reach.” Thinking of her mother weakened Elizabeth’s confidence. Still, she refused to believe Mr. Darcy cared greatly about those objections. His pride would suffer more from the low social standing of Bingley’s new relations than from their poor sense. Elizabeth finally decided that two things had guided Darcy. He had the worst kind of pride and wanted to keep Mr. Bingley available for his sister.

Her anger and tears caused a headache that grew much worse towards evening. She also did not want to see Mr. Darcy. Therefore, she decided not to join her cousins for tea at Rosings. Mrs. Collins saw that Elizabeth was truly unwell and did not urge her. She also tried to stop her husband from doing so. However, Mr. Collins could not hide one fear. Lady Catherine might be displeased that Elizabeth stayed home.
