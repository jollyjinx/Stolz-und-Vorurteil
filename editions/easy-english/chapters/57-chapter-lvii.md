# Chapter LVII

This extraordinary visit had deeply upset Elizabeth, and she could not easily recover. For many hours, she could hardly stop thinking about it. Lady Catherine had truly travelled from Rosings only to end Elizabeth’s supposed engagement to Mr. Darcy. Certainly, it was a reasonable plan! But Elizabeth could not imagine where the report of their engagement had begun. Then she remembered that Darcy was Bingley’s close friend, while she was Jane’s sister. The expected marriage made everyone eager to imagine another one. These facts had been enough to create the idea. Elizabeth herself knew that Jane’s marriage would bring Darcy and her together more often. Her neighbours at Lucas Lodge had therefore called something immediate and almost certain that Elizabeth considered a future possibility. Through their contact with the Collinses, Elizabeth believed, their report had reached Lady Catherine.

Still, Lady Catherine’s words made Elizabeth uneasy about the possible results of her continued interference. She had firmly promised to prevent the marriage. Elizabeth therefore thought she must plan to speak to her nephew. How would Darcy react to the same description of every problem connected with marrying Elizabeth? She could not guess. She did not know exactly how much he loved his aunt or trusted her judgment. Naturally, however, he probably respected her opinion far more than Elizabeth did. His aunt would list the troubles of marrying someone whose close family ranked so far below his own. This would attack his weakest point. Because of his ideas about dignity, Darcy might find good sense and strong reasoning in arguments that Elizabeth considered weak and ridiculous.

Perhaps he had already been uncertain about what to do, as often seemed likely. The advice and requests of such a close relative might remove every doubt. Then he could immediately choose the happiness that came from keeping his dignity perfectly safe. If so, he would not return. Lady Catherine might meet him while passing through London, and he would cancel his promise to visit Bingley at Netherfield again.

“Therefore, if his friend receives an excuse within a few days,” she thought, “I shall understand it. Then I shall give up every hope and every wish that he remains constant. He might have gained my affection and my hand. If he prefers simply to regret losing me, I shall soon stop regretting him.”

The rest of the family was extremely surprised to learn their visitor’s identity. However, they kindly accepted the same explanation that had satisfied Mrs. Bennet’s curiosity. Elizabeth therefore escaped much teasing about the subject.

The next morning, while she was going downstairs, she met her father. He came from the library holding a letter.

“Lizzy,” he said, “I was looking for you. Come into my room.”

She followed him. The letter in his hand increased her curiosity about what he wanted to say. Suddenly, she thought it might be from Lady Catherine. She anxiously imagined all the explanations that would follow.

She followed her father to the fireplace, and they both sat. Then he said—

“This morning, I received a letter that has surprised me greatly. It mainly concerns you, so you should know its contents. I did not realize that two of my daughters were nearly married. Let me congratulate you on winning an extremely important man.”

Elizabeth’s cheeks immediately became red. She was suddenly certain the letter came from the nephew, not the aunt. She did not know whether to feel pleased that he had explained himself or offended that he had not written directly to her. Then her father continued—

“You look as if you know something. Young ladies understand such matters very well. But even your intelligence cannot discover your admirer’s name. This letter is from Mr. Collins.”

“From Mr. Collins! What can he have to say?”

“Something perfectly relevant, naturally. He begins by congratulating me on my eldest daughter’s coming marriage. Apparently, some kind, gossiping Lucases told him about it. I shall not test your patience by reading that part. This is what concerns you:—‘After giving you Mrs. Collins’s and my sincere congratulations on this happy event, let me offer a brief warning about another. The same people have informed us of it. People believe your daughter Elizabeth will not keep the name Bennet for long after her eldest sister gives it up. The man chosen to share her life may reasonably be considered one of this country’s most important persons.’ Can you guess whom he means, Lizzy? ‘This young gentleman possesses everything that a human heart could especially desire—great property, noble relatives, and extensive influence. Yet despite these advantages, I must warn my cousin Elizabeth and you about the dangers of too quickly accepting his proposal. Naturally, you will wish to accept it immediately.’ Do you know who this gentleman is, Lizzy? Now he finally reveals it. ‘My reason for warning you is this:—We have reason to believe that his aunt, Lady Catherine de Bourgh, does not approve of the marriage.’ You see, the man is Mr. Darcy! Now, Lizzy, I think I have surprised you. Could he or the Lucases have chosen anyone we know who more clearly proves their story false? Mr. Darcy sees women only to find their faults, and he has probably never looked at you! It is excellent!”

Elizabeth tried to join her father’s joke but could only force one unwilling smile. His humour had never been directed in a way that pleased her less.

“Does this not amuse you?”

“Oh, yes. Please continue reading.”

“‘When I mentioned this likely marriage to her Ladyship last night, she kindly expressed her feelings as usual. It became clear that she would never approve what she called such a shameful marriage. She has certain objections to my cousin’s family. I considered it my duty to inform my cousin immediately. Then she and her noble admirer may understand the situation and not hurry into an improperly approved marriage.’ Mr. Collins also adds, ‘I am truly delighted that my cousin Lydia’s sad affair has been hidden so successfully. I only regret that everyone knows they lived together before marrying. Still, I must perform the duties of my position. I must express my astonishment that you welcomed the young couple immediately after their marriage. This encouraged immoral behaviour. If I had been Longbourn’s parish priest, I would have opposed it very strongly. As a Christian, you should certainly forgive them. But you should never see them or allow their names to be spoken before you.’ That is his idea of Christian forgiveness! The rest only concerns dear Charlotte’s condition and his expectation of a young child. But Lizzy, you look as though you do not enjoy this. Surely you will not become foolishly sensitive and pretend an empty report offends you. Why do we live except to entertain our neighbours and then laugh at them in return?”

“Oh,” Elizabeth cried, “I am extremely amused. But it is so strange!”

“Yes, that makes it amusing. Any other man would have made the story uninteresting. But his complete indifference and your obvious dislike make it wonderfully ridiculous! I hate writing, but nothing could make me give up Mr. Collins’s letters. When I read one, I even prefer him to Wickham. Yet I greatly admire my son-in-law’s boldness and hypocrisy. And Lizzy, what did Lady Catherine say about this report? Did she visit to refuse her approval?”

His daughter answered only with a laugh. Her father had asked without any suspicion, so his repeated question did not trouble her. Elizabeth had never found it harder to hide her true feelings. She had to laugh when she wished to cry. Her father’s comment about Mr. Darcy’s indifference had painfully embarrassed her. She could only wonder how he had understood so little—or fear that perhaps she herself had imagined too much.
