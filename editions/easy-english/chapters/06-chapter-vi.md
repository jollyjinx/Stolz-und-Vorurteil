# Chapter VI

The Longbourn ladies soon made a formal visit to the ladies at Netherfield. The Netherfield ladies properly returned it. Miss Bennet's pleasing manners slowly won Mrs. Hurst and Miss Bingley's approval. They found Mrs. Bennet unbearable and the younger girls not worth speaking to. However, they wished to know the two eldest daughters better. Jane received this attention with great pleasure. Elizabeth still saw pride in their treatment of nearly everyone, including Jane. She could not like them. Still, their limited kindness to Jane had some value. It probably came from their brother's admiration. Whenever they met, Bingley's admiration was clear. Elizabeth also saw that Jane's early preference for him was growing into deep love. She was pleased that most people would probably not notice. Jane felt strongly, but her calm temper and steady cheerfulness hid it from nosy observers. Elizabeth mentioned this to her friend Miss Lucas.

“Perhaps it is pleasant to hide your feelings from the public,” Charlotte replied. “But such great caution can be a disadvantage. If a woman hides her love equally well from the man, she may lose him. Then knowing that everyone else was equally ignorant will bring little comfort. Almost every attachment includes gratitude or vanity, so love should not be left entirely alone. Anyone can begin with a small preference. But few people can become truly in love without encouragement. In nine cases out of ten, a woman should show more love than she feels. Bingley certainly likes your sister. But he may never do more than like her unless she encourages him.”

“But she encourages him as much as her nature permits. If I can see her feelings, he must be very foolish not to see them.”

“Remember, Eliza, that he does not know Jane's character as you do.”

“But if a woman likes a man and does not hide it, he must discover it.”

“Perhaps, if he sees enough of her. Bingley and Jane meet fairly often, but never for many hours alone. They always meet in large mixed parties. They cannot spend every moment speaking together. Jane should use every half-hour when she has his attention. Once she is sure of him, she can take as much time as she likes to fall in love.”

“Your plan is good if the only goal is a good marriage,” Elizabeth replied. “If I wanted a rich husband, or any husband, I might use it. But Jane does not feel that way. She has no plan. She cannot yet know how strong or sensible her feelings are. She has known him for only two weeks. She danced four dances with him at Meryton. She saw him once at his house and has dined with him four times. That is not enough to understand his character.”

“Not when you describe it that way. If she had only dined with him, she might know only whether he eats well. But remember, they also spent four evenings together. Four evenings can achieve a great deal.”

“Yes. Those four evenings taught them that they both prefer Vingt-un to Commerce. I do not think they discovered any other important part of their characters.”

“Well,” said Charlotte, “I sincerely wish Jane success. If she married him tomorrow, her chance of happiness would be as good as after studying him for a year. Happiness in marriage is entirely a matter of chance. Even when two people know each other's characters well, it does not increase their happiness. After marriage, they always grow different enough to suffer their share of trouble. It is better to know as little as possible about the faults of your future partner.”

“You make me laugh, Charlotte, but your idea is not sensible. You know it is not, and you would never act that way yourself.”

Elizabeth watched Mr. Bingley's attention to her sister. She never suspected that his friend was becoming interested in her. At first, Mr. Darcy had hardly admitted that she was pretty. At the ball, he looked at her without admiration. At their next meeting, he looked only to criticise. He told himself and his friends that her face had almost no good feature. Then he noticed that her beautiful dark eyes made it unusually intelligent. Other discoveries followed and annoyed him equally. His critical eye found several imperfections in her shape. Still, he had to admit that her figure was light and pleasing. He said her manners were not fashionable, yet their easy playfulness attracted him. Elizabeth knew nothing of this. To her, he was merely unpleasant everywhere and had thought her too plain to dance with.

He began wanting to know her better. To prepare for speaking with her, he listened to her conversations with others. She noticed this at a large gathering at Sir William Lucas's house.

“What does Mr. Darcy mean,” she asked Charlotte, “by listening to my conversation with Colonel Forster?”

“Only Mr. Darcy can answer that question.”

“If he does it again, I shall certainly show him that I notice. He has a very mocking eye. If I do not begin by being rude myself, I shall soon become afraid of him.”

Soon afterward, Darcy came nearer without appearing to intend to speak. Miss Lucas dared Elizabeth to mention the subject. This challenge immediately made Elizabeth do it. She turned to him and said:

“Did you not think I spoke remarkably well just now, Mr. Darcy? I was teasing Colonel Forster to give us a ball at Meryton.”

“You spoke with great energy. But that subject always makes a lady energetic.”

“You judge us harshly.”

“Soon it will be her turn to be teased,” said Miss Lucas. “I am going to open the piano, Eliza, and you know what follows.”

“You are a very strange friend! You always want me to play and sing before everyone. If music made me vain, you would be extremely helpful. But I would rather not perform before people used to hearing the finest musicians.” Miss Lucas continued asking, so Elizabeth added, “Very well. If I must, I must.” She looked seriously at Mr. Darcy. “There is an old saying that everyone here surely knows: ‘Save your breath to cool your porridge.’ I shall save mine to strengthen my song.”

Elizabeth's performance was pleasing but not excellent. She sang one or two songs. Several people asked her to sing again, but Mary eagerly took her place at the piano. Mary was the only plain daughter in the family. Therefore, she had worked hard for knowledge and accomplishments and was always impatient to display them.

Mary had neither natural talent nor good taste. Vanity made her practise, but also made her formal and conceited. Those faults would have damaged even greater skill than hers. Elizabeth's easy, natural performance pleased the listeners much more, though Mary played twice as well. After a long concerto, Mary was glad to earn praise by playing Scottish and Irish tunes. Her younger sisters had requested them. They joined some Lucases and two or three officers in eager dancing at one end of the room.

Mr. Darcy stood nearby, silently offended by this use of the evening. The dancing prevented all conversation. He was so lost in his thoughts that he did not notice Sir William Lucas beside him until Sir William spoke.

“What a charming activity this is for young people, Mr. Darcy! Nothing can equal dancing. I consider it one of the finest arts of civilised society.”

“Certainly, sir. It is also popular in less civilised societies. Every savage can dance.”

Sir William only smiled. After seeing Bingley join the dancers, he continued, “Your friend dances beautifully. I am sure you are also an expert, Mr. Darcy.”

“You saw me dance at Meryton, I believe, sir.”

“Yes, indeed, and the sight gave me real pleasure. Do you often dance at St. James's?”

“Never, sir.”

“Do you not think dancing there would be a proper compliment to the place?”

“I never pay that compliment to any place if I can avoid it.”

“You have a house in London, I suppose?”

Mr. Darcy bowed.

“I once thought of settling in London myself because I enjoy the finest society. But I was not sure London's air would suit Lady Lucas.”

He paused, hoping for an answer, but Darcy did not wish to give one. At that moment, Elizabeth walked toward them. Sir William suddenly imagined doing something very polite and called to her.

“My dear Miss Eliza, why are you not dancing? Mr. Darcy, allow me to present this young lady as a very desirable partner. Surely you cannot refuse with such beauty before you.” He took her hand and tried to give it to Mr. Darcy. Darcy was greatly surprised but willing to receive it. Elizabeth immediately pulled back and spoke to Sir William with some discomfort.

“Indeed, sir, I have no intention of dancing. Please do not think I came here to beg for a partner.”

Mr. Darcy formally asked for the honour of her hand, but without success. Elizabeth remained determined. Sir William's efforts did not change her mind.

“You dance so well, Miss Eliza, that refusing us this pleasure is cruel. This gentleman usually dislikes dancing. Still, he surely cannot object to pleasing us for half an hour.”

“Mr. Darcy is perfectly polite,” Elizabeth said with a smile.

“Indeed he is. But with such a reason, my dear Miss Eliza, his willingness is natural. Who would object to such a partner?”

Elizabeth gave a playful look and turned away. Her refusal did not make Darcy dislike her. He was thinking of her with some pleasure when Miss Bingley spoke to him.

“I can guess what you are thinking about.”

“I doubt it.”

“You are thinking how unbearable many evenings like this would be, among such people. I completely agree. I have never been more annoyed! These people are dull yet noisy, empty yet full of their own importance. I would give anything to hear your severe comments about them.”

“Your guess is completely wrong. My thoughts were much more pleasant. I was considering the great pleasure that beautiful eyes can give when they belong to a pretty woman.”

Miss Bingley immediately stared at him and asked him to name the lady. Mr. Darcy answered without fear.

“Miss Elizabeth Bennet.”

“Miss Elizabeth Bennet!” Miss Bingley repeated. “I am amazed. How long has she been such a favourite? When should I congratulate you?”

“That is exactly the question I expected. A lady's imagination moves very quickly. It jumps from admiration to love and from love to marriage in one moment. I knew you would congratulate me.”

“Well, if you are so serious, I shall consider it settled. You will have a charming mother-in-law. Of course, she will always be visiting you at Pemberley.”

He listened with complete indifference while she amused herself in this way. His calmness convinced her there was no danger, so she continued her jokes.
