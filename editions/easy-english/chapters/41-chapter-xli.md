# Chapter XLI

The first week after their return passed quickly. The second began, and it was the regiment’s last week in Meryton. All the neighbourhood’s young ladies quickly became sad. Almost everyone shared the sadness. Only the older Miss Bennets could still eat, drink, sleep, and follow their usual activities. Kitty and Lydia often criticised them for this lack of feeling. Their own misery was extreme, so they could not understand their family’s hard hearts.

“Good heavens! What will happen to us? What will we do?” they often cried bitterly. “How can you smile like that, Lizzy?”

Their loving mother shared all their grief. She remembered suffering in the same way twenty-five years earlier.

“I am certain,” she said, “I cried for two whole days when Colonel Miller’s regiment left. I thought my heart would break.”

“I am certain mine will break,” said Lydia.

“If only one could go to Brighton!” Mrs. Bennet observed.

“Oh yes!—if only one could go to Brighton! But papa is so unpleasant.”

“A little swimming in the sea would completely restore me.”

“And Aunt Philips is certain it would do me a great deal of good,” Kitty added.

Such complaints constantly filled Longbourn House. Elizabeth tried to find them amusing, but shame destroyed every feeling of pleasure. She again understood why Mr. Darcy had objected to her family. She had never felt so ready to forgive his interference in his friend’s plans.

But Lydia’s dark future soon became bright. Mrs. Forster invited her to travel with her to Brighton. Mrs. Forster was the regiment’s colonel’s wife. This priceless friend was very young and had married only recently. She and Lydia had similar cheerful, lively characters. They had known each other for three months and had been close friends for two.

Lydia’s joy, her worship of Mrs. Forster, and Mrs. Bennet’s delight were hard to describe. Kitty’s humiliation was just as great. Lydia ignored her sister’s feelings and rushed excitedly around the house. She demanded everyone’s congratulations and laughed and talked more wildly than ever. Unlucky Kitty remained in the sitting room and complained about her fate. Her words were as unreasonable as her voice was annoyed.

“I cannot understand why Mrs. Forster did not invite me as well as Lydia,” she said. “I may not be her special friend. But I have as much right to an invitation as Lydia, and more. I am two years older.”

Elizabeth tried without success to make Kitty reasonable. Jane tried equally hard to make her accept the situation. Elizabeth herself felt nothing like her mother and Lydia. She believed the invitation would destroy Lydia’s final possibility of gaining common sense. Elizabeth knew Lydia would hate her if she discovered what happened next. Still, she secretly advised her father not to let Lydia go. She described Lydia’s generally improper behaviour. She explained how little Lydia could gain from friendship with a woman like Mrs. Forster. With such a companion in Brighton, Lydia would probably behave even more carelessly. The temptations there would be greater than at home. Her father listened carefully and then said—

“Lydia will never be satisfied until she embarrasses herself in some public place. We will never find a cheaper or more convenient opportunity for her to do it.”

“If you understood how much Lydia’s public and careless behaviour harms all of us, you would decide differently,” said Elizabeth. “In fact, it has already harmed us.”

“Already harmed you!” Mr. Bennet repeated. “What! Has she frightened away some of your lovers? Poor little Lizzy! But do not be unhappy. Young men who cannot bear a little foolishness in the family deserve no regret. Come, show me the list of weak men Lydia’s foolishness has driven away.”

“Indeed, you misunderstand me. I am not complaining of any personal injury. I am speaking of the harm to all of us. Lydia’s wild changes of mood, boldness, and refusal of all control must affect our position. They must also damage the world’s respect for us. Forgive me, because I must speak plainly. My dear father, you must make the effort to control her extreme spirits. You must teach her that her present pleasures cannot become her whole life. Otherwise, she will soon be impossible to improve. Her character will become fixed. At sixteen, she will be the most determined flirt who ever made herself and her family ridiculous. And her flirting will be of the worst and lowest kind. She has nothing attractive except youth and a fairly pretty face. Her mind is ignorant and empty. Therefore, she cannot protect herself from the general scorn that her hunger for attention will cause. Kitty is also in this danger. She will follow wherever Lydia leads. They are vain, ignorant, lazy, and completely uncontrolled! Oh, my dear father, can you believe people will not criticise and despise them? Can you believe their sisters will not often share their shame?”

Mr. Bennet saw that Elizabeth cared deeply about the matter. He affectionately took her hand and replied—

“Do not worry, my dear. Wherever people know you and Jane, they must respect and value you. Having two—or perhaps three—very foolish sisters will not make you appear worse. We will have no peace at Longbourn unless Lydia goes to Brighton. So let her go. Colonel Forster is sensible and will keep her from serious trouble. Luckily, she is too poor for anyone to hunt for her money. In Brighton, she will matter even less as a common flirt than she does here. The officers will find women more worthy of their attention. Let us hope this teaches her how unimportant she is. In any case, she cannot become much worse. If she does, we will have good reason to lock her away for life.”

Elizabeth had to accept this answer, though her opinion did not change. She left him disappointed and sorry. However, she did not increase her pain by continuing to think about it. She was sure she had done her duty. Worrying over unavoidable problems, or making them worse through anxiety, was not part of her character.

If Lydia and her mother had known about this conversation, they could hardly have expressed their anger loudly enough. In Lydia’s imagination, a Brighton visit contained every possible earthly happiness. She imagined that lively seaside town’s streets covered with officers. Dozens of unknown officers watched her with admiration. She saw all the camp’s glory. Its tents stood in beautiful, even lines, crowded with young, cheerful people and bright red uniforms. To complete the picture, she sat beneath a tent, gently flirting with at least six officers at once.

What would Lydia have felt if she knew her sister tried to destroy such hopes and realities? Only her mother could have understood, because she would have felt almost the same. Lydia’s Brighton journey was Mrs. Bennet’s only comfort. She sadly knew her husband never intended to go there himself.

But they knew nothing about the conversation. Their happiness continued almost without interruption until Lydia left home.

Elizabeth would now see Mr. Wickham for the last time. Since returning, she had often been in his company, so her nervous feelings had almost ended. Her former attraction to him had completely disappeared. The gentleness that first delighted her now appeared false and always the same. It disgusted and tired her. His present behaviour gave her another reason for annoyance. He soon showed that he wished to begin again the attention he gave her when they first met. After everything that had happened, this only angered her. He had selected her for such empty and meaningless flirting, so she stopped caring about him. She firmly discouraged his attention. Still, she felt insulted by what his actions suggested. He believed that whenever he renewed his attention, her vanity would be pleased and her affection secured. He did not care how long he had ignored her or why.

On the regiment’s final day in Meryton, Wickham and several other officers dined at Longbourn. Elizabeth had no wish to part from him with friendly feelings. He asked how she had spent her time at Hunsford. She mentioned that Colonel Fitzwilliam and Mr. Darcy had both spent three weeks at Rosings. Then she asked whether he knew the Colonel.

He looked surprised, displeased, and alarmed. After a moment, he remembered himself and smiled again. He replied that he had often seen the Colonel in the past. After calling him a very gentlemanly man, Wickham asked how Elizabeth liked him. She answered with warm praise. Soon afterwards, Wickham added with an indifferent manner, “How long did you say he stayed at Rosings?”

“Nearly three weeks.”

“And you saw him often?”

“Yes, almost every day.”

“His manners are very different from his cousin’s.”

“Yes, very different. But I think Mr. Darcy improves when one knows him better.”

“Indeed!” cried Wickham with a look she noticed. “And may I ask—” He stopped himself and continued more cheerfully. “Does his manner improve? Has he kindly added any politeness to his usual style? For I cannot hope,” he continued more quietly and seriously, “that his true character has improved.”

“Oh, no!” said Elizabeth. “I believe his true character remains very much what it always was.”

As she spoke, Wickham did not know whether to celebrate her words or distrust their meaning. Something in her face made him listen with fear and anxiety. She continued—

“When I said that knowing him improved my opinion, I did not mean he was changing. Neither his mind nor his manners are improving. I meant that knowing him better helped me understand his character better.”

Wickham’s red face and troubled expression now showed greater alarm. He remained silent for several minutes. Then he overcame his embarrassment, turned to her again, and spoke in his gentlest voice—

“You know my feelings towards Mr. Darcy very well. So you will understand my sincere pleasure that he can at least pretend to behave rightly. His pride might help many others, even if it does not help him. It must prevent the terrible behaviour from which I suffered. But I fear this careful manner you describe appears only when he visits his aunt. He greatly fears her opinion and judgment. I know her presence has always controlled him. We must also remember his wish to marry Miss de Bourgh. I am certain that marriage matters greatly to him.”

Elizabeth could not prevent a smile, but she answered only with a small movement of her head. She saw that he wanted to discuss his old complaints again. She had no desire to encourage him. For the rest of the evening, he appeared as cheerful as usual. But he made no further attempt to give Elizabeth special attention. At last, they parted politely. Perhaps both hoped never to meet again.

When the gathering ended, Lydia returned to Meryton with Mrs. Forster. They would leave early the next morning. Lydia’s farewell from her family was more noisy than emotional. Only Kitty cried, and her tears came from annoyance and jealousy. Mrs. Bennet offered many good wishes for her daughter’s happiness. She strongly ordered Lydia not to miss any chance of enjoying herself fully. Everyone had reason to believe Lydia would follow this advice. Lydia’s own noisy joy filled her farewell. Nobody heard the quieter goodbyes of her sisters.
