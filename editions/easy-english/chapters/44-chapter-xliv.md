# Chapter XLIV

Elizabeth decided that Mr. Darcy would bring his sister the day after Miss Darcy reached Pemberley. Therefore, she planned to remain near the inn all morning. But she was wrong because the visitors came the morning after the Gardiners arrived in Lambton. Elizabeth and the Gardiners had walked around town with new friends. They had just returned to dress before dining with the same family. Then a carriage sound drew them to the window. A gentleman and lady drove up the street in a small open carriage. Elizabeth immediately recognised the servants’ uniforms and guessed their purpose. She greatly surprised her relations by explaining the honour she expected. Her uncle and aunt were amazed. Her embarrassed manner, the visit itself, and the previous day’s events suggested a new possibility. They had never considered it before. Now only Mr. Darcy’s love for their niece seemed to explain such attention. While these new ideas filled their minds, Elizabeth became more nervous each moment. Her own strong feelings surprised her. Among other worries, she feared Darcy’s love had made him praise her too much. She wanted more than usual to please, so naturally feared every ability would fail her.

She stepped away from the window because she feared being seen. She walked around the room and tried to calm herself. Her uncle and aunt’s surprised, questioning looks made everything worse.

Miss Darcy and her brother entered, and the frightening introduction took place. Elizabeth was amazed that Miss Darcy felt at least as embarrassed as she did. In Lambton, Elizabeth had heard that Miss Darcy was extremely proud. Within minutes, she saw that the girl was only extremely shy. Elizabeth could hardly obtain more than one word from her.

Miss Darcy was tall and more strongly built than Elizabeth. Though only a little over sixteen, she had an adult, graceful figure. Her brother was more handsome, but her face showed good sense and good humour. Her manners were perfectly gentle and not proud. Elizabeth had expected another sharp, confident observer like Mr. Darcy himself. She felt greatly relieved to discover someone very different.

They had not been together long when Darcy said Bingley was also coming to visit Elizabeth. She barely had time to express pleasure and prepare before his quick steps sounded outside. A moment later, he entered. Elizabeth’s anger towards him had ended long ago. Even if it remained, his natural warmth at seeing her would have destroyed it. He asked friendly but general questions about her family. He looked and spoke with his usual cheerful ease.

Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner found him almost as interesting as Elizabeth did. They had long wished to meet him. Indeed, everyone before them strongly held their attention. Their new suspicions about Mr. Darcy and their niece made them watch both carefully but secretly. They soon became certain that at least one knew what love felt like. They remained unsure about Elizabeth. But the gentleman clearly overflowed with admiration.

Elizabeth herself had much to do. She wanted to understand each visitor’s feelings and calm her own. She also wanted to please everyone. She most feared failing at the last task, yet its success was most certain. Everyone she tried to please already liked her. Bingley was ready, Georgiana eager, and Darcy determined to be pleased.

Seeing Bingley naturally made her think about Jane. How strongly she wished to know whether he had similar thoughts! Sometimes, she imagined he spoke less than before. Once or twice, she believed he studied her face for a family likeness. Perhaps she imagined this, but she could clearly judge his behaviour towards Miss Darcy. His sister had presented Georgiana as Jane’s rival. Neither of them showed any special affection. Nothing between them supported Miss Bingley’s hopes. Elizabeth soon felt satisfied on this point. Before they left, two or three small details seemed meaningful to her anxious mind. Bingley appeared to remember Jane with tenderness. He seemed to wish Elizabeth would mention her, though he did not dare ask. While the others talked, he told Elizabeth with real regret, “It has been a very long time since I had the pleasure of seeing you.” Before she answered, he added, “It is over eight months. We have not met since 26 November, when we all danced together at Netherfield.”

Elizabeth was pleased that he remembered the date so exactly. Later, when nobody listened, he asked whether all her sisters were at Longbourn. The question and earlier comment said little by themselves. But his look and manner gave them meaning.

Elizabeth could rarely look directly at Mr. Darcy. Whenever she did, his expression showed general kindness. Everything he said lacked pride or scorn for his companions. The improved manners she saw yesterday had lasted at least one more day. He now tried to know people whose company would have shamed him months earlier. He wanted their good opinion. He behaved politely to Elizabeth and the relatives he had openly scorned. She remembered their last emotional meeting in Hunsford Parsonage. The difference was so great that she could hardly hide her surprise. Even among close friends at Netherfield or important relatives at Rosings, Darcy had never tried so hard to please. He had never appeared so free from self-importance and cold reserve. Yet success here could give him no importance. The Netherfield and Rosings ladies would laugh at and criticise his new acquaintances.

Their visitors stayed over half an hour. When they rose to leave, Mr. Darcy asked his sister to join his invitation. They wished Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner and Miss Bennet to dine at Pemberley before leaving the area. Miss Darcy was not used to giving invitations and spoke shyly, but willingly obeyed. Mrs. Gardiner looked at Elizabeth, because the invitation concerned her most. She wanted to know whether her niece wished to accept, but Elizabeth turned away. Her aunt decided this careful avoidance showed temporary embarrassment, not dislike of the plan. Her social husband clearly wished to accept. So Mrs. Gardiner promised they would attend, and they chose the day after next.

Bingley showed great pleasure that he would see Elizabeth again. He still had much to say and many questions about all their Hertfordshire friends. Elizabeth believed he wished to hear about Jane, and this pleased her. For this and other reasons, she later remembered the half-hour with some satisfaction. During the visit itself, she enjoyed very little. She wanted to be alone and feared questions or hints from her uncle and aunt. She stayed only long enough to hear their good opinion of Bingley, then hurried away to dress.

But Elizabeth did not need to fear Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner’s curiosity. They did not wish to force her to speak. She clearly knew Mr. Darcy far better than they had imagined. He was clearly deeply in love with her. They saw much of interest, but nothing that gave them the right to ask questions.

They now strongly wished to think well of Mr. Darcy. From what they knew, they could find no fault. His politeness affected them. If they judged him only from their own feelings and his servant’s report, people who knew Mr. Darcy in Hertfordshire would not recognise that description of him. Still, they now had a reason to believe the housekeeper. She had known him since he was four, and her own manners showed good character. They realised they should not quickly dismiss her judgment. Nothing their Lambton friends said seriously weakened it. They could accuse Darcy only of pride. He probably had some, and people would certainly accuse him anyway. A small market town often blamed a family that did not visit. Everyone agreed, however, that Darcy was generous and helped poor people greatly.

The travellers soon learned that local people did not greatly respect Wickham. They did not fully understand his problems with his supporter’s son. But everyone knew he had left many debts behind when he departed Derbyshire. Mr. Darcy later paid them.

That evening, Elizabeth thought even more about Pemberley than the evening before. The evening felt long, yet offered too little time to understand her feelings for its owner. She lay awake for two full hours trying to discover them. She certainly did not hate him. No, that hatred had disappeared long ago. For nearly as long, she had felt ashamed of ever truly disliking him. Becoming certain of his valuable qualities created respect. At first, she had accepted it unwillingly. But for some time, respect had no longer troubled her. Yesterday’s strong testimony showed his character kindly and increased her respect into something friendlier. Above all, another feeling went beyond respect and admiration: gratitude. She felt grateful not only because he had once loved her. He still loved her enough to forgive her angry rejection and unfair accusations. She had expected him to avoid her like his greatest enemy. Instead, after their accidental meeting, he strongly wished to continue knowing her. When they were alone, he showed no improper affection or special manner. But he worked to win her friends’ good opinion and introduce her to his sister. Such a change in so proud a man caused surprise and gratitude. It must come from love—strong love. That thought pleased her and deserved encouragement, though she could not exactly name her feelings. She respected and admired him. She felt grateful and truly cared about his happiness. She only needed to know two things. How much did she want his happiness to depend on her? Would both be happier if she used the power she imagined she still possessed and encouraged another proposal?

That evening, Elizabeth and her aunt agreed about Miss Darcy’s remarkable politeness. She had visited them on the very day she reached Pemberley, arriving only for a late breakfast. They could not equal this kindness, but should imitate it by making their own effort. Therefore, they decided to visit her at Pemberley the next morning. Elizabeth was pleased, though she could give herself little reason why.

Mr. Gardiner left soon after breakfast. The fishing plan had been renewed the previous day. He had firmly promised to meet several Pemberley gentlemen by noon.
