# Chapter XVI

No one objected to the young people's evening with their aunt. Mr. Collins worried about leaving Mr. and Mrs. Bennet for one evening during his visit. But they firmly rejected every objection. At the proper time, the coach took him and his five cousins to Meryton. As the girls entered the drawing room, they heard welcome news. Mr. Wickham had accepted their uncle's invitation and was already in the house.

After hearing this and sitting down, Mr. Collins had time to look around and admire. The room's size and furniture impressed him greatly. He declared that he might almost be in the small summer breakfast room at Rosings. At first, Mrs. Philips did not find the comparison very flattering. Then Mr. Collins explained what Rosings was and who owned it. He described just one of Lady Catherine's drawing rooms. The fireplace alone had cost eight hundred pounds. Mrs. Philips then understood the full compliment. She would hardly have been offended by comparison with the housekeeper's room.

Mr. Collins happily described Lady Catherine and her grand house. Sometimes he also praised his own humble home and its improvements. This kept him busy until the gentlemen joined them. Mrs. Philips listened very carefully. With every detail, she thought him more important. She planned to repeat everything to her neighbours as soon as possible. The girls could not listen to their cousin. They had no instrument to play and could only wish for one. They examined their own poor copies of china on the fireplace. The wait seemed very long. At last, however, it ended. The gentlemen entered. When Mr. Wickham came in, Elizabeth knew her admiration had been perfectly reasonable. The ——shire officers were generally a respectable, gentlemanly group. The best of them were there that evening. But Mr. Wickham's appearance, face, manner, and walk were far above theirs. They, in turn, were far above the broad-faced, heavy Mr. Philips. Their uncle followed them into the room, breathing strongly of port wine.

Almost every woman looked towards Mr. Wickham, making him a fortunate man. He finally sat beside Elizabeth, making her the fortunate woman. He immediately began a pleasant conversation. They only discussed the wet evening and the chance of a rainy season. Yet his skill made even the dullest, most ordinary subject seem interesting.

With Mr. Wickham and the officers competing for the young women's attention, Mr. Collins seemed unimportant. To the young ladies, he was certainly nothing. But Mrs. Philips still listened kindly at times. She also made sure he had plenty of coffee and muffins.

When the card tables appeared, Mr. Collins could repay her kindness by joining the game of whist.

“At present, I know little about the game,” he said. “But I shall be happy to improve, because in my position in life——” Mrs. Philips thanked him warmly for agreeing to play. However, she could not wait to hear his reason.

Mr. Wickham did not play whist. The other table gladly welcomed him between Elizabeth and Lydia. At first, Lydia seemed likely to take all his attention because she talked with great determination. But she also loved the lottery-ticket game. Soon she cared too much about bets and prizes to focus on anyone. Apart from the game's ordinary demands, Mr. Wickham was therefore free to speak with Elizabeth. She was very willing to listen. Most of all, she wanted to hear about his connection with Mr. Darcy. Yet she could not expect him to tell that story. She did not even dare mention Darcy. Unexpectedly, however, Mr. Wickham satisfied her curiosity himself. He asked how far Netherfield was from Meryton. After she answered, he hesitantly asked how long Mr. Darcy had been staying there.

“About a month,” Elizabeth said. She did not want the subject to end, so she added, “I understand he owns a very large property in Derbyshire.”

“Yes,” Wickham replied. “His estate there is magnificent. It provides ten thousand pounds a year. No one could give you more certain information about it than I can. I have had a special connection with his family since childhood.”

Elizabeth could not hide her surprise.

“Your surprise is understandable, Miss Bennet. Yesterday, you probably saw how coldly we met. Do you know Mr. Darcy well?”

“As well as I ever wish to,” Elizabeth cried warmly. “I spent four days in the same house with him. I think he is very unpleasant.”

“I have no right to judge whether he is pleasant,” Wickham said. “I am not able to form a fair opinion. I have known him too long and too well. I cannot be impartial. Still, your opinion would surprise most people. Perhaps you would not express it so strongly anywhere else. Here, you are with your own family.”

“Honestly, I say nothing here that I would not say in any local house, except Netherfield. No one in Hertfordshire likes him. His pride disgusts everyone. You will not hear anyone speak more favourably of him.”

After a short pause, Wickham said, “I cannot pretend to regret that he, or anyone, is judged fairly. But that rarely happens to him. His wealth and importance blind people. His proud, powerful manner frightens them. They see only the person he chooses to show them.”

“Even from my slight knowledge, I would judge him to be bad-tempered.”

Wickham only shook his head.

At his next chance to speak, he said, “I wonder whether he will remain in this area much longer.”

“I do not know at all. While I was at Netherfield, I heard nothing about his leaving. I hope his presence will not change your plans to join the ——shire.”

“Oh, no. Mr. Darcy will not drive me away. If he wants to avoid me, he must leave. We are not friends, and meeting him always causes me pain. But I have no reason to avoid him that I could not announce to the whole world. He treated me very badly, and I deeply regret the man he has become. His father, Miss Bennet, the late Mr. Darcy, was one of the best men who ever lived. He was also the truest friend I ever had. Being with his son fills me with a thousand painful and tender memories. His behaviour towards me has been shameful. Yet I honestly believe I could forgive him anything. The one thing I cannot forgive is how he disappointed his father's hopes and dishonoured his memory.”

The story interested Elizabeth more and more, and she listened eagerly. But its personal nature stopped her from asking further questions.

Mr. Wickham moved to more general subjects: Meryton, the neighbourhood, and local society. He seemed very pleased with everything he had seen. When discussing the society, especially, he offered gentle but very clear compliments.

“The hope of constant and good company chiefly persuaded me to join the ——shire,” he added. “I knew it was a respectable and pleasant regiment. My friend Denny further tempted me by describing their present home. He told me about the great attention and excellent friends Meryton had given them. I admit that I need society. I have suffered disappointment, and my spirits cannot bear loneliness. I need work and company. I was not intended for a military life, but circumstances now make it suitable. I should have entered the church. I was educated for it. By now, I should have held a very valuable clerical living. I would have done so, if the gentleman we just discussed had allowed it.”

“Really!”

“Yes. The late Mr. Darcy left me the right to receive the best church position he controlled when it next became free. He was my godfather and extremely fond of me. I cannot properly describe his kindness. He meant to provide generously for me and thought he had done so. But when the position became free, it was given to someone else.”

“Good heavens!” Elizabeth cried. “How could that happen? How could anyone ignore his will? Why did you not take legal action?”

“The gift was written with one small legal weakness, so the law offered me no hope. An honourable man could not have doubted its meaning. But Mr. Darcy chose to doubt it. Perhaps he treated it only as advice that depended on my conduct. He claimed I had lost my right through waste, carelessness, or almost anything else. The position certainly became free two years ago, just when I was old enough to hold it. It was certainly given to another man. And I certainly cannot accuse myself of doing anything that deserved this loss. I have a warm and careless temper. Perhaps I sometimes expressed my opinion of him too freely, even to his face. I can remember nothing worse. The truth is that we are very different men, and he hates me.”

“This is shocking! He deserves public shame.”

“One day, he will receive it—but not from me. Until I can forget his father, I can never challenge or expose him.”

Elizabeth admired him for these feelings. As he expressed them, she thought him more handsome than ever.

After a pause, she asked, “But what could be Darcy's reason? What made him behave so cruelly?”

“A complete and determined dislike of me. I must partly blame jealousy. If the late Mr. Darcy had liked me less, his son might have accepted me better. I believe his father's unusual affection for me annoyed him from an early age. He could not bear our kind of competition. He could not accept that his father often preferred me.”

“I never thought Mr. Darcy was this bad. I have never liked him, but I did not think so badly of him. I believed he despised everyone in general. I never suspected him of such cruel revenge, injustice, and lack of humanity!”

After thinking for several minutes, she continued, “I remember him boasting at Netherfield that his anger never ended. He said he had an unforgiving temper. His character must be terrible.”

“I cannot trust myself to discuss him,” Wickham replied. “I can hardly be fair to him.”

Elizabeth thought deeply again. After some time, she cried, “To treat his father's godson, friend, and favourite this way!” She might have added, “And a young man like you, whose face itself proves your goodness.” Instead, she only said, “You were probably his childhood companion too. I think you said you were connected in the closest way.”

“We were born in the same parish and within the same park. We spent most of our youth together. We lived in the same house, shared the same pleasures, and received the same fatherly care. My father began in the profession that your uncle, Mr. Philips, honours so well. But he gave it up to serve the late Mr. Darcy. He devoted all his time to managing the Pemberley estate. Mr. Darcy valued him greatly and trusted him as a close friend. Mr. Darcy often said that my father's active management had placed him under a great obligation. Just before my father died, Mr. Darcy freely promised to provide for me. I believe he did this from gratitude to my father as much as affection for me.”

“How strange!” Elizabeth cried. “How terrible! I wonder that Mr. Darcy's pride did not make him treat you fairly. Even without a better reason, he should have been too proud to be dishonest. I must call it dishonesty.”

“It is surprising,” Wickham replied. “Almost every action of his comes from pride. Pride has often been his best friend. It has brought him closer to goodness than any other feeling. But none of us is always consistent. In his treatment of me, stronger feelings defeated even his pride.”

“Can such terrible pride ever make him do good?”

“Yes. It has often made him generous. He gives money freely, welcomes guests, helps his tenants, and supports poor people. Pride in his family and father causes this. He is very proud of the man his father was. He will not appear to dishonour his family or become worse than its popular members. He also wants to keep Pemberley House influential. These are powerful reasons for him. He has pride as a brother too. Together with some brotherly affection, it makes him a kind and careful guardian to his sister. You will usually hear people praise him as the most caring and best of brothers.”

“What sort of girl is Miss Darcy?”

He shook his head. “I wish I could call her pleasant. Speaking badly of a Darcy causes me pain. But she is too much like her brother—very, very proud. As a child, she was loving and pleasant, and extremely fond of me. I spent many hours amusing her. But now she is nothing to me. She is a handsome girl of about fifteen or sixteen. I understand that she is highly educated and talented. Since her father's death, she has lived in London. A lady lives with her and directs her education.”

They paused many times and tried several other subjects. Yet Elizabeth could not stop herself from returning once more to Darcy.

“His close friendship with Mr. Bingley amazes me. Mr. Bingley seems completely good-tempered. I truly believe he is a good man. How can he be friends with someone like Darcy? How can they suit each other? Do you know Mr. Bingley?”

“Not at all.”

“He is sweet-tempered, kind, and charming. He cannot know what Mr. Darcy really is.”

“Probably not. But Mr. Darcy can be pleasing when he chooses. He is certainly able to speak well. He can be a good companion when he thinks someone deserves the effort. With people equal to him in wealth and importance, he behaves very differently. His pride never leaves him. But with the rich, he is generous, fair, sincere, sensible, honourable, and perhaps pleasant. His fortune and appearance also help.”

Soon afterwards, the whist game ended. The players gathered around the other table. Mr. Collins stood between his cousin Elizabeth and Mrs. Philips. Mrs. Philips asked the usual questions about his success. He had not done very well and had lost every point. She began to express concern. With great and serious importance, he assured her that it did not matter at all. He considered the money a small amount and begged her not to worry.

“I understand very well, madam,” he said, “that people who play cards must accept such risks. Fortunately, five shillings means nothing in my circumstances. Many people could certainly not say the same. But thanks to Lady Catherine de Bourgh, I have no need to worry about such little matters.”

Mr. Wickham noticed this. After watching Mr. Collins for a few moments, he quietly asked Elizabeth whether her relations knew the De Bourgh family closely.

“Lady Catherine de Bourgh recently gave him a clerical living,” she replied. “I hardly know how she first noticed Mr. Collins. But he certainly has not known her long.”

“You know, of course, that Lady Catherine de Bourgh and Lady Anne Darcy were sisters. Therefore, she is the present Mr. Darcy's aunt.”

“No, I did not know. I knew nothing about Lady Catherine's family. Until the day before yesterday, I had never even heard of her.”

“Her daughter, Miss de Bourgh, will have a very large fortune. People believe that she and her cousin will marry and unite the two estates.”

This news made Elizabeth smile as she thought of poor Miss Bingley. If Darcy had already chosen another woman, all Miss Bingley's attention was wasted. Her affection for his sister and her praise of Darcy himself were equally useless.

“Mr. Collins speaks very highly of Lady Catherine and her daughter,” Elizabeth said. “But some details make me think his gratitude misleads him. Although she is his patron, I suspect she is proud and self-important.”

“I believe she is very much both,” Wickham replied. “I have not seen her for many years. But I clearly remember disliking her. Her manner gave orders and insulted people. She is said to be unusually sensible and clever. I think some of that reputation comes from her rank and money. Some comes from her commanding manner. The rest comes from her nephew's pride. He expects everyone connected with him to possess an excellent mind.”

Elizabeth agreed that his explanation was very reasonable. They continued talking with mutual pleasure until supper ended the card games. Then the other women could share Mr. Wickham's attention. Mrs. Philips's supper party was too noisy for conversation. Still, his manners pleased everyone. Everything he said sounded good, and everything he did looked graceful. Elizabeth left with her head full of him. Throughout the journey home, she could think only of Mr. Wickham and his story. Yet she had no time even to say his name. Neither Lydia nor Mr. Collins was silent for a moment. Lydia talked endlessly about lottery tickets and the fish she had won and lost. Mr. Collins praised Mr. and Mrs. Philips's politeness. He insisted that his whist losses did not matter. He listed every dish served at supper and repeatedly feared that he was crowding his cousins. He had more to say than he could finish before the coach stopped at Longbourn House.
