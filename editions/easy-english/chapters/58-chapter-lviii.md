# Chapter LVIII

Elizabeth half expected Mr. Bingley to receive an excuse from his friend. Instead, only a few days after Lady Catherine’s visit, he brought Darcy to Longbourn. The gentlemen arrived early. Mrs. Bennet had not yet told Darcy about his aunt’s visit, which Elizabeth feared every moment. Bingley wanted to be alone with Jane, so he suggested that everyone take a walk. They agreed. Mrs. Bennet did not usually walk, and Mary could never spare the time. The remaining five left together. However, Bingley and Jane soon let the others move ahead. They remained behind, leaving Elizabeth, Kitty, and Darcy to entertain one another. None of them said much. Kitty was too afraid of Darcy to speak. Elizabeth secretly prepared a desperate decision, and perhaps Darcy did the same.

They walked towards the Lucases’ home because Kitty wanted to visit Maria. Elizabeth saw no reason why everyone should join that visit. When Kitty left them, Elizabeth boldly continued alone with Darcy. Now she had to carry out her decision. While her courage remained strong, she immediately said—

“Mr. Darcy, I am very selfish. To relieve my own feelings, I do not care how much I may hurt yours. I must thank you for your extraordinary kindness to my poor sister. Since learning about it, I have desperately wanted to express my gratitude. If the rest of my family knew, I would have more than my own thanks to offer.”

“I am extremely sorry,” Darcy replied with surprise and emotion, “that anyone told you something which may have wrongly caused you pain. I did not think Mrs. Gardiner could be trusted so little.”

“You must not blame my aunt. Lydia’s carelessness first revealed your involvement. Naturally, I could not rest until I knew every detail. On behalf of my entire family, let me thank you again and again. Your generous concern made you take enormous trouble and suffer many embarrassing experiences to find them.”

“If you must thank me,” he replied, “thank me only for yourself. I cannot deny that the wish to make you happy strengthened my other reasons. But your family owes me nothing. I respect them greatly, yet I believe I thought only of you.”

Elizabeth was too embarrassed to speak. After a brief pause, he added, “You are too generous to play with my feelings. If you still feel as you did last April, tell me immediately. My love and wishes have not changed. But one word from you will silence me on this subject forever.”

Elizabeth understood the unusual discomfort and anxiety of his position. She forced herself to speak, though not very smoothly. She explained that her feelings had changed greatly since the time he mentioned. She now received his promise with gratitude and pleasure. Her reply gave him a happiness he had probably never known before. He answered with all the honest warmth expected from a man deeply in love. If Elizabeth could have met his eyes, she would have seen heartfelt delight shining beautifully across his face. She could not look, but she could listen. His description of her importance to him made his love more valuable every moment.

They continued walking without knowing where they went. They had too much to think, feel, and say to notice anything else. Elizabeth soon learned that they owed their new understanding to his aunt’s efforts. On her return through London, she had indeed visited him. She described her journey to Longbourn, its purpose, and her conversation with Elizabeth. She repeated every expression that supposedly showed Elizabeth’s stubborn confidence. His aunt thought this account would help gain the promise Elizabeth had refused. Unfortunately for her, it had exactly the opposite effect.

“It taught me to hope,” he said, “when I had hardly allowed myself any hope before. I know your nature well enough to be certain of one thing. If you had decided completely and forever against me, you would have told Lady Catherine honestly and openly.”

Elizabeth became red and laughed. “Yes, you know my honesty well enough to believe that. After speaking so terribly about you directly to your face, I would not hesitate to do it before all your relations.”

“What did you say about me that I did not deserve? Your accusations were based on mistaken beliefs, but my behaviour deserved the strongest criticism. It was unforgivable. I cannot remember it without hating it.”

“We will not argue about who deserves more blame for that evening,” Elizabeth said. “If carefully examined, neither of us behaved perfectly. But since then, I hope we have both become more polite.”

“I cannot forgive myself so easily. For many months, remembering everything has caused me indescribable pain—my words, behaviour, manner, and expressions. I shall never forget your well-deserved criticism: ‘If you had behaved in a more gentlemanlike manner.’ Those were your words. You cannot imagine how they have tortured me. Still, I admit that some time passed before I became reasonable enough to accept their truth.”

“I certainly never expected them to affect you so strongly. I had no idea you would ever feel them in that way.”

“I easily believe that. You thought I lacked every proper feeling. I am sure you did. I shall never forget your expression when you spoke. You said I could not possibly have addressed you in any way that would make you accept me.”

“Oh, do not repeat what I said then. These memories are no good at all. I assure you, I have long felt deeply ashamed of it.”

Darcy mentioned his letter. “Did it—did it soon improve your opinion of me?” he asked. “When you read it, did you believe anything it contained?”

She explained its effect on her and how it had gradually removed all her former prejudices.

“I knew my words would hurt you,” he said, “but the letter was necessary. I hope you have destroyed it. There was one part—especially its beginning—that I would hate you to read again. I remember certain expressions that could fairly make you hate me.”

“I shall certainly burn the letter if that is necessary to keep my affection. We both know that my opinions can change. But I hope they are not quite as easily changed as your request suggests.”

“When I wrote that letter,” Darcy replied, “I believed I was perfectly calm and controlled. Since then, I have realized that I wrote with terrible bitterness.”

“Perhaps the letter began bitterly, but it did not end that way. The farewell itself is completely kind. But think no more about it. Both writer and reader now feel completely differently. We should forget every unpleasant detail. You must learn some of my philosophy. Remember only the parts of the past that give you pleasure.”

“I cannot believe you truly follow such a philosophy. Your memories must contain no blame at all. Your satisfaction comes not from philosophy, but from something better: ignorance. My situation is different. Painful memories enter my mind and cannot—and should not—be pushed away. In my actions, though not my principles, I have been selfish all my life. As a child, I learned what was right but not how to improve my nature. I received good principles, but pride and self-importance controlled how I followed them. Unfortunately, I was an only son and, for many years, an only child. My parents spoiled me. They were good people, especially my kind and loving father. Yet they allowed, encouraged, and almost taught me to be selfish and controlling. I cared only about my family circle and thought badly of everyone else. At least, I wished to consider their intelligence and worth below my own. That was my character from the age of eight to twenty-eight. I might still be that man without you, dearest, loveliest Elizabeth! I owe you so much! You taught me a difficult but extremely valuable lesson. You properly taught me humility. I proposed without doubting your answer. You showed me how little I deserved to please a woman worthy of being pleased.”

“Had you truly convinced yourself that I would accept?”

“Indeed I had. What must you think of my pride? I believed you wanted and expected my proposal.”

“My manners must have been wrong, though never intentionally. I assure you, I never meant to deceive you. But my lively spirits may often have misled me. How you must have hated me after that evening!”

“Hate you! Perhaps I was angry at first, but my anger soon turned in the proper direction.”

“I am almost afraid to ask what you thought when we met at Pemberley. Did you blame me for coming?”

“No, indeed. I felt only surprise.”

“Your surprise could not equal mine when you paid attention to me. My conscience said I deserved no special politeness. I admit that I expected nothing beyond what I deserved.”

“My purpose then,” Darcy replied, “was to show through every possible kindness that I was not small-minded enough to resent the past. By showing that I had accepted your criticism, I hoped to gain forgiveness and improve your opinion. I cannot say how soon other wishes appeared. But I believe they returned about half an hour after seeing you.”

Then he described Georgiana’s delight in knowing Elizabeth and her disappointment when the visit suddenly ended. Naturally, this led to the cause of that interruption. Elizabeth soon learned that before leaving the inn, Darcy had decided to follow her from Derbyshire and search for Lydia. His seriousness and thoughtful silence there came only from the difficulties involved in this decision.

Elizabeth expressed her gratitude again. But the subject was too painful for both, so they discussed it no further.

They had walked slowly for several miles, too occupied to notice. At last, they checked their watches and discovered it was time to return home.

“What can have happened to Mr. Bingley and Jane?” This question began a discussion of that couple. Darcy was delighted by their engagement, and his friend had told him immediately.

“I must ask whether you were surprised,” Elizabeth said.

“Not at all. When I left, I felt certain it would happen soon.”

“That means you had given your permission. I guessed as much.” Although he objected to the word, she discovered this was mostly true.

“The evening before I left for London,” he said, “I confessed something that I should have admitted long ago. I told him everything that made my previous interference foolish and improper. He was greatly surprised and had never suspected anything. I also said I believed I had been wrong to think your sister indifferent. I could easily see that his love remained strong. Therefore, I had no doubt they would be happy together.”

Elizabeth could not help smiling at his easy way of directing his friend.

“Did you say my sister loved him because you had observed her yourself,” she asked, “or only because I told you last spring?”

“Because I observed her. During my two recent visits here, I watched her closely and became certain of her affection.”

“And I suppose your certainty immediately convinced him.”

“It did. Bingley is honestly modest. His lack of confidence prevented him from trusting his own judgment in such an anxious matter. But he trusts mine, which made everything easy. I had to confess one thing that fairly angered him for a time. Your sister spent three months in London last winter. I knew and deliberately hid it from him. He was angry. But I am convinced his anger lasted only until he felt certain of your sister’s love. Now he has completely forgiven me.”

Elizabeth wanted to remark that Mr. Bingley was a delightful friend. Because he was so easily guided, his value was beyond measure. But she stopped herself. Darcy still had to learn how to accept teasing, and beginning now seemed too early. He imagined Bingley’s coming happiness, which naturally would be second only to his own. They continued talking until they reached the house, then separated in the hall.
