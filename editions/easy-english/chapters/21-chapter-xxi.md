# Chapter XXI

The discussion of Mr. Collins’s proposal was now almost over. Elizabeth only had to bear the uncomfortable feelings it had caused. She also had to endure her mother’s occasional angry remarks. Mr. Collins showed his feelings in a different way. He was not embarrassed or unhappy, and he did not avoid Elizabeth. Instead, he behaved stiffly and kept an offended silence. He hardly ever spoke to her. For the rest of the day, he gave all his careful attention to Miss Lucas. He had been very proud of giving that attention to Elizabeth before. Miss Lucas politely listened to him. This brought welcome relief to everyone, especially Elizabeth.

The next day brought no improvement in Mrs. Bennet’s bad temper or poor health. Mr. Collins remained in the same state of offended pride. Elizabeth had hoped his anger might shorten his visit. However, it did not change his plan at all. He had always meant to leave on Saturday, and he still intended to stay until then.

After breakfast, the girls walked to Meryton. They wanted to learn whether Mr. Wickham had returned. They also wished to mourn his absence from the Netherfield ball. He met them as they entered the town and went with them to their aunt’s house. There they all discussed his regret, his annoyance, and everyone’s concern. However, he freely admitted something to Elizabeth. He had chosen for himself not to attend the ball.

“As the time came nearer,” he said, “I realised I should not meet Mr. Darcy. Spending so many hours in the same room and the same party might be unbearable. It might also cause unpleasant scenes for people besides myself.”

Elizabeth strongly approved of his self-control. They had plenty of time to discuss it fully. They also politely praised each other. Wickham and another officer walked back to Longbourn with them. During the walk, Wickham paid particular attention to Elizabeth. His company gave her two advantages. She appreciated the compliment he paid her. She was also glad to have the chance to introduce him to her parents.

Soon after they returned, Miss Bennet received a letter from Netherfield and opened it at once. The envelope contained a small sheet of fine, smooth paper. A lady’s beautiful, flowing handwriting covered it. Elizabeth watched her sister’s face change as she read. Jane studied some particular parts very carefully. She soon controlled herself, put away the letter, and tried to join the conversation cheerfully. However, Elizabeth was too worried about the letter to attend even to Wickham. As soon as he and his companion left, Jane gave Elizabeth a look. It invited her to follow Jane upstairs. In their room, Jane took out the letter and said, “This is from Caroline Bingley. Its contents have surprised me greatly. The whole party has now left Netherfield and is travelling to London. They do not intend to return. I will read you what she says.”

Jane read the first sentence aloud. It said that they had just decided to follow their brother to London immediately. They planned to dine that day in Grosvenor Street, where Mr. Hurst had a house. The next part said:—“‘My dearest friend, I will not pretend to regret leaving anything in Hertfordshire except your company. But I hope we will enjoy many more of our delightful meetings in the future. Meanwhile, frequent and completely open letters can reduce the pain of separation. I depend on you for that.’” Elizabeth listened to these grand words with complete distrust. Their sudden departure surprised her, but she saw no true reason for sorrow. Their absence from Netherfield would not necessarily prevent Mr. Bingley from returning there. As for their company, Jane would soon stop missing it when she enjoyed his.

“It is unfortunate,” Elizabeth said after a short pause, “that you cannot see your friends before they leave. But perhaps Miss Bingley’s future happiness will arrive earlier than she expects. The delightful friendship you shared may soon become even happier when you are sisters. They will not keep Mr. Bingley in London.”

“Caroline clearly says that none of them will return to Hertfordshire this winter. I will read it to you.

“‘When my brother left us yesterday, he thought his business in London would take three or four days. We are certain it will take longer. We also believe that once Charles reaches London, he will not hurry to leave. Therefore, we have decided to follow him. He should not have to spend his free hours in an uncomfortable hotel. Many of my friends are already in London for the winter. My dearest friend, I wish you intended to join them, but I cannot hope for that. I sincerely hope your Christmas in Hertfordshire will bring all the usual pleasures of the season. I also hope you have so many admirers that you do not miss the three we take away.’

“This clearly shows,” Jane added, “that he will not return this winter.”

“It only shows that Miss Bingley does not intend him to return.”

“Why do you think that? It must be his own decision, because he is free to choose. But you do not know everything. I will read the part that hurts me most. I will hide nothing from you. ‘Mr. Darcy is eager to see his sister. To tell the truth, we are almost equally eager to see her again. I truly believe no one equals Georgiana Darcy in beauty, elegance, and talent. Louisa and I love her greatly. Our love grows even stronger because of one hope. We dare to hope she may one day become our sister. I do not know whether I have mentioned my feelings about this before. But I will not leave Hertfordshire without trusting you with them. I hope you will not think them unreasonable. My brother already admires her greatly. He will now have many chances to see her as a close friend. Her relations want the marriage as much as his own family does. I am his sister and naturally think highly of him. Still, I believe Charles could win any woman’s heart. Everything supports such a love, and nothing prevents it. My dearest Jane, am I wrong to hope for an event that would make so many people happy?’ What do you think of this passage, my dear Lizzy?” Jane asked when she finished. “Is it not clear enough? Caroline neither expects nor wants me to become her sister. She is completely certain that her brother does not care for me. If she suspects my feelings, she means—most kindly!—to warn me. Can anyone understand it differently?”

“Yes, they can, because I understand it completely differently. Will you hear my opinion?”

“Very gladly.”

“I can explain it briefly. Miss Bingley sees that her brother loves you, but wants him to marry Miss Darcy. She follows him to London, hoping to keep him there. She also tries to persuade you that he does not care about you.”

Jane shook her head.

“Truly, Jane, you should believe me. Nobody who has seen you together can doubt his love. Miss Bingley certainly cannot, because she is not foolish. If Mr. Darcy had shown half as much love for her, she would have ordered her wedding clothes. The truth is that we are not rich or important enough for them. She especially wants her brother to marry Miss Darcy for another reason. If one marriage joins the families, a second marriage may be easier. The idea is certainly clever. It would probably succeed if Miss de Bourgh were not in the way. But, my dearest Jane, think seriously. Miss Bingley says her brother greatly admires Miss Darcy. That does not make him value you less than he did on Tuesday. Nor can his sister persuade him that he loves her friend instead of you.”

“If we agreed about Miss Bingley,” Jane replied, “your explanation would make me quite peaceful. But I know it is based on an unfair belief. Caroline could never deliberately deceive anyone. I can only hope that she is deceiving herself.”

“Exactly. Since my idea will not comfort you, you could not have found a happier one. Believe that she is mistaken. You have now done your duty as her friend and must stop worrying.”

“But, my dear sister, could I be happy even in the best situation? How could I accept a man when his sisters and friends all want him to marry someone else?”

“You must decide for yourself,” said Elizabeth. “Think about it carefully. Perhaps upsetting his two sisters would cause more pain than being his wife would bring happiness. If so, I certainly advise you to refuse him.”

“How can you speak like that?” said Jane with a weak smile. “Their disapproval would deeply upset me. But you know I could not hesitate.”

“I did not think you could. Therefore, I cannot feel much pity for your situation.”

“But if he does not return this winter, I will never need to choose. A thousand things may happen in six months.”

Elizabeth completely rejected the idea that he would not return. She believed it came only from Caroline’s selfish wishes. Those wishes might be expressed openly or cleverly. Still, Elizabeth could not imagine them influencing a young man who was completely independent.

Elizabeth explained her opinion to her sister as strongly as possible. She soon had the pleasure of seeing its good effect. Jane was not naturally hopeless. She slowly began to hope that Bingley would return to Netherfield. Sometimes her uncertainty in love overcame that hope. Still, she hoped he would return and fulfil every wish of her heart.

They agreed to tell Mrs. Bennet only that the family had left. They would not worry her about Mr. Bingley’s behaviour. Even this limited news upset her greatly. She said it was terribly unlucky that the ladies had left so suddenly. They had gone just when everyone was becoming close. After complaining for some time, she found some comfort. Mr. Bingley would soon return and dine at Longbourn. Finally, she made one comforting promise. Although he had only been invited to a family dinner, she would serve two full courses.
