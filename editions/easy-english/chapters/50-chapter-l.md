# Chapter L

Mr. Bennet had often wished he had saved part of his yearly income. It would have provided better for his children and for his wife after his death. Now he wished it more than ever. If he had done his duty, Lydia would not owe her uncle for saving her honour. Mr. Bennet could have paid for the pleasure of making one of the most worthless young men in Great Britain her husband.

He was seriously concerned that his brother-in-law alone would pay for this affair. The result gave little advantage to anyone. Mr. Bennet wanted to discover how much Mr. Gardiner had paid. Then he meant to repay the debt as soon as possible.

When Mr. Bennet first married, saving money had seemed completely unnecessary. Of course, they would have a son. When their son came of age, he and his father could end the entail. The widow and younger children would then be secure. Five daughters were born one after another, but they still expected a son. Mrs. Bennet remained certain for many years after Lydia’s birth. At last, they lost hope, but it was too late to begin saving. Mrs. Bennet had no talent for saving money. Only her husband’s love of financial independence kept their spending within their income.

The marriage agreement secured five thousand pounds for Mrs. Bennet and the children. The parents could decide how to divide it among the children. They now had to decide Lydia’s share. Mr. Bennet agreed to the proposal without hesitation. He wrote a very short but grateful answer to his brother-in-law. He approved everything and promised to fulfil every agreement made for him. He had never expected Wickham to marry Lydia at so little cost to himself. The annual hundred pounds would truly cost him less than ten pounds a year. Lydia’s food and allowance had already cost almost that amount. Her mother had also given her frequent money presents.

The small amount of effort required from him was another pleasant surprise. At present, he wanted as little trouble as possible. His first violent anger had made him active in searching for Lydia. When it passed, he naturally returned to his usual laziness. He quickly sent his letter. He began business slowly but completed it fast. He asked how much he owed his brother-in-law. But he was too angry with Lydia to send her any message.

The good news quickly spread through the house and then through the neighbourhood. The neighbours received it with proper calm. A different ending would have offered better conversation. Miss Lydia Bennet might have become a prostitute in London. In a happier version, she might have been hidden from society in a distant farmhouse. Still, her marriage gave them much to discuss. All Meryton’s unkind old ladies had earlier wished her well with false kindness. Now the situation had changed, but their wishes remained nearly as strong. With such a husband, they still considered Lydia’s misery certain.

Mrs. Bennet had not come downstairs for two weeks. On this happy day, she returned to the head of her table. Her extremely high spirits troubled everyone. No shame weakened her triumph. Since Jane was sixteen, her greatest wish had been a daughter’s marriage. Now it was about to happen. She thought and spoke only about an elegant wedding: fine muslin, new carriages, and servants. She searched the whole neighbourhood for a suitable house for Lydia. She did not know what the couple’s income would be. Still, she rejected many houses as too small and unimportant.

“Haye Park might do if the Gouldings left it,” she said. “The great house at Stoke might suit if its drawing room were larger. But Ashworth is too far away. I could not bear Lydia living ten miles from me. And the rooms under the roof at Purvis Lodge are dreadful.”

Her husband let her speak while the servants remained. When they left, he said, “Mrs. Bennet, we must understand each other before you choose any of these houses. There is one house in this neighbourhood where they will never enter. I will not encourage their foolish behaviour by receiving them at Longbourn.”

A long argument followed, but Mr. Bennet remained firm. It soon led to another argument. Mrs. Bennet learned with shock that her husband would not pay one guinea for Lydia’s clothes. He said he would show her no affection on this occasion. Mrs. Bennet could hardly understand it. His anger seemed unbelievably severe to her. Without new clothes, Lydia’s marriage would hardly seem real. Mrs. Bennet felt more shame about missing clothes than about Lydia’s behaviour. Lydia had run away and lived with Wickham for two weeks before the wedding.

Elizabeth now deeply regretted telling Mr. Darcy about their fears for Lydia. Her distress had made her speak. The coming marriage would soon give the elopement a proper ending. They could perhaps hide its shameful beginning from everyone outside the immediate area.

Elizabeth did not fear that Mr. Darcy would spread the story. She trusted few people’s secrecy more. Yet no one’s knowledge of her sister’s weakness could shame her more. She did not fear a personal disadvantage because an uncrossable distance already seemed to divide them. Even if Lydia had married honourably, Darcy could hardly marry into their family now. Every earlier objection would be joined by a close relationship with the man he rightly despised.

Elizabeth could not wonder that he would avoid such a connection. In Derbyshire, she had believed he still wanted her love. She could not reasonably expect that wish to survive this blow. She felt humbled and sad. She regretted something, though she hardly knew what. She now wanted his respect when it could no longer help her. She wanted news of him when there was almost no chance of receiving it. She knew she could have been happy with him when they were unlikely to meet again.

She often thought how he might triumph if he knew the truth. Only four months earlier, she had proudly rejected his proposal. Now she would accept it with joy and gratitude. She believed he was as generous as any man could be. But he was human, so he must feel some triumph.

Elizabeth now began to understand that his character and abilities exactly suited her. His understanding and nature differed from hers but answered all her wishes. A marriage would have helped both of them. Her relaxed and lively manner might have softened his mind and improved his manners. His judgment, knowledge, and experience of the world would have helped her even more.

But such a happy marriage could no longer show admiring society what married happiness meant. A very different marriage would soon enter their family. That marriage made the other impossible.

Elizabeth could not imagine how Wickham and Lydia would support themselves in reasonable independence. But she could easily guess how little lasting happiness they would have. They had come together only because their desires were stronger than their morals.

Mr. Gardiner soon wrote to his brother again. He briefly answered Mr. Bennet’s thanks. He was always eager to help any family member and begged him never to mention it again. The main purpose of his letter was different. Mr. Wickham had decided to leave the militia.

“I greatly wanted him to do this once the marriage was settled,” he added. “I think you will agree that leaving that unit is wise for him and my niece. Mr. Wickham plans to join the regular army. Some former friends can and will still help him there. He has been promised the lowest officer’s position in General ——’s regiment. It is now stationed in the north. Its distance from this part of the country is helpful. He makes good promises. Among different people, they will both have reputations to protect. I hope this will make them more sensible. I have written to Colonel Forster about our arrangements. I asked him to promise quick payment to Mr. Wickham’s creditors in and near Brighton. I have personally promised that payment. Will you please make the same promise to his creditors in Meryton? I will add a list based on his information. He has reported all his debts. I hope he has not lied to us. Haggerston has our instructions, and everything will be completed within a week. Then they will join his regiment unless they are invited to Longbourn first. Mrs. Gardiner tells me my niece greatly wishes to see you all before leaving the south. She is well and sends respectful greetings to you and her mother. Yours and so on.

“E. Gardiner.”

Mr. Bennet and his daughters clearly saw every advantage of Wickham leaving the ——shire regiment. They agreed completely with Mr. Gardiner. Mrs. Bennet was less pleased. Lydia would live in the north just when her mother expected pleasure and pride in her company. Mrs. Bennet had not abandoned her plan for the couple to live in Hertfordshire. The news was therefore a severe disappointment. Lydia also had to leave a regiment where she knew everyone and liked many people.

“She is so fond of Mrs. Forster,” said Mrs. Bennet. “Sending her away will be terrible! She also likes several of the young men very much. The officers in General ——’s regiment may not be so pleasant.”

Lydia’s wish to visit before going north could be understood as a request. At first, her father completely refused it. But Jane and Elizabeth wanted their parents to recognise Lydia after her marriage. This would help their sister’s feelings and social position. They asked their father earnestly but calmly and sensibly. They wanted him to receive Lydia and her husband at Longbourn after the wedding. At last, he agreed to think as they did and act as they wished. Their mother was delighted. She could show her married daughter to the neighbourhood before Lydia went north. In his next letter, Mr. Bennet allowed them to visit. They would travel to Longbourn as soon as the ceremony ended. Elizabeth was surprised that Wickham agreed. If she had followed only her own wishes, she would never have met him again.
