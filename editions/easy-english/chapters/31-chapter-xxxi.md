# Chapter XXXI

Everyone at the Parsonage greatly admired Colonel Fitzwilliam’s manners. The ladies believed he would add much pleasure to their visits to Rosings. However, several days passed without an invitation. While Rosings had other visitors, the Collinses were not needed. At last, on Easter Day, almost a week after the gentlemen arrived, Lady Catherine invited them. Even then, she only asked them after church to come that evening. During the week, they had seen little of Lady Catherine or her daughter. Colonel Fitzwilliam had visited the Parsonage more than once. They had seen Mr. Darcy only at church.

They naturally accepted and arrived at the proper time in Lady Catherine’s drawing room. Her Ladyship received them politely. However, their company was clearly less welcome when she had other guests. She gave almost all her attention to her nephews, especially Darcy. She spoke to them far more than anyone else.

Colonel Fitzwilliam seemed truly glad to see them, because any change was welcome at Rosings. Mrs. Collins’s pretty friend had also attracted him greatly. He sat beside Elizabeth and spoke pleasantly about Kent, Hertfordshire, travel, home, books, and music. Elizabeth had never enjoyed that room half as much. Their lively, easy conversation attracted Lady Catherine’s attention and Mr. Darcy’s. Darcy repeatedly looked towards them with curiosity. After a while, her Ladyship openly showed the same feeling and called out,—

“What are you saying, Fitzwilliam? What are you discussing? What are you telling Miss Bennet? Let me hear it.”

“We were talking about music, madam,” he said when he could no longer avoid answering.

“Music! Then speak aloud. It is my favourite subject. If you discuss music, I must join the conversation. Few people in England enjoy music more than I do or have better natural taste. If I had learned, I would have become an excellent musician. Anne would have too, if her health had allowed her to practise. I am sure she would have played beautifully. How is Georgiana progressing, Darcy?”

Mr. Darcy spoke with loving praise of his sister’s skill.

“I am very glad to hear such a good report,” said Lady Catherine. “Tell her from me that she cannot become excellent without practising a great deal.”

“I assure you, madam,” he replied, “she does not need that advice. She practises very regularly.”

“So much the better. One cannot practise too much. When I next write, I will order her never to neglect it. I often tell young ladies that musical excellence requires constant practice. I have told Miss Bennet several times that she will never play truly well without more practice. Mrs. Collins has no instrument, but she is welcome at Rosings every day. As I have often told her, she may play the piano in Mrs. Jenkinson’s room. She would be in nobody’s way in that part of the house.”

Mr. Darcy looked slightly ashamed of his aunt’s rudeness and did not answer.

After coffee, Colonel Fitzwilliam reminded Elizabeth of her promise to play. She immediately sat at the instrument, and he placed a chair near her. Lady Catherine listened to half a song. Then she returned to talking with her other nephew. Mr. Darcy soon walked away from her. With his usual careful pace, he approached the piano. He stood where he could clearly see the performer’s face. Elizabeth noticed him. At the first pause, she turned with a playful smile and said,—

“You mean to frighten me, Mr. Darcy, by coming so seriously to hear me. But I will not be afraid, although your sister plays very well. I am too stubborn to let others frighten me. Every attempt to do so only increases my courage.”

“I will not say you are mistaken,” he replied. “You cannot truly believe I mean to frighten you. I have known you long enough to understand something. You greatly enjoy stating opinions that are not really your own.”

Elizabeth laughed heartily at this picture of herself. She said to Colonel Fitzwilliam, “Your cousin will give you a fine idea of me. He will teach you not to believe anything I say. I am especially unlucky to meet someone who can expose my true character. I had hoped to appear respectable in this part of the world. Truly, Mr. Darcy, it is ungenerous to mention everything you knew against me in Hertfordshire. It is also unwise. You are encouraging me to answer, and I may reveal things that will shock your relations.”

“I am not afraid of you,” he said with a smile.

“Please tell me your accusations,” cried Colonel Fitzwilliam. “I want to know how he behaves among strangers.”

“Then you shall hear, but prepare for something terrible. I first saw him in Hertfordshire at a ball. And what do you think he did there? He danced only four dances! I am sorry to hurt you, but it is true. Gentlemen were scarce, and more than one young woman had no partner. Mr. Darcy, you cannot deny it.”

“At that time, I did not know any lady at the ball outside my own party.”

“True, and introductions at a ball are completely impossible. Well, Colonel Fitzwilliam, what shall I play next? My fingers wait for your orders.”

“Perhaps,” said Darcy, “I would have chosen better if I had sought an introduction. But I am not skilled at pleasing strangers.”

“Shall we ask your cousin why?” said Elizabeth, still speaking to Colonel Fitzwilliam. “Why is a sensible, educated, experienced man unable to please strangers?”

“I can answer without asking him,” said Fitzwilliam. “He will not make the effort.”

“I certainly lack a skill that some people possess,” said Darcy. “I cannot easily talk with people I have never met. I cannot follow their style or appear interested in their concerns as others often do.”

“My fingers,” said Elizabeth, “do not move across this instrument as skilfully as many women’s. They lack the same strength, speed, and expression. But I have always considered that my own fault. I would not make the effort to practise. I do not believe my fingers are less able than another woman’s.”

Darcy smiled and said, “You are perfectly right. You have used your time much better. Nobody with the honour of hearing you could find anything missing. Neither of us performs for strangers.”

Lady Catherine interrupted them and demanded to know what they were discussing. Elizabeth immediately began playing again. Lady Catherine approached and listened for a few minutes. Then she said to Darcy,—

“Miss Bennet would play fairly well with more practice and a London teacher. She understands fingering quite well, although her taste does not equal Anne’s. Anne would have been a delightful performer if her health had allowed her to learn.”

Elizabeth looked at Darcy to see how warmly he agreed with this praise of his cousin. She saw no sign of love then or at any other time. His behaviour towards Miss de Bourgh offered Miss Bingley one comfort. If she had been his relation, he might have been equally likely to marry her.

Lady Catherine continued commenting on Elizabeth’s playing. She added many instructions about skill and taste. Elizabeth received them with patient politeness. At the gentlemen’s request, she remained at the piano until her Ladyship’s carriage was ready. It took them all home.
