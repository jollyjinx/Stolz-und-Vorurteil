# Chapter XXIV

Miss Bingley’s letter arrived and ended all doubt. Its first sentence said they had settled in London for the winter. It ended with her brother’s regret. He had not had time to say goodbye to his Hertfordshire friends before leaving.

Hope was over, completely over. When Jane could read the rest, she found little comfort in it. The writer still claimed great affection for her, but offered nothing else. Most of the letter praised Miss Darcy and repeated her many attractions. Caroline joyfully described their growing friendship. She even predicted the success of the hopes mentioned in her earlier letter. She was also very pleased that her brother was staying in Mr. Darcy’s house. With great excitement, she mentioned some of Mr. Darcy’s plans for new furniture.

Jane soon told Elizabeth the main points. Elizabeth listened in silent anger. She felt concern for her sister and anger against everyone else. She did not believe Caroline’s claim that her brother preferred Miss Darcy. Elizabeth still had no doubt that Bingley truly loved Jane. She had always been ready to like him. Now she thought angrily, almost with contempt, about his easy nature and weak will. These qualities made him a servant to his clever friends. He sacrificed his own happiness to their changing wishes. If he had risked only his own happiness, he could have treated it as he pleased. But her sister’s happiness was involved, and he must know it. Elizabeth would think about this uselessly for a long time. She could think of nothing else. Perhaps Bingley’s love had truly died. Perhaps his friends had only suppressed it. Perhaps he had noticed Jane’s love, or perhaps he had missed it. The answer would greatly affect Elizabeth’s opinion of him. However, Jane’s situation and pain remained the same.

One or two days passed before Jane could discuss her feelings with Elizabeth. At last, Mrs. Bennet left them alone. She had just complained longer than usual about Netherfield and its owner. Jane could not help saying,—

“Oh, I wish our dear mother could control herself better! She cannot know how much her constant remarks about him hurt me. But I will not complain. It cannot last long. We will forget him, and everything will be as it was before.”

Elizabeth looked at her sister with worried disbelief but said nothing.

“You doubt me,” cried Jane, colouring slightly. “You truly have no reason. I may remember him as the most pleasant man I know, but that is all. I have nothing to hope or fear, and no reason to blame him. Thank God I do not have that pain. I only need a little time. I will certainly try to overcome—”

Soon she added more firmly, “I already have one comfort. It was only a mistake of imagination on my side. It has harmed no one except me.”

“My dear Jane,” Elizabeth cried, “you are too good. Your kindness and selflessness are truly angelic. I do not know what to say. I feel I have never valued you enough or loved you as you deserve.”

Miss Bennet eagerly denied having any unusual goodness. She said her sister’s loving heart caused such praise.

“No,” said Elizabeth, “that is not fair. You want to think everyone respectable and dislike hearing me criticise anybody. I only want to think you perfect, and you oppose me. Do not fear that I will go too far. I will not take away your right to love everyone. You need not worry. There are few people I truly love and even fewer I respect. The more I see of the world, the less I like it. Every day proves that human characters are inconsistent. Appearances of goodness or sense cannot be trusted. I have recently seen two examples. I will not name one; the other is Charlotte’s marriage. It cannot be explained! However I view it, it cannot be explained!”

“My dear Lizzy, do not surrender to such feelings. They will destroy your happiness. You do not consider enough how situations and characters differ. Remember Mr. Collins’s respectable position and Charlotte’s careful, steady nature. Remember that she belongs to a large family. In money matters, this marriage is an excellent choice. For everyone’s sake, be ready to believe she may feel some liking and respect for our cousin.”

“For you, I would try to believe almost anything. But such a belief would help nobody else. If Charlotte truly liked him, I would think less of her understanding. Now I only think less of her heart. My dear Jane, Mr. Collins is proud, pompous, narrow-minded, and foolish. You know it as well as I do. A woman who marries him cannot think properly. You must not defend her, even though she is Charlotte Lucas. For one person, you must not change the meaning of principle and honesty. Do not persuade yourself or me that selfishness is careful judgment. Do not call blindness to danger a safe path to happiness.”

“Your words about them are too strong,” Jane replied. “I hope their happiness together will convince you. But enough of that. You mentioned something else and spoke of two examples. I understand you. Still, dear Lizzy, please do not hurt me by blaming that person. Do not say you now think badly of him. We should not quickly imagine that someone has deliberately injured us. A lively young man cannot always be careful and wise. Often, our own pride deceives us. Women imagine that admiration means more than it does.”

“And men make sure they imagine it.”

“If men do it deliberately, they cannot be excused. But I do not believe there is as much deliberate deceit as some people imagine.”

“I do not think Mr. Bingley planned to do wrong,” said Elizabeth. “But people can make mistakes and cause misery without an evil plan. Thoughtlessness, neglect of others’ feelings, and weak decisions are enough.”

“And do you accuse him of one of those?”

“Yes, the last. But if I continue, my opinions about people you respect will upset you. Stop me while you can.”

“Then you still believe his sisters influence him?”

“Yes, together with his friend.”

“I cannot believe it. Why would they try to influence him? They can only wish for his happiness. If he loves me, no other woman can give it to him.”

“Your first belief is false. They may wish for many things besides his happiness. They may want him to gain more wealth and social importance. They may want him to marry a girl with money, powerful relations, and pride.”

“They certainly want him to choose Miss Darcy,” Jane replied. “But their feelings may be better than you suppose. They have known her much longer than they have known me. Naturally, they love her more. Whatever they want, they probably did not oppose their brother. What sister would feel free to do that without a serious objection? If they believed he loved me, they would not separate us. If he truly loved me, they could not succeed. Your belief makes everyone behave unnaturally and wrongly, and makes me most unhappy. Do not upset me with it. I am not ashamed of my mistake—or only a little. It is nothing beside the pain of thinking badly of him or his sisters. Let me understand the situation in the best possible way.”

Elizabeth could not oppose this wish. From then on, they hardly ever mentioned Mr. Bingley’s name together.

Mrs. Bennet continued to wonder and complain about his failure to return. Elizabeth explained it clearly almost every day. Still, Mrs. Bennet never seemed likely to understand it. Elizabeth tried to persuade her of something she did not believe herself. She said his attention to Jane had been an ordinary, short-lived liking. It had ended when he no longer saw her. Mrs. Bennet accepted this as possible each time. However, Elizabeth had to repeat the same story every day. Mrs. Bennet’s greatest comfort was that Mr. Bingley must return in the summer.

Mr. Bennet treated the matter differently. “So, Lizzy,” he said one day, “your sister has been disappointed in love. I congratulate her. Next to marriage, a girl enjoys a little romantic disappointment now and then. It gives her something to think about and makes her special among her friends. When is your turn? You will not enjoy Jane’s advantage for long. This is your time. There are enough officers in Meryton to disappoint every young lady in the country. Choose Wickham. He is pleasant and would leave you in a respectable manner.”

“Thank you, sir, but a less pleasant man would satisfy me. We cannot all expect Jane’s good fortune.”

“True,” said Mr. Bennet. “But whatever happens to you, your loving mother will always make as much trouble from it as possible.”

Mr. Wickham’s company helped to remove the gloom caused by recent events at Longbourn. They saw him often. His other attractions now included complete openness. Everyone discussed the story Elizabeth had already heard. They knew his claims against Mr. Darcy and all he had suffered from him. Everyone was pleased to remember that they had always disliked Mr. Darcy before knowing any of this.

Only Miss Bennet believed there might be circumstances that Hertfordshire society did not know. Those facts might partly excuse Mr. Darcy. Her steady kindness always asked for understanding and allowed for possible mistakes. Everyone else condemned Mr. Darcy as the worst of men.
