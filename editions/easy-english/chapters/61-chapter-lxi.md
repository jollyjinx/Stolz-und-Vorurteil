# Chapter LXI

The day Mrs. Bennet got rid of her two most deserving daughters made her motherly feelings extremely happy. One can imagine how proudly she later visited Mrs. Bingley and spoke about Mrs. Darcy. For her family’s sake, I wish I could say that achieving her great desire improved her permanently. After arranging marriages for so many daughters, perhaps she became sensible, pleasant, and well informed. Unfortunately, she did not. Perhaps this was lucky for her husband, who might not have enjoyed such an unusual kind of family happiness. She still sometimes suffered from nerves and always remained foolish.

Mr. Bennet missed his second daughter greatly. His affection for her drew him away from home more often than anything else. He loved visiting Pemberley, especially when nobody expected him.

Mr. Bingley and Jane remained at Netherfield for only one year. Living so near her mother and Meryton relations troubled even his relaxed nature and her loving heart. His sisters’ favourite wish was then fulfilled. He bought an estate in a county beside Derbyshire. Besides every other source of happiness, Jane and Elizabeth now lived within thirty miles of each other.

Kitty spent most of her time with her two elder sisters, which greatly benefited her. Their society was much better than any she had usually known, and she improved greatly. Her temper was easier to control than Lydia’s. Away from Lydia’s example, good care and guidance made her less easily annoyed, less ignorant, and less boring. Naturally, everyone carefully protected Kitty from Lydia’s harmful company. Mrs. Wickham often invited her to visit, promising balls and young men, but her father never agreed.

Mary was the only daughter who remained home. Mrs. Bennet could not bear sitting alone, so Mary had to spend less time developing her accomplishments. She was forced to meet more people. Still, she could discuss moral lessons after every morning visit. Her sisters’ beauty was no longer compared with hers, so she no longer felt embarrassed. Her father suspected that she accepted the change quite willingly.

As for Wickham and Lydia, her sisters’ marriages caused no change in their characters. Wickham calmly accepted that Elizabeth would now learn about every act of ingratitude and dishonesty he had hidden. Despite everything, he still hoped Darcy might be persuaded to improve his fortune. Lydia’s letter congratulating Elizabeth on her marriage showed that at least Wickham’s wife shared this hope. Perhaps Wickham did too. The letter said—

“My dear Lizzy,

“I wish you happiness. If you love Mr. Darcy half as much as I love my dear Wickham, you must be very happy. It is wonderful that you are so rich. When you have nothing else to do, I hope you will think about us. I am sure Wickham would greatly like a position at the royal court. I do not think we shall have enough money to live without some help. Any position paying about three or four hundred pounds a year would do. However, do not speak to Mr. Darcy about it if you would prefer not to.

“Yours,” etc.

Elizabeth very much preferred not to, so her answer tried to end every such request and expectation. However, she often sent them whatever help she could provide by carefully limiting her private spending. Their income had always been too small for two people with expensive desires and no concern for the future. Whenever they moved, they always asked either Jane or Elizabeth for a little help paying their bills. Their way of life remained extremely unsettled, even after peace allowed them to have a permanent home. They constantly moved in search of a cheap place and always spent too much. Wickham’s affection for Lydia soon became indifference, while hers lasted slightly longer. Despite her youth and manners, marriage allowed her to keep her respectable social position. Darcy could never welcome Wickham at Pemberley, but for Elizabeth’s sake, he helped Wickham further in his career. Lydia occasionally visited Pemberley when her husband went to enjoy London or Bath. Both often stayed with the Bingleys for so long that even Bingley lost his patience. He went as far as suggesting that they should leave.

Miss Bingley was deeply upset by Darcy’s marriage. But she wanted to keep the right to visit Pemberley, so she abandoned her anger. She became more affectionate than ever towards Georgiana and almost as attentive to Darcy as before. She also repaid Elizabeth every piece of politeness she had previously failed to give.

Pemberley was now Georgiana’s home, and the sisters’ affection was exactly what Darcy had hoped for. They could love each other as much as they had intended. Georgiana had the highest possible opinion of Elizabeth. At first, however, she often listened with surprised alarm to Elizabeth’s lively, playful conversations with her brother. Darcy had always inspired such great respect in Georgiana that it almost defeated her affection. Now she saw him openly teased. She learned something she had never understood before. With Elizabeth’s guidance, she began to see that a woman may speak freely to her husband. A brother does not always allow such freedom from a sister more than ten years younger.

Lady Catherine was extremely angry about her nephew’s marriage. Her character made her express herself with complete and natural directness. In answering his announcement, she sent terribly insulting words, especially about Elizabeth. All contact ended for a time. At last, Elizabeth persuaded Darcy to forgive the offence and seek peace. His aunt resisted a little longer, but her anger finally weakened. Perhaps her love for him caused it, or perhaps she wanted to see how his wife behaved. She lowered herself enough to visit them at Pemberley. She came despite the pollution that its woods had supposedly suffered. Not only did Pemberley have such a mistress, but her uncle and aunt from London also visited.

They always remained extremely close to the Gardiners. Darcy truly loved them, and so did Elizabeth. Both felt the warmest gratitude towards the people whose journey with Elizabeth to Derbyshire had brought the couple together.
