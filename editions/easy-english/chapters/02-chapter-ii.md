# Chapter II

Mr. Bennet was among the first people to make a formal visit to Mr. Bingley. He had always meant to go, though he kept telling his wife he would not. She knew nothing about it until the evening after the visit. Then he revealed it in the following way. He saw his second daughter decorating a hat and suddenly spoke to her.

“I hope Mr. Bingley will like it, Lizzy.”

“We have no way to learn what Mr. Bingley likes,” her mother said angrily, “because we are not going to visit him.”

“But you forget, Mama,” said Elizabeth, “that we will meet him at the public dances. Mrs. Long has promised to introduce him.”

“I do not believe Mrs. Long will do that. She has two nieces of her own. She is selfish and false, and I think very badly of her.”

“So do I,” said Mr. Bennet. “I am glad you do not depend on her help.”

Mrs. Bennet chose not to answer. However, she could not remain quiet and began scolding one daughter.

“Do not keep coughing like that, Kitty, for heaven's sake! Have some pity for my nerves. You are tearing them to pieces.”

“Kitty has no judgment about her coughs,” said her father. “She chooses bad moments for them.”

“I do not cough for pleasure,” Kitty replied crossly. “When is your next ball, Lizzy?”

“Two weeks from tomorrow.”

“Yes, that is right,” cried her mother. “Mrs. Long returns only the day before. She cannot introduce him because she will not know him herself.”

“Then, my dear, you can do your friend a favour and introduce Mr. Bingley to her.”

“Impossible, Mr. Bennet! I do not know him myself. How can you tease me so?”

“I respect your caution. Two weeks is certainly a very short acquaintance. No one can know a man's true character after only two weeks. But if we do not take the risk, someone else will. Mrs. Long and her nieces must take their chance. She will think it kind if you refuse the task, so I will do it myself.”

The girls stared at their father. Mrs. Bennet said only, “Nonsense, nonsense!”

“What can that forceful cry mean?” he asked. “Do you call the rules of introduction nonsense? People consider those rules very important. I cannot agree with you there. What do you say, Mary? You think deeply, read serious books, and copy useful passages from them.”

Mary wanted to say something very wise, but she did not know what to say.

“While Mary arranges her thoughts,” he continued, “let us return to Mr. Bingley.”

“I am tired of Mr. Bingley,” cried his wife.

“I am sorry to hear that. Why did you not tell me sooner? If I had known this morning, I certainly would not have visited him. It is very unfortunate. But I have made the visit, so now we cannot escape knowing him.”

The ladies were exactly as astonished as he had hoped. Mrs. Bennet was perhaps the most amazed. When her first wild joy passed, she began saying that she had expected it all along.

“How good of you, my dear Mr. Bennet! But I knew I would persuade you at last. I knew you loved your girls too much to ignore such a useful acquaintance. Oh, how pleased I am! And what a good joke that you went this morning but said nothing until now.”

“Now, Kitty, you may cough as much as you like,” said Mr. Bennet. Then he left the room, tired by his wife's delight.

“What an excellent father you have, girls,” she said after the door closed. “I do not know how you can ever repay his kindness—or mine. At our age, meeting new people every day is not very pleasant. But we would do anything for you. Lydia, my love, you are the youngest. Still, Mr. Bingley will probably dance with you at the next ball.”

“Oh,” Lydia said boldly, “I am not worried. I may be the youngest, but I am the tallest.”

They spent the rest of the evening guessing when Mr. Bingley would return Mr. Bennet's visit. They also decided when to invite him to dinner.
