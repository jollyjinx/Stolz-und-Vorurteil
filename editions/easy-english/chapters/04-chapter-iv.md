# Chapter IV

When Jane and Elizabeth were alone, Jane spoke freely about how much she admired Mr. Bingley. Earlier, she had praised him more carefully.

“He is exactly what a young man should be,” she said. “He is sensible, cheerful, and lively. I have never seen such pleasant manners—so relaxed and yet so perfectly polite.”

“He is also handsome,” Elizabeth replied. “A young man should be handsome if possible. That makes his character complete.”

“I was very flattered when he asked me to dance a second time. I did not expect such a compliment.”

“Did you not? I expected it for you. That is one great difference between us. Compliments always surprise you and never surprise me. Asking you again was completely natural. He could see you were five times prettier than every other woman. He deserves no praise for noticing that. Well, he is certainly very pleasant, and you may like him. You have liked many people who were much more foolish.”

“Dear Lizzy!”

“Oh, you are much too ready to like everyone. You never see a fault in anybody. In your eyes, the whole world is good and pleasant. I have never heard you speak badly of another person.”

“I do not want to judge anyone too quickly. But I always say what I truly think.”

“I know, and that is why it is so surprising. You have good sense, yet you honestly fail to see other people's foolishness. Many people pretend to judge kindly. That happens everywhere. But you do it without showing off or following a plan. You take the good in every character and make it even better. You say nothing about the bad. Only you can do that. So, do you also like this man's sisters? Their manners are not as pleasant as his.”

“Not at first, certainly. But when you speak with them, they are very pleasing women. Miss Bingley will live with her brother and manage his house. Unless I am greatly mistaken, she will be a charming neighbour.”

Elizabeth listened silently but did not agree. The sisters' behaviour at the ball had not been generally pleasing. Elizabeth noticed things faster than Jane and was less willing to bend. The sisters had also shown Elizabeth no special attention to influence her judgment. She had little desire to approve of them. They were elegant ladies who could be cheerful when pleased. They could also be pleasant when they chose. But they were proud and conceited. They were fairly handsome and had attended one of London's best private schools. Each had a fortune of twenty thousand pounds. They often spent too much and mixed with people of high rank. They therefore believed they had every right to admire themselves and look down on others. They came from a respectable family in northern England. They remembered this far more clearly than another fact: trade had created both their brother's fortune and their own.

Mr. Bingley had inherited property worth nearly one hundred thousand pounds. His father had planned to buy an estate but died before doing it. Mr. Bingley also intended to buy one and sometimes even chose the county. However, Netherfield gave him a good house and use of an estate. People who knew his easy nature wondered whether he might stay there forever. Perhaps he would leave the next generation to make the purchase.

His sisters strongly wanted him to own an estate. Still, Miss Bingley did not mind leading his household while he remained a tenant. Mrs. Hurst had married a fashionable man without much money. She was also happy to treat her brother's house as home when it suited her. Mr. Bingley had been an adult for less than two years when someone suggested Netherfield House by chance. He viewed the house for half an hour. He liked its position and main rooms and believed the owner's praise. He rented it immediately.

Bingley and Darcy had a firm friendship despite their very different characters. Darcy loved Bingley's easy, open, flexible nature. No personality could have been more unlike Darcy's, though Darcy seemed satisfied with his own. Bingley completely trusted Darcy's affection and respected his judgment. Darcy understood more and was very intelligent, though Bingley was not foolish. Darcy was also proud, reserved, and difficult to please. His manners were polite but not welcoming. In this matter, Bingley had a great advantage. People always liked Bingley, while Darcy continually offended them.

Their descriptions of the Meryton public ball showed their different characters. Bingley had never met pleasanter people or prettier girls. Everyone had been kind and attentive. No one was formal or stiff. He quickly felt that he knew the whole room. As for Miss Bennet, he could not imagine a more beautiful angel. Darcy saw people with little beauty and no fashion. None interested him or gave him attention or pleasure. He admitted Miss Bennet was pretty, but said she smiled too much.

Mrs. Hurst and Miss Bingley agreed, but they still admired and liked Jane. They called her a sweet girl and were willing to know her better. Miss Bennet was therefore officially a sweet girl. Their brother now felt free to think about her exactly as he wished.
